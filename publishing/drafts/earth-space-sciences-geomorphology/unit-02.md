# Unit 2 — Weathering, soils and hillslope dynamics

## Guiding scientific question

How does rock become sediment, and when does material move downslope?

## Unit perspective

Hillslopes receive and produce material through chemical alteration, physical breakdown, bioturbation, rockfall and mass movement. Soil thickness is a state variable governed by production from bedrock, surface transport, deposition and removal. A thick soil layer need not mean a stable old surface: rapid production and supply may coexist with rapid erosion. Conversely, a bare rock slope can persist because weathering is slow, transport is rapid or rockfalls expose fresh surfaces. The manner in which material moves depends on slope geometry, grain size, vegetation, water pressure, cohesion and time. Slow hillslope diffusion is a useful long-term approximation where many small disturbances accumulate. It cannot capture a large debris flow, which may initiate abruptly and move far beyond the source slope. We must keep continuum descriptions distinct from event-driven failures. Even a textbook factor-of-safety calculation is an idealized balance, not an operational landslide prediction. For conservation, housing and road design, geology must be supplemented by site measurements, engineering assessment and an explicit treatment of uncertainty.

### Learning outcomes and scale discipline

By the end of this unit, students should be able to describe at least five independent landform or sediment observations, demonstrate one dimensionally consistent quantitative calculation, design a comparative measurement, and distinguish an interpretation from the observations used to support it. Practice viewing the same landform in a map, a section and a material description. Keep an explicit column for phenomena that are absent, unobserved or only assumed. At the finest scale, a few grain contacts may govern transport; at larger scales, inherited valley geometry and event histories control the response. Switching scale is legitimate only after specifying which variables remain meaningful. At the start of the field exercise, make a drawing of soil depth along one crest-to-hollow traverse and timed tracer distances; list which parts would be measured directly and which reconstructed from other evidence.

The unit's measurement choices should include bedrock exposure, soil composition, slope angle and water conditions; the strongest report also checks parent-rock identity and direct soil contacts. Even a carefully derived rate does not establish its formative mechanism unless the sedimentary or stratigraphic evidence agrees. Interpret changes in the context of the process and observation interval, and use at least two sources of independent evidence. Most useful scientific uncertainties are not just numerical instrument errors: researchers must also consider incorrect boundaries, missing events, biased sample sites, mistaken ages and incomplete constitutive laws.

### Worked quantitative example — Catchment solute export

**Given.** 15 metric tonnes per hectare per year from a 35 ha catchment. **Calculation:** 15 × 35. **Result:** **525 tonnes/yr**. Work through the units before using the output to discuss Earth-surface history. If runoff chemistry changes seasonally, a single water sample is unrepresentative. Solute yield also includes atmospheric and non-weathering sources. A correct result from an incorrect conceptual model is not evidence that the process operated. A weathering index integrates parent composition and drainage as well as time. State the assumed parent rock before assigning any alteration gradient to climate. ### Alternatives and field tests

A fresh debris fan can be a flood deposit rather than a hillslope failure if no source scar is documented. **Given.** c=6 kPa, soil unit weight 19 kN/m³, z=2 m, angle 30°, friction angle 28°, water ratio 0.40. **Calculation:** [6 + (19×2−0.40×9.81×2)cos²30° tan28°] / [(19×2)sin30°cos30°]. **Result:** **1.09539 ratio**. ## Integrated field-and-model capstone — Soil mantle and episodic slope failure

An imaginary hillside has three auger transects, three vegetation cover classes and one dated debris-flow deposit. Construct a source-to-storage diagram. Compute a simplified diffusive flux where slopes are gentle, then identify exactly where the approximation fails. Use rainfall and headscarp notes to distinguish storm-related failures from long-term creep without pretending that a classroom stability ratio is an engineering assessment.

### Protocol and evidence allocation

Write a project proposal that names the study question, a defined sampling region, hypotheses H1 and H2 and three observations that could discriminate them. Build an input table with at least twelve synthetic measurements; record a unit, identifier, uncertainty class and data source for every row. Design one transect, one temporal repeat survey and one test using a different kind of evidence. State how the field team would avoid confirmation bias: preregister predicted outcomes, assign a second mapper to identify landforms independently and blind the initial data processing to the favored model. Any unmeasured quantity must be marked as an assumption. Base the arithmetic on the numerical cases in the unit, but do not present the invented data as actual measurements.

In the analysis, start with a mass or elevation balance rather than a purely visual explanation. If the balance does not close, label the residual and list processes that may lie outside the boundary, including sediment stored in a channel, transport at an unmonitored exit and error in estimated density or surface area. Separate interpretation of rate from reconstruction of age and chronology. An apparently consistent average rate can conceal long inactive periods punctuated by large storms, landslides or channel switching. Explore a parameter range wide enough to expose this sensitivity. Explain what observation would tell you whether the residual is an actual physical loss or a measurement artifact.

### Reporting template and peer review

The final report should contain: a title that describes an inquiry rather than a conclusion; data source and acquisition notes; a map or section with scale; a table of raw observations; a short derivation with units; alternative interpretations; uncertainty and detection limits; and an honest conclusion identifying what the exercise cannot show. A peer reviewer should trace one plotted value back to an original input without assistance. Report uncertainty in both physical data and causal attribution. Describe how sampling locations could bias the result, and identify one independent validation datum that was not used when choosing the model. Treat a good fit as a starting point for further testing, not proof of a unique historical trajectory.

### Assessment prompts and rubric

**Interpretation.** Provide two competing process histories consistent with the invented evidence, predict one differentiating field measurement, and identify which uncertainties could reverse the preferred interpretation. **Quantitative reasoning.** Recompute the selected unit case in SI units, vary two inputs independently, and interpret signs, magnitudes and nonlinearities. **Evidence ethics.** Identify all fabricated datasets and explain why they must never be described as real hazard, glacial or coastal monitoring records. **Review standard.** Full credit requires correct units, reproducible methods, a mass or geometry balance, explicit chronology limits and an alternative interpretation; numerical correctness alone is insufficient.

## Unit synthesis

Return to the guiding question: **How does rock become sediment, and when does material move downslope?** Make a process diagram contrasting an observable pattern with a process that could produce it. Now add a second process capable of leaving a similar pattern. Underline measurements that would distinguish the two. Ask whether the lesson's calculations predict the type of change measured by those instruments or only provide a scale for discussing it. The emphasis throughout geomorphology is not on naming spectacular landforms but on reconstructing defensible histories, with uncertainties, from repeatable measurements and physical consistency.
