# Unit 7 — Karst, tectonic and volcanic landforms

## Guiding scientific question

How do bedrock properties and deep Earth forcing influence surface processes?

## Unit perspective

Landscape evolution is not controlled by climate alone. Soluble limestone can develop caves and closed depressions when groundwater pathways focus dissolution; similar surface holes may result from mining subsidence or buried infrastructure. Rock uplift changes potential energy and gradients, but river incision does not always track uplift instantaneously. River terraces may record coupled effects of climate, sediment supply and tectonic motion. In volcanic regions, lava, tephra and weak volcanic deposits create distinct slopes and drainage networks. Post-eruption erosion and debris flows can modify those surfaces rapidly. Separating production of relief from destruction of relief requires an accounting of rock uplift, surface lowering and sediment routing. Any proposed history needs chronological and stratigraphic evidence, not just a visually appealing sequence of terraces or crater-like depressions. This unit develops multiple-working-hypotheses reasoning and emphasizes that real hazards require qualified assessments.

### Learning outcomes and scale discipline

By the end of this unit, students should be able to describe at least five independent landform or sediment observations, demonstrate one dimensionally consistent quantitative calculation, design a comparative measurement, and distinguish an interpretation from the observations used to support it. Practice viewing the same landform in a map, a section and a material description. Keep an explicit column for phenomena that are absent, unobserved or only assumed. At the finest scale, a few grain contacts may govern transport; at larger scales, inherited valley geometry and event histories control the response. Switching scale is legitimate only after specifying which variables remain meaningful. At the start of the field exercise, make a drawing of spring chemistry, terrace age, lava-flow chronology and valley profile; list which parts would be measured directly and which reconstructed from other evidence.

The unit's measurement choices should include bedrock solubility, uplift controls and crosscutting deposits; the strongest report also checks traceable age constraints and stratigraphic order. Even a carefully derived rate does not establish its formative mechanism unless the sedimentary or stratigraphic evidence agrees. Interpret changes in the context of the process and observation interval, and use at least two sources of independent evidence. Most useful scientific uncertainties are not just numerical instrument errors: researchers must also consider incorrect boundaries, missing events, biased sample sites, mistaken ages and incomplete constitutive laws.

## Integrated field-and-model capstone — Surface form versus tectonic and karst history

A carbonate plateau beside a volcanic field exhibits enclosed depressions, terraces and a lava-dammed former channel. Draw two cross-sections showing a karst and a fault-based interpretation. Use invented spring chemistry and terrace ages to calculate conditional export and incision rates; present alternative climate-driven explanations.

### Protocol and evidence allocation

Write a project proposal that names the study question, a defined sampling region, hypotheses H1 and H2 and three observations that could discriminate them. Build an input table with at least twelve synthetic measurements; record a unit, identifier, uncertainty class and data source for every row. Design one transect, one temporal repeat survey and one test using a different kind of evidence. State how the field team would avoid confirmation bias: preregister predicted outcomes, assign a second mapper to identify landforms independently and blind the initial data processing to the favored model. Any unmeasured quantity must be marked as an assumption. Base the arithmetic on the numerical cases in the unit, but do not present the invented data as actual measurements.

In the analysis, start with a mass or elevation balance rather than a purely visual explanation. If the balance does not close, label the residual and list processes that may lie outside the boundary, including sediment stored in a channel, transport at an unmonitored exit and error in estimated density or surface area. Separate interpretation of rate from reconstruction of age and chronology. An apparently consistent average rate can conceal long inactive periods punctuated by large storms, landslides or channel switching. Explore a parameter range wide enough to expose this sensitivity. Explain what observation would tell you whether the residual is an actual physical loss or a measurement artifact.

### Reporting template and peer review

The final report should contain: a title that describes an inquiry rather than a conclusion; data source and acquisition notes; a map or section with scale; a table of raw observations; a short derivation with units; alternative interpretations; uncertainty and detection limits; and an honest conclusion identifying what the exercise cannot show. A peer reviewer should trace one plotted value back to an original input without assistance. Report uncertainty in both physical data and causal attribution. Describe how sampling locations could bias the result, and identify one independent validation datum that was not used when choosing the model. Treat a good fit as a starting point for further testing, not proof of a unique historical trajectory.

### Assessment prompts and rubric

**Interpretation.** Provide two competing process histories consistent with the invented evidence, predict one differentiating field measurement, and identify which uncertainties could reverse the preferred interpretation. **Quantitative reasoning.** Recompute the selected unit case in SI units, vary two inputs independently, and interpret signs, magnitudes and nonlinearities. **Evidence ethics.** Identify all fabricated datasets and explain why they must never be described as real hazard, glacial or coastal monitoring records. **Review standard.** Full credit requires correct units, reproducible methods, a mass or geometry balance, explicit chronology limits and an alternative interpretation; numerical correctness alone is insufficient.

## Unit synthesis

Return to the guiding question: **How do bedrock properties and deep Earth forcing influence surface processes?** Make a process diagram contrasting an observable pattern with a process that could produce it. Now add a second process capable of leaving a similar pattern. Underline measurements that would distinguish the two. Ask whether the lesson's calculations predict the type of change measured by those instruments or only provide a scale for discussing it. The emphasis throughout geomorphology is not on naming spectacular landforms but on reconstructing defensible histories, with uncertainties, from repeatable measurements and physical consistency.
