# Unit 5 — Dryland and aeolian geomorphology

## Guiding scientific question

How can episodic water and wind build deserts and dust records?

## Unit perspective

Drylands are commonly described as places dominated by wind, but episodic runoff and sediment-laden floods can accomplish major geomorphic work even where annual rainfall is low. Sparsely vegetated surfaces, patchy protective crusts and variable sediment availability modify wind entrainment thresholds. Wind transport includes suspension, saltation and surface creep; the size and migration of a dune depend on sediment supply, wind regime and topographic setting. A dune crest therefore cannot be used as a direct wind-speed instrument. Desert pavements, alluvial fans, playas and loess deposits record different combinations of sediment sorting, deposition and exposure. A playa may fill during rare storms and then concentrate evaporites; those chemical layers may subsequently be deflated. Students must use landform assemblages and stratigraphy rather than a single isolated dune shape to reconstruct environment. Human disturbance of protective vegetation and soil crust can alter dust emissions over periods much shorter than the formation timescale of the underlying basin.

### Learning outcomes and scale discipline

By the end of this unit, students should be able to describe at least five independent landform or sediment observations, demonstrate one dimensionally consistent quantitative calculation, design a comparative measurement, and distinguish an interpretation from the observations used to support it. Practice viewing the same landform in a map, a section and a material description. Keep an explicit column for phenomena that are absent, unobserved or only assumed. At the finest scale, a few grain contacts may govern transport; at larger scales, inherited valley geometry and event histories control the response. Switching scale is legitimate only after specifying which variables remain meaningful. At the start of the field exercise, make a drawing of dune crests, fan lobes, loess exposures and playa salt chemistry; list which parts would be measured directly and which reconstructed from other evidence.

The unit's measurement choices should include sediment source, wind regime, storm rainfall and surface crust; the strongest report also checks source fingerprints and storm-event timing. Even a carefully derived rate does not establish its formative mechanism unless the sedimentary or stratigraphic evidence agrees. Interpret changes in the context of the process and observation interval, and use at least two sources of independent evidence. Most useful scientific uncertainties are not just numerical instrument errors: researchers must also consider incorrect boundaries, missing events, biased sample sites, mistaken ages and incomplete constitutive laws.

Identify one independently measurable outcome that would distinguish the two interpretations, and one negative result that would undermine the leading explanation.

### Worked quantitative example — Simplified wind transport proxy

**Given.** Wind friction velocity .45 m/s above a threshold .25 m/s; proxy proportional to excess speed cubed. **Calculation:** (0.45 − 0.25)³. **Result:** **0.008 (m/s)³**. Work through the units before using the output to discuss Earth-surface history. A small increase above threshold produces a nonlinear proxy change. This is not calibrated sediment discharge and has no field-use coefficient. A correct result from an incorrect conceptual model is not evidence that the process operated. Document surface crust; independently check wind direction. Assess sensitivity to storm-runoff episodes and verify provenance. Record each raw observation, units, uncertainty and assumptions before model fitting.

The threshold velocity is not a universal constant across soils or turbulence regimes. Treat dust source attribution as a trajectory and composition problem, not visual guesswork. A high-resolution shape map without ages and a dated deposit without spatial coverage offer different constraints. ## Integrated field-and-model capstone — Dust source versus spectacular landform

In a dry basin, compare an alluvial fan and dune field using a synthetic wind-frequency rose and sediment grain-size table. Trace the potential origin of dust to playa, farmland and old fan deposits. Design a two-sided sampling campaign and explain how salt crusting could alter emissions during one season without changing the underlying landform.

### Protocol and evidence allocation

Write a project proposal that names the study question, a defined sampling region, hypotheses H1 and H2 and three observations that could discriminate them. Build an input table with at least twelve synthetic measurements; record a unit, identifier, uncertainty class and data source for every row. Design one transect, one temporal repeat survey and one test using a different kind of evidence. State how the field team would avoid confirmation bias: preregister predicted outcomes, assign a second mapper to identify landforms independently and blind the initial data processing to the favored model. Any unmeasured quantity must be marked as an assumption. Base the arithmetic on the numerical cases in the unit, but do not present the invented data as actual measurements.

In the analysis, start with a mass or elevation balance rather than a purely visual explanation. If the balance does not close, label the residual and list processes that may lie outside the boundary, including sediment stored in a channel, transport at an unmonitored exit and error in estimated density or surface area. Separate interpretation of rate from reconstruction of age and chronology. An apparently consistent average rate can conceal long inactive periods punctuated by large storms, landslides or channel switching. Explore a parameter range wide enough to expose this sensitivity. Explain what observation would tell you whether the residual is an actual physical loss or a measurement artifact.

### Reporting template and peer review

The final report should contain: a title that describes an inquiry rather than a conclusion; data source and acquisition notes; a map or section with scale; a table of raw observations; a short derivation with units; alternative interpretations; uncertainty and detection limits; and an honest conclusion identifying what the exercise cannot show. A peer reviewer should trace one plotted value back to an original input without assistance. Report uncertainty in both physical data and causal attribution. Describe how sampling locations could bias the result, and identify one independent validation datum that was not used when choosing the model. Treat a good fit as a starting point for further testing, not proof of a unique historical trajectory.

### Assessment prompts and rubric

**Interpretation.** Provide two competing process histories consistent with the invented evidence, predict one differentiating field measurement, and identify which uncertainties could reverse the preferred interpretation. **Quantitative reasoning.** Recompute the selected unit case in SI units, vary two inputs independently, and interpret signs, magnitudes and nonlinearities. **Evidence ethics.** Identify all fabricated datasets and explain why they must never be described as real hazard, glacial or coastal monitoring records. **Review standard.** Full credit requires correct units, reproducible methods, a mass or geometry balance, explicit chronology limits and an alternative interpretation; numerical correctness alone is insufficient.

## Unit synthesis

Return to the guiding question: **How can episodic water and wind build deserts and dust records?** Make a process diagram contrasting an observable pattern with a process that could produce it. Now add a second process capable of leaving a similar pattern. Underline measurements that would distinguish the two. Ask whether the lesson's calculations predict the type of change measured by those instruments or only provide a scale for discussing it. The emphasis throughout geomorphology is not on naming spectacular landforms but on reconstructing defensible histories, with uncertainties, from repeatable measurements and physical consistency.
