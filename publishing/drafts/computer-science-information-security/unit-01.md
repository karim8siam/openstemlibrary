# Information Security — Unit 1: Security Foundations

> Original instructional editorial draft, 24 October 2026. All organizations, prices, probabilities, incidents, and architectural examples are fictional teaching scenarios. This unit is not independently peer reviewed and is not production approved. Simulations are explanatory models, not security assessments of real systems.

## Learning outcomes

After studying this unit, a reader should be able to inventory assets in relation to a specific mission; distinguish confidentiality, integrity, availability, authenticity, and accountability requirements; construct an explicit threat scenario; draw a data-flow diagram with trust boundaries; separate threat actors from weaknesses and consequences; estimate a bounded expected loss under declared assumptions; and explain why numerical risk estimates cannot substitute for investigation, judgment, and monitoring. The worked examples use a fictional university laboratory's sample-submission service throughout, so changes in assumptions can be followed from one section to the next.

## 1.1 Assets, security objectives, and system context

### Security protects a mission, not an inventory of devices

A computer system exists to support something people care about. In the fictional North River University laboratory, a web service lets researchers submit sample identifiers, receive test results, and share approved reports with collaborators. Its servers and code matter because they make these tasks possible. The laboratory would still face a serious security problem if every server remained online but the results were silently assigned to the wrong patient. Conversely, an unavailable development dashboard might cause little damage if it is not needed for current work. Begin an assessment by asking what the organization is trying to accomplish, who depends on it, and which failures would obstruct that purpose. Only then decide which resources require protection.

An *asset* is anything whose loss, disclosure, misuse, alteration, or unavailability could affect a relevant objective. Assets include information, services, identities, cryptographic keys, physical instruments, relationships, contractual rights, public confidence, and the time needed to recover. Some are tangible, such as a temperature-controlled freezer. Others are informational, such as the mapping between a specimen code and the researcher who submitted it. Others are capabilities, such as the ability to release a verified report before a clinical deadline. A complete inventory is not a list of every object in a building. It is a structured account of the things that matter to defined workflows, with an owner, location or custodian, dependencies, and consequences of compromise.

Consider three copies of a laboratory report: an original result in the laboratory information system, a PDF attached to a collaborator's email, and a backup held by a storage provider. All three represent the same underlying finding, but they have different access paths and retention controls. Treating them as one item may conceal the possibility that an email forwarding rule leaks the PDF while the main database remains well protected. Treating every byte as an independent business asset creates the opposite problem: an unmanageable inventory without useful priorities. The practical unit of analysis is a meaningful information set or service together with its copies and dependencies. Inventory entries should state what the information represents and which processes use it.

### Distinguish the security properties that matter

Confidentiality concerns preventing unauthorized disclosure. Integrity concerns preventing unauthorized or accidental alteration and ensuring that information is sufficiently accurate and complete for its intended use. Availability concerns timely access to a service or resource when authorized people need it. These three objectives are often introduced together, but they are not interchangeable. Encrypting a database may protect its confidentiality while doing nothing to correct a mistaken sample-to-person mapping. A redundant server may improve availability while faithfully replicating corrupted data. A digitally signed report may help detect modification while still revealing sensitive content to everyone who receives it.

Additional properties sharpen the analysis. *Authenticity* concerns whether an identity, message, or origin is genuine in the required context. *Accountability* concerns whether actions can be traced to an appropriate actor or process, subject to the system's evidentiary limits. *Privacy* includes questions of appropriate collection, use, retention, and disclosure that cannot be reduced to encryption alone. In the laboratory, a report may be technically confidential yet still be retained longer than permitted by the research agreement. A log may accurately identify an account but not establish which human used a shared password. A sound requirement says which property must hold for which asset, under which operational circumstances, rather than promising that the whole system will be 'secure.'

Security properties also conflict with other legitimate objectives. Restricting every report to a single reviewer may lower accidental disclosure but delay urgent work. Requiring an additional authentication step on every routine instrument measurement may be burdensome enough that staff invent unsafe workarounds. Security engineering must identify the constraint explicitly: for example, verified researchers may retrieve an approved result within five minutes during staffed hours, while the service must reject unauthorized access even if the requester knows a sample number. Such requirements can be tested. Vague statements like 'protect all data at all times' cannot guide trade-offs or demonstrate success.

### Map ownership, sensitivity, and dependency

A useful asset record includes an identifier, a plain-language description, a business owner, a technical custodian, where copies exist, sensitivity, retention expectations, dependencies, and the impact of plausible failures. For the laboratory, the sample-to-researcher mapping is owned by the research operations team and maintained by the application team. It appears in a production database and an encrypted backup, and an authorized export may reach the reporting service. Its integrity is critical because a mismatch can invalidate scientific conclusions. Its confidentiality may also be high when codes can be linked to participants. Availability has a deadline-sensitive component: a short outage during a routine maintenance window may be acceptable, but a prolonged outage during a scheduled experiment may not.

Dependencies often reveal risks that are invisible in an asset-by-asset ranking. A report delivery service depends on identity verification, database queries, a queue, storage, and a notification provider. The system can fail even if the report file itself is intact. A cloud administrator's credential may have greater effective reach than any single report because it can change access rules for thousands of files. A backup is not useful simply because it exists: it must be recoverable within the time and data-loss limits the mission can tolerate. Ask how a failure propagates through the workflow and whether the apparent fallback relies on the same component as the primary path.

Classifying an asset is a decision aid, not a proof of protection. Labels such as public, internal, confidential, and restricted can help teams choose handling rules, but the categories must be defined locally. The same dataset may be public after aggregation but restricted while it contains direct identifiers. A public website still has integrity and availability requirements; public does not mean disposable. Conversely, a restricted file does not automatically need the most expensive control in the catalog. Its actual access paths, potential harm, and applicable obligations determine the appropriate safeguards. Review classifications when the purpose, audience, or linking information changes.

### Evidence for an asset inventory

Start with interviews and system diagrams, then test claims against observed behavior. Ask operators what happens when an experiment is submitted, where data is stored, which third parties receive it, who can recover deleted records, and what staff do during an outage. Examine configuration, access reviews, storage inventories, and documented contracts where authorized. Compare a data-flow sketch against application logs and real workflows. An inventory derived solely from a procurement spreadsheet can miss personal spreadsheets and ad hoc exports; an inventory derived solely from network discovery can miss legal obligations and human dependencies. Record the source and date of each material claim, including uncertainty.

The inventory is a living model. When a new reporting vendor is introduced, the team should not merely add a hostname; it should update the data types transferred, the identity used, the contractual retention expectations, and the ability to revoke access. When a project ends, deleting the main database may not remove old attachments or backups. Maintaining a useful inventory therefore requires an owner and a change trigger. Without those, the most beautifully formatted list becomes a historical artifact rather than a security control.

## 1.2 Threat models: scenarios, assumptions, and defenses

### A threat model asks what can go wrong in a specific system

A threat model is a reasoned description of how a system could fail under relevant adverse events, how those events might occur, and what measures could prevent, detect, or limit harm. It is not a prediction that every imaginable attacker will strike. It is also not merely a catalog of known software vulnerabilities. The same insecure configuration can be inconsequential in an isolated classroom demonstration and dangerous in a production service that stores sensitive reports. The value of modeling lies in connecting system context to plausible paths and consequences, then making decisions that can be revisited as the system changes.

Define the scope first. For the laboratory, suppose the assessment covers a web browser, the application programming interface, a database, and an external notification provider. The team's immediate concern is unauthorized retrieval or modification of submitted reports. Explicitly exclude the physical security of the laboratory building from this iteration, but record it as an open dependency rather than declaring it safe. State the assumed user roles, normal workflow, authentication mechanisms, and the trust placed in third-party components. If the team cannot agree on these assumptions, numerical rankings later in the process will create false precision.

The central questions are straightforward: What are we building? What could go wrong? What are we going to do about it? How will we know that our analysis remains adequate? A data-flow diagram answers part of the first question by identifying processes, data stores, external actors, and flows. An abuse case answers part of the second by describing an unwanted outcome. Controls and tests address the third. Reviews, monitoring, and changes to the diagram address the fourth. The questions are iterative; a new flow discovered during testing may change the original scope.

### Separate threat actor, event, vulnerability, and impact

A *threat source* is an actor or circumstance that may cause harm: a malicious outsider, an overprivileged insider, a mistaken operator, a hardware failure, or a flood affecting a data center. A *threat event* is a particular occurrence, such as a researcher receiving another researcher's report. A *vulnerability* is a weakness that makes the event possible or more damaging, such as an API accepting a report identifier without checking ownership. *Impact* is the resulting consequence: disclosure of sensitive data, loss of research integrity, delay, or legal exposure. These terms should not be collapsed into 'the hacker risk.' An outsider may exist without a reachable weakness; a weakness may exist without being exploited; an incident may occur through error rather than adversarial action.

For example, the laboratory API accepts a request for a report by identifier. A valid researcher signs in, but the server checks only whether the session is authenticated, not whether that researcher is authorized for the requested report. The threat source could be an authenticated user who deliberately guesses another identifier. The threat event is unauthorized retrieval. The weakness is missing object-level authorization. The impacted asset is the report and its associated identity information. The appropriate defense is to enforce authorization on the server for each object, with tests covering cross-user access; hiding the identifier in the interface does not repair the underlying trust decision. The example is a conceptual teaching scenario, not an instruction to probe real services.

A second scenario illustrates a different failure mechanism. The reporting service relies on an external notification provider. An operator accidentally configures a template to include complete report contents in the message body rather than a short notice. Even if no attacker is involved, the information crosses into a system with a different retention policy and potentially a wider audience. A threat model that considers only hostile outsiders will miss this exposure. Include mistakes, process failures, and supply-chain dependencies when their consequences matter to the mission.

### Use structured categories without mistaking them for completeness

Teams often use prompts such as spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, and elevation of privilege to explore classes of failure. These categories are useful for generating questions, not for certifying that all possible threats have been found. Spoofing asks whether an actor can appear to be someone else; tampering asks whether data or instructions can be changed; repudiation asks whether a consequential action could later be denied without adequate evidence. Disclosure asks who can see information; denial of service asks whether legitimate access can be prevented; elevation of privilege asks whether an actor can gain authority beyond what was intended. The same scenario may involve several categories, and categorization does not itself estimate likelihood.

An abuse case should be written at a level that suggests a test or design review. 'Attackers might compromise the system' is not actionable. 'A user authenticated to Project A requests a Project B report; the API returns the file because it checks login status but not project membership' is actionable. It identifies a precondition, a crossed boundary, an undesirable result, and a control point. Another abuse case might be 'a service account intended to send notifications can read every report because its database role is broader than its job requires.' This can be addressed through scoped permissions, data minimization, and periodic access review. Both examples focus on authorized defensive analysis.

### Prioritize and validate the model

Prioritization should combine plausible exposure, severity, feasibility of remediation, and uncertainty. A scenario affecting one test account is not necessarily as important as a scenario that could disclose every report, but the difference depends on what the account can access and the harm caused. Teams should identify assumptions they can validate cheaply: whether the API actually performs object-level checks, whether service credentials are limited to necessary tables, whether backups are recoverable, and whether notification templates include sensitive fields. A control proposal becomes stronger when it specifies an observable acceptance condition, such as 'a signed-in user outside the project receives a consistent access-denied response and no report bytes.'

Threat models become stale when architecture changes. Adding a mobile client may introduce new token storage paths. Replacing an email provider may alter data retention. Migrating from a monolith to microservices may create internal service-to-service authorization requirements. Revisit the model during major design changes, incidents, and periodic reviews. Record who approved the residual risks and which findings remain open. The objective is to make failures harder and consequences smaller, not to claim that a diagram has eliminated uncertainty.

## 1.3 Trust boundaries, identity, and the limits of perimeter thinking

### A trust boundary is a change in authority or assumptions

A trust boundary exists where data or a request moves between components that should not be assumed to share the same privileges, identity assurances, or integrity guarantees. In a typical application, the browser and server are separated by a boundary because the server cannot trust client-supplied role claims merely because the browser displays them. A service and its database may cross another boundary when the service account receives privileges to read or modify records. An application and an external provider cross a boundary because the provider operates under a separate administrative domain. Boundaries are not limited to firewalls or physical network segments. They are about decisions: which claims are accepted, which operations are authorized, and where evidence is checked.

Draw the laboratory's main flow in words. A researcher uses a browser to send a request to the web API. The API verifies a session, looks up project membership, and asks the database for a report. The database returns data to the API, which filters the response and sends it to the browser. Separately, a background worker sends a minimal notification to a provider. At each arrow ask what the sender can control and what the receiver must verify. A browser can submit any report identifier; the server must not infer authorization from the fact that the identifier appeared in a previous page. A worker may be trusted to send notifications but should not automatically be trusted to change project membership.

Boundaries also appear within an organization. Two employees may both use company laptops but have different obligations and access rights. A build pipeline may be allowed to publish application code but not read all production research records. A data scientist may analyze anonymized aggregate results without needing the original identity mapping. Treating everyone on an internal network as equally trusted makes accidental exposure and compromised accounts more damaging. The guiding idea of zero-trust architecture is that physical or network location alone does not confer implicit trust; decisions should be tied to identity, device or workload context, resource, and policy. This does not mean distrusting people personally. It means not substituting network proximity for authorization.

### Authentication, authorization, and enforcement are different steps

Authentication establishes evidence about an identity. Authorization decides whether that identity may perform a particular action on a particular resource in a particular context. Enforcement makes the decision effective at the actual access point. A login screen can authenticate a researcher successfully while the application still makes an authorization mistake. A database permission policy may be sound on paper while a shared service account bypasses its intended distinctions. Each step needs its own test. 'The user is logged in' answers who the system believes the requester is, not which report the requester may read.

Least privilege means granting only the access necessary for an assigned purpose, with appropriate scope and duration. It is not the same as making every operation cumbersome. In the laboratory, a notification worker needs permission to retrieve a notification template and an approved delivery address; it may not need access to the full report. A report reviewer may read assigned reports but not edit raw instrument measurements. A temporary incident responder may need elevated access for a short investigation, with approval and logging. Permission design should follow tasks and ownership, not simply organizational titles. Review privileges when people change projects and when service responsibilities change.

Defense in depth places independent checks at multiple appropriate points. The API verifies project membership before serving a report; the database role restricts which operations the application can perform; logs record denied requests; monitoring detects unusual volumes; backups protect recoverability. These controls do not all address the same failure, and none is a substitute for the missing authorization check. If a control is bypassed by the same shared assumption as another, the defenses may be correlated rather than independent. Counting controls is less useful than analyzing how each would fail and whether a single configuration error could defeat them all.

### Quantifying exposure across hypothetical paths

For teaching purposes, imagine a report is exposed through any of three independent routes, each with the same hypothetical probability *p* during a defined period. The probability of at least one exposure is `1 − (1 − p)^3`. If *p* is 0.10, the probability is `1 − 0.9³ = 0.271`, or 27.1%. The arithmetic illustrates why multiple routes matter, but it is not a measured incident probability. Real routes may share credentials, software libraries, and operators. A common failure could make them positively correlated, invalidating the independence assumption. Conversely, mutually exclusive conditions could make the formula overstate the chance of at least one event. A useful model declares what each route represents, how the period is defined, and why the assumed dependence is plausible.

A diagram should also distinguish *data* from *authority*. An encrypted report flowing to a collaborator may preserve confidentiality against an intermediate observer, but the collaborator can read it and may have permission to forward it. A signed API request may prove possession of a credential but not prove that the requester is still a member of the relevant project. When a component accepts a claim, document which authority issued it, how it is validated, whether it can be revoked, and what happens when validation fails. Avoid treating cryptography as a general-purpose replacement for business rules.

### Test boundaries with negative cases

A positive test shows that an authorized user can retrieve their own report. A negative test shows that the same user cannot retrieve another project's report, even when the request contains a syntactically valid identifier. Additional tests consider expired sessions, revoked membership, service-account misuse, and attempts to replay stale approval. These are authorized tests in a controlled environment; they should not be conducted against systems without permission. Where possible, tests should assert that unauthorized data is absent from the response, logs, caches, and notifications rather than merely checking a displayed error message.

The strongest boundary model is understandable to people outside the development team. It should show the important actors, the data that crosses each boundary, the decisions made, and the owner of those decisions. It need not include every internal function call. Excessive detail can hide the critical path, while excessive abstraction can hide a dangerous service account. Maintain a high-level diagram for business review and focused detailed diagrams for sensitive flows. When an incident occurs, update both the technical controls and the assumptions that allowed the problem to remain unseen.

## 1.4 Risk analysis, uncertainty, and decisions

### Risk is a question about consequences and likelihood

Security risk analysis evaluates what adverse events might happen, how harmful they could be, and how plausible they are under specified conditions. The laboratory might face disclosure of participant-linked results, loss of experimental integrity, or a prolonged reporting outage. These are different risks because they affect different objectives and require different responses. A threat description without consequences is incomplete; a consequence without a plausible causal scenario is difficult to prioritize. The purpose of analysis is to choose actions, not to manufacture a single impressive score.

Qualitative methods use ordered categories such as low, medium, and high for likelihood and impact. They can support early decisions when trustworthy frequencies are unavailable, but categories must be defined and their disagreements recorded. One team might call an incident 'high impact' because it affects reputation; another might reserve that label for safety-critical disruption. Neither scale is meaningful without agreed criteria. Multiplying ordinal labels as if they were precise numbers is particularly hazardous: a rating of four is not necessarily twice as severe as a rating of two. A risk matrix is a conversation tool, not a mathematical law.

Quantitative analysis may use a probability and an estimated loss over a fixed period. In a deliberately simplified model, expected loss is `P(event) × Loss(event)`. If the chance of a specified report-disclosure event in one year is assumed to be 0.08 and the conditional loss is assumed to be $120,000, the modeled annual expected loss is $9,600. That does not mean a $9,600 invoice will arrive. In many years the event may not occur; in a year when it does, the loss could be much larger. The expectation is a weighted average across hypothetical repeated periods, not a forecast of the most likely single outcome.

### Evaluate a control using a bounded decision model

Suppose an additional access-control review and monitoring process costs $2,400 per year and is assumed to reduce the annual event probability from 0.08 to 0.03, with conditional loss unchanged at $120,000. The original expected loss is $9,600. The controlled expected loss is $3,600. The modeled annual reduction is $6,000, and the net modeled benefit after the $2,400 cost is $3,600. Under those assumptions the control is economically favorable. This arithmetic does not prove that the control is sufficient or that its estimated effect is real. It also ignores the distribution of losses, regulatory duties, secondary harms, and the possibility of correlated events.

An organization may need a safeguard even when a simplistic expected-value calculation is unfavorable. Legal obligations, participant rights, safety requirements, contractual commitments, and risk tolerance constrain the decision. A severe low-probability event may be unacceptable because the organization could not survive it. Some controls are required to meet minimum duties rather than to maximize short-term savings. Conversely, a proposed tool may have an attractive vendor demonstration but little evidence that it changes the specific event probability under discussion. Risk decisions should name the decision-maker, the assumptions, the evidence, the alternative options, and the review date.

Risk responses commonly include reducing the likelihood or impact, avoiding the risky activity, transferring some financial consequence through a contract or insurance, and accepting a documented residual risk. Transfer rarely transfers all responsibility: a university remains accountable to participants even if a service provider owes contractual compensation. Avoidance can remove a dangerous workflow but may also eliminate a valuable research capability. Acceptance should be explicit, time-bounded where appropriate, and made by someone authorized to weigh the consequences. 'We did not fix it' is not the same as a reasoned acceptance decision.

### Separate control effectiveness from confidence in the estimate

A numeric model can be internally correct while externally misleading. If the assumed annual incident probability comes from a handful of unrelated organizations, it may have little predictive value for the laboratory. If the estimated loss includes only server replacement costs, it may omit experiment repetition, participant notification, legal review, and loss of trust. If the control's effectiveness is taken from a vendor claim rather than an observed test, the apparent savings may be optimistic. Record ranges and confidence levels separately from the point estimate. Ask which assumption would change the decision if it were wrong.

Sensitivity analysis is a practical way to expose this dependence. In the simplified control example, the control breaks even when the probability reduction equals `Cost / Loss`, or `2400 / 120000 = 0.02` (two percentage points). If the actual reduction is only one percentage point, the modeled benefit is $1,200 and does not cover the $2,400 annual cost. If the reduction is five percentage points, the modeled benefit is $6,000. The conclusion therefore depends on evidence about effectiveness, not merely on the severity of the threat. The simulator accompanying this unit lets the reader vary those assumptions without suggesting that a slider can measure real-world security.

The analysis period must be consistent. A monthly probability cannot be multiplied by an annual loss without defining how events accumulate. If repeated events are possible, the expected number of events and the probability of at least one event are not the same quantity. For independent monthly events with probability *p*, the probability of at least one event over twelve months is `1 − (1 − p)^12`; the expected number of event occurrences is `12p` when each month is a Bernoulli trial. These expressions answer different questions. They also require assumptions about independence and stability that may not hold when threats adapt or vulnerabilities persist across months.

### Use evidence, review cycles, and explicit residual risk

A useful risk register records the scenario, affected mission, asset owner, assumptions, likelihood and impact rationale, existing controls, proposed action, residual risk, accountable decision-maker, and next review trigger. A change in architecture, a serious incident, a new legal obligation, or a newly discovered dependency should trigger reconsideration. Avoid turning the register into a static list of colors. For high-consequence risks, attach evidence: test results, recovery exercises, access reviews, and the reasons estimates were chosen. State what is not known. Uncertainty should motivate targeted investigation, not be concealed behind decimal places.

The laboratory can now make a coherent decision. It inventories reports and their dependencies; models unauthorized cross-project access as a concrete event; locates the server-side authorization boundary; and estimates the possible benefit of additional controls. The next step is not to declare the system safe. It is to verify the access checks in an authorized test environment, assess the likelihood assumptions with appropriate evidence, decide which residual risks are acceptable, and schedule a review. Security engineering is a continuing process of preserving the mission under uncertainty.

## Fully worked problems

### Problem 1 — Asset priorities for a research workflow

**Question.** A fictional laboratory has a public course schedule, a restricted participant-to-sample mapping, and a temporary rendering cache that can be rebuilt in ten minutes. Rank their confidentiality, integrity, and availability needs using high, medium, or low. Explain why no single total ordering is defensible without a stated mission and give one change that would alter your ranking.

**Step 1: identify the protected workflows.** The schedule helps people attend events; the mapping links participant identities to sensitive results; the cache speeds report rendering but does not hold the authoritative copy. These purposes are more informative than the file formats.

**Step 2: evaluate each property separately.** The public schedule has low confidentiality need because publication is intended, medium-to-high integrity need because wrong dates mislead students, and medium availability need during enrollment. The participant mapping has high confidentiality and integrity needs because unauthorized disclosure or incorrect linkage could cause serious harm; availability may be medium for routine research but high during a time-critical investigation. The rebuildable cache has low confidentiality only if it contains no restricted content, low-to-medium integrity because corrupt cache entries should be rejected, and low availability because the authoritative renderer can regenerate it quickly.

**Step 3: test assumptions.** If the cache contains identifiable reports, its confidentiality becomes high even though it is temporary. If the course schedule is used for emergency evacuation, availability and integrity become more important. If an approved backup can restore the mapping rapidly, the impact of a short outage may decrease, but confidentiality and integrity requirements do not disappear.

**Answer.** The mapping is the clearest high-priority confidentiality and integrity asset under the stated assumptions; the schedule and cache have different priorities for different properties. There is no valid universal ranking without the workflow, data content, recovery capability, and harm model. A label like 'temporary' is not evidence that an asset is harmless.

### Problem 2 — Trust boundaries and a bounded exposure calculation

**Question.** An application sends a report through three hypothetical independent routes, each with a 10% probability of unintended exposure during a single fixed period. Calculate the probability that at least one route exposes the report. Then explain how the result changes conceptually if all routes use the same compromised credential. Identify a server-side authorization test that would address a different class of failure.

**Step 1: define the events.** Let A, B, and C mean that each route exposes the report. Under the problem's independence assumption, the probability that no route exposes it is `P(not A) × P(not B) × P(not C) = 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.729`.

**Step 2: take the complement.** The probability of at least one exposure is `1 − 0.729 = 0.271`, or **27.1%**. This is the probability of one or more events, not the expected number of exposed copies.

**Step 3: examine the model boundary.** If a single credential is shared by all three routes, exposure events may be strongly correlated. The independence formula no longer follows from the assumptions. Without joint probabilities, the exact probability cannot be calculated. The shared credential is a common-mode failure to investigate, not a reason to reuse 27.1% as a measured result.

**Step 4: specify a defensive test.** In a controlled test environment, sign in as a researcher who belongs to Project A and request a Project B report identifier. The server must deny access and return no report data, regardless of whether the browser normally displays that identifier. The test checks authorization at the API boundary rather than relying on user-interface visibility.

**Answer.** Under independence, the modeled chance of at least one exposure is **27.1%**. With shared credentials, the model is insufficient; joint behavior must be characterized. The authorization test targets a separate boundary and cannot be replaced by probability arithmetic.

### Problem 3 — Expected loss, control cost, and sensitivity

**Question.** A fictional service has an assumed annual probability of a specified incident of 0.08, and the conditional financial loss is assumed to be $120,000. A proposed control costs $2,400 per year and is assumed to reduce the incident probability to 0.03. Calculate the baseline expected loss, the controlled expected loss, the gross modeled reduction, the net modeled benefit, and the break-even probability reduction. Explain two reasons the decision might differ in practice.

**Step 1: calculate baseline expected loss.** `0.08 × $120,000 = $9,600` per year. This is an expectation, not a guaranteed annual payment.

**Step 2: calculate residual expected loss.** `0.03 × $120,000 = $3,600` per year. The model holds the conditional loss constant and assumes the control changes only the event probability.

**Step 3: compare with control cost.** The gross expected-loss reduction is `$9,600 − $3,600 = $6,000`. The net modeled benefit is `$6,000 − $2,400 = $3,600` per year.

**Step 4: solve the break-even condition.** Let Δp be the annual probability reduction. At break-even, `Δp × $120,000 = $2,400`, so `Δp = 0.02`, or two percentage points. A smaller reduction does not cover the cost in this simplified model.

**Step 5: challenge the inputs.** The event probability and control effectiveness may be poorly estimated. The loss figure may omit participant harm or legal obligations that cannot be represented adequately by expected dollars. A risk-averse organization may also reject an extreme tail loss even if its expectation appears small.

**Answer.** Baseline expected loss is **$9,600**, controlled expected loss is **$3,600**, gross reduction is **$6,000**, net modeled benefit is **$3,600**, and the break-even probability reduction is **0.02**. The calculation informs but does not settle the decision.

## Guided simulation exercises

**Simulation A — independent exposure paths.** Vary the number of hypothetical paths from one to ten and the per-path exposure probability from zero to one. Observe the complement formula for at least one exposure. Explain why increasing the number of independent routes raises modeled exposure probability, then identify a realistic common dependency that would make independence implausible. The display must label its assumptions and must not present the result as a measured incident rate.

**Simulation B — risk-control economics.** Vary the baseline annual probability, controlled annual probability, conditional loss, and annual control cost. The simulator reports expected loss before and after control, gross reduction, net benefit, and break-even reduction. Examine cases in which the controlled probability exceeds baseline, the cost is zero, and both probabilities are zero. Explain why a negative modeled net benefit does not automatically mean a safeguard should be removed.
