# Unit 02 — Population persistence, genetics and connectivity

> **Book 18 / undergraduate editorial draft**. All numerical cases in this book are invented for classroom use. Independent academic review is pending.

## Core unit inquiry

**Question:** What combination of demography, genetics and dispersal predicts whether populations can persist?

**Bounded setting:** time-indexed animal or plant populations linked by birth, death and movement. **Fictional running setting:** a hypothetical isolated grassland mammal metapopulation. The five lessons share the conceptual aim of following an ecological claim from biological mechanism to measurement, inference and a justifiable decision. The same population count can accompany very different recruitment, survival, effective size and future trajectories. The emphasis is neither pessimism nor optimism; it is disciplined reasoning about actions that influence living systems over time.

A population is an evolving process rather than a number on a clipboard. Even a perfectly counted census can conceal a loss of breeding adults, recruitment failure or demographic imbalance. Stage-specific life cycles connect survival and fecundity to growth; genetics and movement change vulnerability in ways not captured by one rate. The risk can be gradual, such as persistently lower recruitment, or abrupt, such as a rare drought that simultaneously affects many patches. An elementary growth equation can clarify direction but does not determine the distribution of future outcomes.

Evaluate models at the scale where they are used. Patch persistence matters for a meadow network; local recruitment matters for a breeding colony; effective size matters for loss of genetic variation over generations. If dispersal increases, demographic rescue may improve, but pathogens, synchronous disturbances or unsuitable destination habitat may change the result. The following lessons ask students to connect parameters to field methods and to identify why precise-looking forecasts can be fragile.

Before starting, create a two-column notebook. In the first column write statements that directly follow from a defined observation; in the second place the wider interpretation that needs extra assumptions. At the end of the unit, record which observation would most change your preferred conservation decision. This exercise is deliberately not a ranking of students' values. Its purpose is to make the relationship between observations, inference and public choices explicit.

**Guiding question.** How can we distinguish a defensible finding about **population growth and density dependence** from a pattern that could arise through an alternative mechanism or a biased sample? ### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

Exponential growth is a limited approximation at low density; logistic models add density effects but often miss delays, resource pulses and demographic structure. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? ### A field measurement contract

Compare short-interval growth estimates with food availability, disease burden and habitat quality, rather than fitting a carrying capacity from two points. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — repeat observations through time.** Schedule repeated observations during biologically informative seasons and record observer effort, equipment and protocol changes. A trend can reflect a shift in the detection process; retain a calibration subset when methods change. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

A reintroduced herbivore population grows quickly after release but later stabilizes below the theoretical habitat capacity. This is a fictional case, not a measured local trend. **Main interpretive trap.** A fitted K is not a fixed property of a species or location, and environmental variation can change it substantially. **Research integrity.** Scientific disagreement is most useful when it is attached to a falsifiable assumption. ## Lesson 2.3 — Genetic diversity and effective size

**Guiding question.** How can we distinguish a defensible finding about **genetic diversity and effective size** from a pattern that could arise through an alternative mechanism or a biased sample? The aim is to explain the ecological idea, define an appropriate quantity, reproduce an elementary numerical example, interrogate a counterargument, and connect the evidence to a legitimate practical decision.

### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

Effective population size describes the scale of genetic drift and inbreeding in idealized models; it can be far smaller than census size under unequal breeding, skewed sex ratio or fluctuations. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? Name one observation that could contradict the explanation and distinguish the measured state from its inferred cause.

### A field measurement contract

Combine pedigree, marker and demographic information with explicit assumptions; avoid inferring fitness from a single heterozygosity statistic. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — mechanism-based evidence.** Construct a directed causal diagram with the proposed driver, mediating process, ecological response and confounders. Association can be entirely real yet not caused by the proposed mechanism; test plausible alternatives. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

### Synthetic ecological case and competing interpretation

A captive fish population numbers 400 animals but only a small set of parents contributes to each generation. This is a fictional case, not a measured local trend. Identify response, suspected driver and an unmeasured common cause. Would the conclusion change if the monitoring method became more sensitive?

**Main interpretive trap.** Census totals do not establish effective population size, and introducing individuals can carry disease or outbreeding risks. Name a comparison site and independent indicator that could expose this error. Specify the result that would force revision of the preferred explanation.

### Worked quantitative case: Idealized heterozygosity retained over generations

**Assumed invented input.** Neutral drift approximation after eight generations assuming constant Ne=40, random mating and no selection.

**Expression:** `(1-1/(2*40))^8*100`. **Calculated answer:** **90.427 % baseline retained**.

Identify the units and denominator. This arithmetic alone supplies no uncertainty interval or causal evidence. Change a central input by 20%; report whether the management interpretation changes and why.

### Evidence appraisal and practical choice

**Scale and transfer.** An effect observed in one patch does not automatically apply to a landscape, and a ten-year forecast is not validated by a two-month trial. Consider spatial heterogeneity, ecological feedbacks, temporal autocorrelation and the potential displacement of pressures. When extending a conclusion, explicitly identify which biological processes are plausibly stable and which depend on new conditions. Poor transportability often matters more than precision within the original sample. Apply this lens to **genetic diversity and effective size**: identify the actual observation, the larger unsupported inference and the smallest useful independent check. Explain how new evidence might change the next action.

**Implementation feasibility.** State equipment, expertise, permits, tenure and maintenance requirements. A biological gain that cannot be delivered under the available institutions is not a feasible forecast. Evaluate genetic rescue and breeding management with specialist review and long-term monitoring. A decision brief must still specify feasible alternatives, baseline and rights-related constraints; no quantitative success probability is implied.

### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

PVA projects persistence under explicit survival, reproduction, environmental variance, catastrophes and management alternatives; probabilities are conditional on model structure and data quality. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? Name one observation that could contradict the explanation and distinguish the measured state from its inferred cause.

### A field measurement contract

Compare transparent baseline, favorable and unfavorable parameter sets; reserve independent data for model checking where possible. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — hierarchical evidence.** Separate raw observations from modeled parameters, predictions, classifications and management choices. Model uncertainty often has several layers; numerical output should not hide unmeasured inputs. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

### Synthetic ecological case and competing interpretation

An invented tortoise population appears stable under average rainfall but declines sharply under correlated dry years. This is a fictional case, not a measured local trend. Identify response, suspected driver and an unmeasured common cause. Would the conclusion change if the monitoring method became more sensitive?

**Main interpretive trap.** A precise extinction probability from weak inputs creates false certainty; catastrophes and density feedback may be poorly constrained. ### Worked quantitative case: Scenario population persistence

**Assumed invented input.** Toy abundance falls 8% each year for five years; this is not a stochastic population viability model.

### Evidence appraisal and practical choice

**Field reasoning.** Represent the causal pathway as a set of stages: ecological source condition, exposure or interaction, demographic or community response, observed indicator and management interpretation. Describe which stage the proposed evidence directly addresses. A value measured at the end of a chain is not automatically a direct measurement of its cause. ## Lesson 2.5 — Metapopulations and rescue effects

**Guiding question.** How can we distinguish a defensible finding about **metapopulations and rescue effects** from a pattern that could arise through an alternative mechanism or a biased sample? The aim is to explain the ecological idea, define an appropriate quantity, reproduce an elementary numerical example, interrogate a counterargument, and connect the evidence to a legitimate practical decision.

### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

Occupancy of habitat patches reflects colonization, local extinction, dispersal and changing patch quality; connected patches can reduce risk but may also transmit pathogens or synchronized disturbances. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? Name one observation that could contradict the explanation and distinguish the measured state from its inferred cause.

### A field measurement contract

Map patch identity, barriers, occupied status and repeated detections; analyze colonization and extinction separately. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — decision-focused study.** Specify the decision that will change if a result is high or low, then choose the least complex credible measurement. Collecting more data is not always useful if the result cannot change an authorized decision. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

### Synthetic ecological case and competing interpretation

A fictional butterfly occupies five meadow fragments with one intermittently used stepping-stone corridor. This is a fictional case, not a measured local trend. Identify response, suspected driver and an unmeasured common cause. Would the conclusion change if the monitoring method became more sensitive?

**Main interpretive trap.** Treating patch occupancy as a stable binary can hide seasonal dynamics or imperfect detection. Name a comparison site and independent indicator that could expose this error. Specify the result that would force revision of the preferred explanation.

### Worked quantitative case: Patch occupancy turnover

**Assumed invented input.** Five of twenty initially occupied patches became unoccupied between two standardized survey years.

**Expression:** `5/20*100`. **Calculated answer:** **25 % annual patch loss**.

Identify the units and denominator. This arithmetic alone supplies no uncertainty interval or causal evidence. Change a central input by 20%; report whether the management interpretation changes and why.

### Evidence appraisal and practical choice

**Inference and evidence.** Separate the estimand—the quantity the study intends to learn—from the statistic reported by a field method. A sample mean, proportion or index has a denominator and inclusion rule; a demographic forecast adds model assumptions; a management classification adds policy judgment. Ask whether the observation domain is representative of the population for which a recommendation is made. A confidence interval can capture sampling variation while omitting systematic selection bias. **Comparator selection.** Choose untreated or reference sites using ecological similarity rather than ease of access. A valid comparator can differ from a desired restoration state but must represent a plausible alternative trajectory. Prioritize a network of suitable patches rather than only the largest surviving patch. ## Integrated unit investigation — Island species recovery scenario

**Research and management setting.** A small mammal has three occupied patches with differing juvenile recruitment and habitat quality. A corridor might improve recolonization but road mortality could offset a gain. This dataset is fictional and is not sourced from an actual site. Begin by specifying the intended population and the decision that requires evidence. A useful research question should distinguish a state variable, such as breeding occupancy or native habitat condition, from management inputs, such as field labor or expenditure. Decide which indicator could in principle move because the proposed intervention changed an ecological mechanism rather than because the observation process was altered.

**Classroom data.** Synthetic patch census totals are [42,35,18] animals; annual recruitment proportions are [0.10,0.05,0.12]. Put each synthetic observation into a small table with a column for its unit, survey date or period, uncertainty and collection method. If an input is a judgment or a scenario assumption rather than an observation, label it as such. Draw a diagram showing how each measurement relates to the ecological process. Check dimensions before constructing composite indices: an area in hectares and a conservation score without units cannot be added without an explicit decision rule.

**Analysis assignment.** Compare a corridor, a habitat-quality intervention and improved detection under three alternative recruitment assumptions. For each alternative, state what is observed, what is assumed and what would require external validation. Give a best-case and a downside scenario, but do not attach unsupported probabilities. Explain which changes in sampling design or stakeholder priorities would change the conclusion. Illustrate your results with a chart that shows raw points alongside summary values; avoid hiding sample size in a visually smooth curve.

**Critical evaluation.** Explain why the exercise is not a defensible PVA or genetic rescue recommendation without specialist information. A responsible report names at least two plausible alternative explanations. Propose a stronger monitoring design with a baseline or counterfactual, a biological outcome metric, a record of implementation and a realistic revision trigger. If the intervention entails handling wildlife, exposing locations or changing access, identify independent specialist review, legal permissions and affected decision makers; the invented project grants none of those permissions.

**Submission format.** Produce (1) a one-page causal map, (2) a reproducible calculation sheet, (3) a table separating raw observations from modeled outputs, (4) a comparison of intervention and nonintervention alternatives, and (5) a short decision memo stating uncertainty and who can authorize further work. Peer reviewers should be able to find a changed assumption and recompute the conclusion without modifying undocumented spreadsheet cells. Respond in writing to one challenge from a skeptical reviewer rather than simply repeating the original recommendation.
