# Unit 01 — Conservation foundations and extinction risk

> **Book 18 / undergraduate editorial draft**. All numerical cases in this book are invented for classroom use. Independent academic review is pending.

## Core unit inquiry

**Question:** What does a scientifically justified claim of conservation importance or urgency require?

**Bounded setting:** species identification, ecological composition and the boundaries of assessment. **Fictional running setting:** a fictional river delta undergoing habitat conversion. The five lessons share the conceptual aim of following an ecological claim from biological mechanism to measurement, inference and a justifiable decision. Do not confuse legal designation, species-richness totals or unverified threat labels with demonstrated persistence. The emphasis is neither pessimism nor optimism; it is disciplined reasoning about actions that influence living systems over time.

Conservation starts with a definition of what is being protected: one named species, a population process, a habitat mosaic or a set of interactions. The chosen objective changes the evidence needed. A record of many generalist species may indicate richness but not ecological continuity, while a small reserve containing one threatened habitat can be disproportionately significant. Examine the boundary of each biological claim: taxonomic accuracy, spatial domain, interval of observation and whether the criterion describes a stock, a pressure or a response. A threat assessment is credible only when it documents the relevant driver and distinguishes what is measured from what is presumed.

The conservation-priority problem illustrates a normative layer. Should resources focus on the species with highest extinction probability, the action with the greatest expected response, the most irreplaceable landscape or the livelihoods of those bearing restrictions? There is no purely biological calculation that makes rights or equity disappear. Scientific evidence informs the consequences, while transparent governance sets legitimate objectives and constraints. In the lessons below, note how exactly the same inventory can support different decisions under different aims.

Before starting, create a two-column notebook. In the first column write statements that directly follow from a defined observation; in the second place the wider interpretation that needs extra assumptions. At the end of the unit, record which observation would most change your preferred conservation decision. This exercise is deliberately not a ranking of students' values. Its purpose is to make the relationship between observations, inference and public choices explicit.

### A field measurement contract

**Methodological lens — comparison across space.** Select at least three independent locations within each management class, describe how sites were chosen and report environmental covariates. ### Worked quantitative case: Species richness across comparable plots

**Assumed invented input.** Nineteen observed taxa in a 1-ha plot against fifteen in a comparably sampled plot; no inference on functional diversity.

**Expression:** `(19-15)/15*100`. **Calculated answer:** **26.667 % change**.

### Evidence appraisal and practical choice

**Field reasoning.** Represent the causal pathway as a set of stages: ecological source condition, exposure or interaction, demographic or community response, observed indicator and management interpretation. Describe which stage the proposed evidence directly addresses. A value measured at the end of a chain is not automatically a direct measurement of its cause. **Comparator selection.** Choose untreated or reference sites using ecological similarity rather than ease of access. Prioritize indicators linked to ecological integrity and report uncertainty before declaring one site more valuable. A decision brief must still specify feasible alternatives, baseline and rights-related constraints; no quantitative success probability is implied.

## Lesson 1.2 — Extinction mechanisms and timescales

**Guiding question.** How can we distinguish a defensible finding about **extinction mechanisms and timescales** from a pattern that could arise through an alternative mechanism or a biased sample? The aim is to explain the ecological idea, define an appropriate quantity, reproduce an elementary numerical example, interrogate a counterargument, and connect the evidence to a legitimate practical decision.

### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

Extinction risk arises from interactions among mortality, reproductive failure, environmental variability, catastrophes, genetic erosion and disrupted interactions; local extirpation is not the same as global extinction. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? Name one observation that could contradict the explanation and distinguish the measured state from its inferred cause.

### A field measurement contract

Distinguish local abundance declines, occupied-range contraction, recruitment deficits and directly documented global status; evaluate observation duration relative to generation length. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — repeat observations through time.** Schedule repeated observations during biologically informative seasons and record observer effort, equipment and protocol changes. A trend can reflect a shift in the detection process; retain a calibration subset when methods change. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

### Synthetic ecological case and competing interpretation

A fictional island bird persists in adult surveys but produces very few fledglings for four years, suggesting delayed demographic consequences. This is a fictional case, not a measured local trend. Identify response, suspected driver and an unmeasured common cause. Would the conclusion change if the monitoring method became more sensitive?

**Main interpretive trap.** Abundant adults can mask poor recruitment, and survey gaps can mimic disappearance; single-year trends are not long-term extinction probabilities. Name a comparison site and independent indicator that could expose this error. Specify the result that would force revision of the preferred explanation.

### Worked quantitative case: Observed proportional annual decline

**Assumed invented input.** A synthetic count falls from 80 to 68, with equal protocol and no correction for imperfect detection.

**Expression:** `(80-68)/80*100`. **Calculated answer:** **15 % decline**.

Identify the units and denominator. This arithmetic alone supplies no uncertainty interval or causal evidence. Change a central input by 20%; report whether the management interpretation changes and why.

### Evidence appraisal and practical choice

**Inference and evidence.** Separate the estimand—the quantity the study intends to learn—from the statistic reported by a field method. A sample mean, proportion or index has a denominator and inclusion rule; a demographic forecast adds model assumptions; a management classification adds policy judgment. Ask whether the observation domain is representative of the population for which a recommendation is made. A confidence interval can capture sampling variation while omitting systematic selection bias. Apply this lens to **extinction mechanisms and timescales**: identify the actual observation, the larger unsupported inference and the smallest useful independent check. Explain how new evidence might change the next action.

**Measurement reliability.** State whether the indicator measures abundance, occupancy, area, survival, pressure or a subjective rating. Some indicators can move in opposite directions under the same ecological change. Support urgent habitat and breeding investigations while keeping global-status claims tied to formal assessments. A decision brief must still specify feasible alternatives, baseline and rights-related constraints; no quantitative success probability is implied.

## Lesson 1.3 — IUCN Red List assessment logic

**Guiding question.** How can we distinguish a defensible finding about **iucn red list assessment logic** from a pattern that could arise through an alternative mechanism or a biased sample? The aim is to explain the ecological idea, define an appropriate quantity, reproduce an elementary numerical example, interrogate a counterargument, and connect the evidence to a legitimate practical decision.

### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

IUCN categories use evidence under criteria concerning population reduction, geographic range, small population and decline, very small or restricted populations, and quantitative extinction analyses; thresholds are governed by official guidance. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? Name one observation that could contradict the explanation and distinguish the measured state from its inferred cause.

### A field measurement contract

Compile extent of occurrence, area of occupancy, population trends, maturity, generations and threat evidence with transparent uncertainty and geographic scale. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — mechanism-based evidence.** Construct a directed causal diagram with the proposed driver, mediating process, ecological response and confounders. Association can be entirely real yet not caused by the proposed mechanism; test plausible alternatives. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

### Synthetic ecological case and competing interpretation

A synthetic amphibian has shrinking occupied streams but unmeasured abundance; the record supports investigation, not an automatic category assignment. This is a fictional case, not a measured local trend. Identify response, suspected driver and an unmeasured common cause. Would the conclusion change if the monitoring method became more sensitive?

**Main interpretive trap.** A map boundary is not itself area of occupancy; a local threat category does not automatically equal global IUCN status. Name a comparison site and independent indicator that could expose this error. Specify the result that would force revision of the preferred explanation.

### Worked quantitative case: Occupied cell fraction

**Assumed invented input.** Fourteen occupied grid cells out of 35 sampled cells, an occupancy descriptor rather than IUCN category evidence.

**Expression:** `14/35*100`. **Calculated answer:** **40 % occupied cells**.

Identify the units and denominator. This arithmetic alone supplies no uncertainty interval or causal evidence. Change a central input by 20%; report whether the management interpretation changes and why.

### Evidence appraisal and practical choice

**Decision quality.** An actionable claim should identify a decision maker, a feasible alternative, an indicator that responds on the appropriate time scale and a rule for revisiting the choice. When both options could harm affected people, ecological urgency does not remove the obligation to identify rights and safeguards. Note whether the action prevents a pressure, repairs a process, protects remaining habitat or merely improves knowledge. The intended outcome must match that pathway. Apply this lens to **iucn red list assessment logic**: identify the actual observation, the larger unsupported inference and the smallest useful independent check. Explain how new evidence might change the next action.

**Uncertainty propagation.** Distinguish variation among sites, error in individual measurements, uncertainty in modeled parameters and lack of knowledge about causal structure. Combining them into a single unsupported percentage hides weakness. Refer actual classifications to current IUCN documentation and qualified assessors; this book does not issue classifications. ## Lesson 1.4 — Direct drivers and pressure pathways

**Guiding question.** How can we distinguish a defensible finding about **direct drivers and pressure pathways** from a pattern that could arise through an alternative mechanism or a biased sample? The aim is to explain the ecological idea, define an appropriate quantity, reproduce an elementary numerical example, interrogate a counterargument, and connect the evidence to a legitimate practical decision.

### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

Land- and sea-use change, direct exploitation, climate change, pollution and invasive alien species are widely recognized drivers; their relative influence differs by habitat, taxon, location and time. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? Name one observation that could contradict the explanation and distinguish the measured state from its inferred cause.

### A field measurement contract

Build a threat pathway from source through exposure to biological response, recording temporal sequence and plausible alternative causes. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — hierarchical evidence.** Separate raw observations from modeled parameters, predictions, classifications and management choices. Model uncertainty often has several layers; numerical output should not hide unmeasured inputs. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

### Synthetic ecological case and competing interpretation

A river fish decline overlaps upstream sediment release, new barriers and rising summer temperature, making any one-factor story underdetermined. This is a fictional case, not a measured local trend. Identify response, suspected driver and an unmeasured common cause. Would the conclusion change if the monitoring method became more sensitive?

**Main interpretive trap.** Counting threats does not quantify causal contribution; a pressure score based on expert opinion may conceal disagreement or double count interactions. Name a comparison site and independent indicator that could expose this error. Specify the result that would force revision of the preferred explanation.

### Worked quantitative case: Threat exposure fraction

**Assumed invented input.** Twenty-eight of seventy surveyed sites have independently documented exposure to one named pressure.

**Expression:** `28/70*100`. **Calculated answer:** **40 % sites exposed**.

Identify the units and denominator. This arithmetic alone supplies no uncertainty interval or causal evidence. Change a central input by 20%; report whether the management interpretation changes and why.

### Evidence appraisal and practical choice

**Research integrity.** Scientific disagreement is most useful when it is attached to a falsifiable assumption. Document the version of the habitat map, taxonomic name, protocol, instrument calibration and analysis rules used in the inference. If a relevant record must be generalized for species protection or cultural confidentiality, explain that limitation without revealing the restricted information. A transparent report can be reproducible without disclosing sensitive coordinates. Explain how new evidence might change the next action.

**Implementation feasibility.** State equipment, expertise, permits, tenure and maintenance requirements. A biological gain that cannot be delivered under the available institutions is not a feasible forecast. Select interventions that address mechanisms and monitor whether the supposed pressure actually changes. ## Lesson 1.5 — Conservation priorities and opportunity cost

**Guiding question.** How can we distinguish a defensible finding about **conservation priorities and opportunity cost** from a pattern that could arise through an alternative mechanism or a biased sample? The aim is to explain the ecological idea, define an appropriate quantity, reproduce an elementary numerical example, interrogate a counterargument, and connect the evidence to a legitimate practical decision.

### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

Scarce budgets require explicit objectives, costs, likelihood of success, reversibility, complementarity and distributional consequences; a single flagship species is rarely the only legitimate objective. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? Name one observation that could contradict the explanation and distinguish the measured state from its inferred cause.

### A field measurement contract

Compare candidate actions under consistent outcomes and realistic budgets, state the weights applied and inspect which rankings change under alternative values. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — decision-focused study.** Specify the decision that will change if a result is high or low, then choose the least complex credible measurement. Collecting more data is not always useful if the result cannot change an authorized decision. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

### Synthetic ecological case and competing interpretation

A proposed reserve has high species richness but insecure long-term governance; another offers modest gains and strong local stewardship. This is a fictional case, not a measured local trend. Identify response, suspected driver and an unmeasured common cause. Would the conclusion change if the monitoring method became more sensitive?

**Main interpretive trap.** An attractive numerical score can conceal value judgments, missing rights, ecological thresholds or uncertain implementation costs. Name a comparison site and independent indicator that could expose this error. Specify the result that would force revision of the preferred explanation.

### Worked quantitative case: Expected gain per budget unit

**Assumed invented input.** An invented intervention promises 40 outcome units with 0.75 success probability at cost 10 funding units.

**Expression:** `(40*.75)/10`. **Calculated answer:** **3 outcome units per funding unit**.

Identify the units and denominator. This arithmetic alone supplies no uncertainty interval or causal evidence. Change a central input by 20%; report whether the management interpretation changes and why.

### Evidence appraisal and practical choice

**Scale and transfer.** An effect observed in one patch does not automatically apply to a landscape, and a ten-year forecast is not validated by a two-month trial. Consider spatial heterogeneity, ecological feedbacks, temporal autocorrelation and the potential displacement of pressures. When extending a conclusion, explicitly identify which biological processes are plausibly stable and which depend on new conditions. Poor transportability often matters more than precision within the original sample. Apply this lens to **conservation priorities and opportunity cost**: identify the actual observation, the larger unsupported inference and the smallest useful independent check. Explain how new evidence might change the next action.

**Stakeholder assessment.** Identify who gains, who bears costs, who controls access and who can challenge a decision. Good biodiversity statistics do not supply consent or equitable governance. Publish decision assumptions and alternatives; avoid presenting one formula as ethically neutral. A decision brief must still specify feasible alternatives, baseline and rights-related constraints; no quantitative success probability is implied.

## Integrated unit investigation — Estuary biodiversity priority portfolio

**Research and management setting.** Five habitats include tidal marsh, mudflat, mangrove edge, freshwater pools and grassland fringe. Compare recorded specialist diversity, area and local dependency. A high-richness habitat should not automatically outvote one containing irreplaceable specialist assemblages. This dataset is fictional and is not sourced from an actual site. Begin by specifying the intended population and the decision that requires evidence. A useful research question should distinguish a state variable, such as breeding occupancy or native habitat condition, from management inputs, such as field labor or expenditure. Decide which indicator could in principle move because the proposed intervention changed an ecological mechanism rather than because the observation process was altered.

**Classroom data.** Synthetically assign habitat areas [60,25,80,15,40] hectares and field-observed specialist taxa [6,8,4,5,3]. Put each synthetic observation into a small table with a column for its unit, survey date or period, uncertainty and collection method. If an input is a judgment or a scenario assumption rather than an observation, label it as such. Draw a diagram showing how each measurement relates to the ecological process. Check dimensions before constructing composite indices: an area in hectares and a conservation score without units cannot be added without an explicit decision rule.

**Analysis assignment.** Create a decision matrix with different weights on ecological irreplaceability, management feasibility and affected community priorities; report how the chosen action changes. For each alternative, state what is observed, what is assumed and what would require external validation. Give a best-case and a downside scenario, but do not attach unsupported probabilities. Explain which changes in sampling design or stakeholder priorities would change the conclusion. Illustrate your results with a chart that shows raw points alongside summary values; avoid hiding sample size in a visually smooth curve.

**Critical evaluation.** State which indicators would need independent botanical or faunal identification before a real planning decision. A responsible report names at least two plausible alternative explanations. Propose a stronger monitoring design with a baseline or counterfactual, a biological outcome metric, a record of implementation and a realistic revision trigger. If the intervention entails handling wildlife, exposing locations or changing access, identify independent specialist review, legal permissions and affected decision makers; the invented project grants none of those permissions.

**Submission format.** Produce (1) a one-page causal map, (2) a reproducible calculation sheet, (3) a table separating raw observations from modeled outputs, (4) a comparison of intervention and nonintervention alternatives, and (5) a short decision memo stating uncertainty and who can authorize further work. Peer reviewers should be able to find a changed assumption and recompute the conclusion without modifying undocumented spreadsheet cells. Respond in writing to one challenge from a skeptical reviewer rather than simply repeating the original recommendation.
