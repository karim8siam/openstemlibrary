# Unit 06 — Ecosystem-specific applications and trade-offs

> **Book 18 / undergraduate editorial draft**. All numerical cases in this book are invented for classroom use. Independent academic review is pending.

## Core unit inquiry

**Question:** How do interventions change when the ecological system and its human uses change?

**Bounded setting:** hydrology, fisheries, forest condition, working landscapes and climate interventions. **Fictional running setting:** a constructed catchment from upland forest to coastal reef. The five lessons share the conceptual aim of following an ecological claim from biological mechanism to measurement, inference and a justifiable decision. Single indicators such as vegetation cover, catch or carbon stock cannot represent all ecosystem functions. The emphasis is neither pessimism nor optimism; it is disciplined reasoning about actions that influence living systems over time.

Conservation programs cannot use one universal prescription across freshwater catchments, marine systems, forests and production landscapes. A river may require the timing of floods, not only annual mean discharge. A marine closure may affect fishing effort outside the boundary. Tree planting may improve a carbon metric while replacing species-rich native grassland. Farms operate within livelihood constraints that shape adoption. Climate mitigation creates additional trade-offs through energy infrastructure and land allocation.

These examples expose a common failure: substitution of a convenient proxy for a multi-dimensional objective. Habitat condition should be assessed in units relevant to resident organisms; human impacts must be counted inside a realistic geographical and economic boundary. The five lessons develop different measurement contracts, but all require explicit comparison to an alternative that would otherwise have occurred.

Before starting, create a two-column notebook. In the first column write statements that directly follow from a defined observation; in the second place the wider interpretation that needs extra assumptions. At the end of the unit, record which observation would most change your preferred conservation decision. This exercise is deliberately not a ranking of students' values. Its purpose is to make the relationship between observations, inference and public choices explicit.

### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

Marine biodiversity responds to habitat integrity, extraction, temperature, acidification and connectivity; protected areas may affect both biomass and distribution of effort. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? Name one observation that could contradict the explanation and distinguish the measured state from its inferred cause.

Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — repeat observations through time.** Schedule repeated observations during biologically informative seasons and record observer effort, equipment and protocol changes. A trend can reflect a shift in the detection process; retain a calibration subset when methods change. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

### Synthetic ecological case and competing interpretation

A synthetic reef closure shows larger resident fish but nearby unprotected reefs experience increased harvesting. This is a fictional case, not a measured local trend. **Main interpretive trap.** Catch-per-unit-effort can rise because technology improves or fish aggregate, not because total abundance rises. Name a comparison site and independent indicator that could expose this error. Specify the result that would force revision of the preferred explanation.

**Assumed invented input.** An area mean of 28 cm versus comparison mean 20 cm, before adjustment for fishing and habitat.

**Expression:** `(28-20)/20*100`. **Calculated answer:** **40 % difference**.

Identify the units and denominator. This arithmetic alone supplies no uncertainty interval or causal evidence. Change a central input by 20%; report whether the management interpretation changes and why.

### Evidence appraisal and practical choice

**Inference and evidence.** Separate the estimand—the quantity the study intends to learn—from the statistic reported by a field method. A sample mean, proportion or index has a denominator and inclusion rule; a demographic forecast adds model assumptions; a management classification adds policy judgment. Ask whether the observation domain is representative of the population for which a recommendation is made. ## Lesson 6.3 — Forest conservation and carbon trade-offs

**Guiding question.** How can we distinguish a defensible finding about **forest conservation and carbon trade-offs** from a pattern that could arise through an alternative mechanism or a biased sample? The aim is to explain the ecological idea, define an appropriate quantity, reproduce an elementary numerical example, interrogate a counterargument, and connect the evidence to a legitimate practical decision.

### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

Forest carbon, biodiversity, water regulation and livelihoods overlap but cannot be measured with a single proxy; plantations may store carbon while supporting fewer native specialists. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? Name one observation that could contradict the explanation and distinguish the measured state from its inferred cause.

### A field measurement contract

Map native composition, age structure, disturbance, carbon stocks, land tenure and long-term leakage or permanence risk. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — mechanism-based evidence.** Construct a directed causal diagram with the proposed driver, mediating process, ecological response and confounders. Association can be entirely real yet not caused by the proposed mechanism; test plausible alternatives. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

### Synthetic ecological case and competing interpretation

A proposed monoculture planting increases measured tree cover faster than assisted regeneration of native forest. This is a fictional case, not a measured local trend. **Main interpretive trap.** Short-term carbon uptake cannot establish equal biodiversity outcomes, and carbon projects can affect land access. Name a comparison site and independent indicator that could expose this error. Specify the result that would force revision of the preferred explanation.

**Assumed invented input.** Thirty-five hectares of verified native forest in an eighty-hectare area of tree cover.

### Evidence appraisal and practical choice

**Decision quality.** An actionable claim should identify a decision maker, a feasible alternative, an indicator that responds on the appropriate time scale and a rule for revisiting the choice. When both options could harm affected people, ecological urgency does not remove the obligation to identify rights and safeguards. Note whether the action prevents a pressure, repairs a process, protects remaining habitat or merely improves knowledge. The intended outcome must match that pathway. ## Lesson 6.5 — Climate and biodiversity interventions

**Guiding question.** How can we distinguish a defensible finding about **climate and biodiversity interventions** from a pattern that could arise through an alternative mechanism or a biased sample? The aim is to explain the ecological idea, define an appropriate quantity, reproduce an elementary numerical example, interrogate a counterargument, and connect the evidence to a legitimate practical decision.

### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

Climate mitigation can benefit ecosystems through avoided loss and restoration, but poorly placed infrastructure, afforestation or offset projects can damage biodiversity. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? Name one observation that could contradict the explanation and distinguish the measured state from its inferred cause.

### A field measurement contract

Evaluate intervention counterfactuals, habitat conversion, lifecycle emissions, species response, permanence and governance. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — decision-focused study.** Specify the decision that will change if a result is high or low, then choose the least complex credible measurement. Collecting more data is not always useful if the result cannot change an authorized decision. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

### Synthetic ecological case and competing interpretation

A solar installation would displace intact dry grassland while an alternate degraded site has lower output and higher grid costs. This is a fictional case, not a measured local trend. Identify response, suspected driver and an unmeasured common cause. Would the conclusion change if the monitoring method became more sensitive?

**Main interpretive trap.** Treating every low-carbon project as biodiversity-positive overlooks habitat trade-offs and opportunity costs. Name a comparison site and independent indicator that could expose this error. Specify the result that would force revision of the preferred explanation.

### Worked quantitative case: Avoided habitat conversion value

**Assumed invented input.** One hundred hectares baseline and projected 12% conversion without intervention, purely scenario arithmetic.

**Expression:** `100*(1-.12)`. **Calculated answer:** **88 hectares remaining**.

Identify the units and denominator. This arithmetic alone supplies no uncertainty interval or causal evidence. Change a central input by 20%; report whether the management interpretation changes and why.

### Evidence appraisal and practical choice

**Scale and transfer.** An effect observed in one patch does not automatically apply to a landscape, and a ten-year forecast is not validated by a two-month trial. Consider spatial heterogeneity, ecological feedbacks, temporal autocorrelation and the potential displacement of pressures. When extending a conclusion, explicitly identify which biological processes are plausibly stable and which depend on new conditions. Poor transportability often matters more than precision within the original sample. Apply this lens to **climate and biodiversity interventions**: identify the actual observation, the larger unsupported inference and the smallest useful independent check. Explain how new evidence might change the next action.

**Stakeholder assessment.** Identify who gains, who bears costs, who controls access and who can challenge a decision. Good biodiversity statistics do not supply consent or equitable governance. Explicitly compare sites, designs and ecological safeguards under realistic climate objectives. A decision brief must still specify feasible alternatives, baseline and rights-related constraints; no quantitative success probability is implied.

## Integrated unit investigation — Catchment-to-coast intervention portfolio

**Research and management setting.** A proposed upstream hydrological change may alter wetland habitat, fish migration, mangrove conditions and coastal livelihoods. This dataset is fictional and is not sourced from an actual site. Begin by specifying the intended population and the decision that requires evidence. A useful research question should distinguish a state variable, such as breeding occupancy or native habitat condition, from management inputs, such as field labor or expenditure. Decide which indicator could in principle move because the proposed intervention changed an ecological mechanism rather than because the observation process was altered.

**Classroom data.** Invented dry-season flow values are [16,20,25,21] units across four years; fisher household reports of poor catch are [18,15,11,13]. Put each synthetic observation into a small table with a column for its unit, survey date or period, uncertainty and collection method. If an input is a judgment or a scenario assumption rather than an observation, label it as such. Draw a diagram showing how each measurement relates to the ecological process. Check dimensions before constructing composite indices: an area in hectares and a conservation score without units cannot be added without an explicit decision rule.

**Analysis assignment.** Separate ecological and economic drivers, choose upstream and downstream indicators and explain how confounding can produce an association. For each alternative, state what is observed, what is assumed and what would require external validation. Give a best-case and a downside scenario, but do not attach unsupported probabilities. Explain which changes in sampling design or stakeholder priorities would change the conclusion. Illustrate your results with a chart that shows raw points alongside summary values; avoid hiding sample size in a visually smooth curve.

**Critical evaluation.** Do not infer causation from four numbers or suggest changing flows without legal environmental review and community consultation. A responsible report names at least two plausible alternative explanations. Propose a stronger monitoring design with a baseline or counterfactual, a biological outcome metric, a record of implementation and a realistic revision trigger. If the intervention entails handling wildlife, exposing locations or changing access, identify independent specialist review, legal permissions and affected decision makers; the invented project grants none of those permissions.

**Submission format.** Produce (1) a one-page causal map, (2) a reproducible calculation sheet, (3) a table separating raw observations from modeled outputs, (4) a comparison of intervention and nonintervention alternatives, and (5) a short decision memo stating uncertainty and who can authorize further work. Peer reviewers should be able to find a changed assumption and recompute the conclusion without modifying undocumented spreadsheet cells. Respond in writing to one challenge from a skeptical reviewer rather than simply repeating the original recommendation.
