# Unit 05 — Conservation interventions and restoration

> **Book 18 / undergraduate editorial draft**. All numerical cases in this book are invented for classroom use. Independent academic review is pending.

## Core unit inquiry

**Question:** How can actions be selected, implemented and audited without mistaking effort for ecological impact?

**Bounded setting:** practical interventions, biological responses and documented non-target costs. **Fictional running setting:** a set of intervention trials across disturbed coastal habitats. The five lessons share the conceptual aim of following an ecological claim from biological mechanism to measurement, inference and a justifiable decision. Planting, patrols and releases are activities; their conservation significance depends on ecological outcomes. The emphasis is neither pessimism nor optimism; it is disciplined reasoning about actions that influence living systems over time.

Conservation practice often reports easy-to-count activities: trees planted, hectares designated, patrol hours or animals released. Those are important operational records, but they do not necessarily measure avoided extinction, restored ecosystem function or sustainable populations. Success needs a biologically relevant endpoint, an adequate baseline and a strategy for separating intervention effects from coincident weather, land-use or demographic changes.

Protected areas, restoration, invasive-species management, ex situ programs and wildlife-use regulation work under different causal pathways. Each can also cause non-target harm. Removal of an introduced species may affect another species; release of captive animals may transfer disease; a closure can shift resource extraction elsewhere. Evaluate practical alternatives, necessary authorizations, affected groups, biological time scales and the possibility of stopping when evidence contradicts the original hypothesis.

Before starting, create a two-column notebook. In the first column write statements that directly follow from a defined observation; in the second place the wider interpretation that needs extra assumptions. At the end of the unit, record which observation would most change your preferred conservation decision. This exercise is deliberately not a ranking of students' values. Its purpose is to make the relationship between observations, inference and public choices explicit.

## Lesson 5.2 — Restoration trajectories and reference ecosystems

**Guiding question.** How can we distinguish a defensible finding about **restoration trajectories and reference ecosystems** from a pattern that could arise through an alternative mechanism or a biased sample? The aim is to explain the ecological idea, define an appropriate quantity, reproduce an elementary numerical example, interrogate a counterargument, and connect the evidence to a legitimate practical decision.

### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

Restoration seeks recovery of processes, native biota and resilience relative to explicit goals; trajectories may be nonlinear and historical reference states may be unattainable under change. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? Name one observation that could contradict the explanation and distinguish the measured state from its inferred cause.

### A field measurement contract

Monitor reference and intervention sites for hydrology, soils, recruitment, species composition and time since action. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — repeat observations through time.** Schedule repeated observations during biologically informative seasons and record observer effort, equipment and protocol changes. A trend can reflect a shift in the detection process; retain a calibration subset when methods change. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

### Synthetic ecological case and competing interpretation

A restored wetland gains vegetation cover rapidly but remains nutrient-rich and supports few wetland specialists. This is a fictional case, not a measured local trend. **Main interpretive trap.** Planting survival is an early indicator, not proof of restored food webs or self-sustaining function. Name a comparison site and independent indicator that could expose this error. Specify the result that would force revision of the preferred explanation.

**Assumed invented input.** Observed structural score 72 compared with reference score 90; arbitrary composite units.

**Expression:** `72/90*100`. **Calculated answer:** **80 % reference level**.

Identify the units and denominator. This arithmetic alone supplies no uncertainty interval or causal evidence. Change a central input by 20%; report whether the management interpretation changes and why.

### Evidence appraisal and practical choice

**Scale and transfer.** An effect observed in one patch does not automatically apply to a landscape, and a ten-year forecast is not validated by a two-month trial. Consider spatial heterogeneity, ecological feedbacks, temporal autocorrelation and the potential displacement of pressures. When extending a conclusion, explicitly identify which biological processes are plausibly stable and which depend on new conditions. **Comparator selection.** Choose untreated or reference sites using ecological similarity rather than ease of access. A valid comparator can differ from a desired restoration state but must represent a plausible alternative trajectory. Set staged criteria and plan long-term maintenance, invasive control and adaptive adjustments. ### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

Invasive alien species can alter ecological processes and threaten native biodiversity, but management risk, feasibility and welfare implications depend on species and setting. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? ### A field measurement contract

Identify taxon accurately and establish invasion pathway, affected biodiversity, cost, non-target impacts and post-action surveillance. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — mechanism-based evidence.** Construct a directed causal diagram with the proposed driver, mediating process, ecological response and confounders. **Field reasoning.** Represent the causal pathway as a set of stages: ecological source condition, exposure or interaction, demographic or community response, observed indicator and management interpretation. ## Lesson 5.4 — Ex situ conservation and reintroduction

**Guiding question.** How can we distinguish a defensible finding about **ex situ conservation and reintroduction** from a pattern that could arise through an alternative mechanism or a biased sample? The aim is to explain the ecological idea, define an appropriate quantity, reproduce an elementary numerical example, interrogate a counterargument, and connect the evidence to a legitimate practical decision.

### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

Seed banks, captive breeding and assurance populations may buffer extinction risk but cannot substitute for suitable habitat, genetic management or removal of original threats. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? Name one observation that could contradict the explanation and distinguish the measured state from its inferred cause.

### A field measurement contract

Track provenance, founder diversity, health, behavior, release-site quality, post-release survival and breeding success. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — hierarchical evidence.** Separate raw observations from modeled parameters, predictions, classifications and management choices. Model uncertainty often has several layers; numerical output should not hide unmeasured inputs. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

### Synthetic ecological case and competing interpretation

A fictional rare plant produces thousands of banked seeds but has no secure habitat available for germination. This is a fictional case, not a measured local trend. Identify response, suspected driver and an unmeasured common cause. Would the conclusion change if the monitoring method became more sensitive?

**Main interpretive trap.** Counting stored seeds or animals is not the same as demonstrating viable restored wild populations. Name a comparison site and independent indicator that could expose this error. Specify the result that would force revision of the preferred explanation.

### Worked quantitative case: Seed bank viability fraction

**Assumed invented input.** Three hundred eighty viable seeds under a hypothetical assay from a stored sample of five hundred.

**Expression:** `380/500*100`. **Calculated answer:** **76 % viable**.

Identify the units and denominator. This arithmetic alone supplies no uncertainty interval or causal evidence. Change a central input by 20%; report whether the management interpretation changes and why.

### Evidence appraisal and practical choice

**Inference and evidence.** Separate the estimand—the quantity the study intends to learn—from the statistic reported by a field method. A sample mean, proportion or index has a denominator and inclusion rule; a demographic forecast adds model assumptions; a management classification adds policy judgment. Ask whether the observation domain is representative of the population for which a recommendation is made. A confidence interval can capture sampling variation while omitting systematic selection bias. **Uncertainty propagation.** Distinguish variation among sites, error in individual measurements, uncertainty in modeled parameters and lack of knowledge about causal structure. Combining them into a single unsupported percentage hides weakness. Plan ex situ measures alongside habitat protection and realistic reintroduction goals. ## Lesson 5.5 — Wildlife use, trade and enforcement

**Guiding question.** How can we distinguish a defensible finding about **wildlife use, trade and enforcement** from a pattern that could arise through an alternative mechanism or a biased sample? The aim is to explain the ecological idea, define an appropriate quantity, reproduce an elementary numerical example, interrogate a counterargument, and connect the evidence to a legitimate practical decision.

### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

Sustainable use requires harvest below recoverable population productivity and credible enforcement, while international trade adds identification, market and governance challenges. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? Name one observation that could contradict the explanation and distinguish the measured state from its inferred cause.

### A field measurement contract

Measure population structure, take, reporting completeness, cross-border supply chains and livelihood effects without exposing sensitive enforcement procedures. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — decision-focused study.** Specify the decision that will change if a result is high or low, then choose the least complex credible measurement. Collecting more data is not always useful if the result cannot change an authorized decision. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

**Assumed invented input.** Sixty-eight recorded transactions among one hundred audited opportunities, not a wild population estimate.

**Decision quality.** An actionable claim should identify a decision maker, a feasible alternative, an indicator that responds on the appropriate time scale and a rule for revisiting the choice. ## Integrated unit investigation — Restoration versus protected-area maintenance

**Research and management setting.** One management team proposes planting native vegetation; another argues that preventing recurrent drainage is the upstream intervention. A third proposes invasive-species controls. This dataset is fictional and is not sourced from an actual site. Begin by specifying the intended population and the decision that requires evidence. A useful research question should distinguish a state variable, such as breeding occupancy or native habitat condition, from management inputs, such as field labor or expenditure. Decide which indicator could in principle move because the proposed intervention changed an ecological mechanism rather than because the observation process was altered.

**Classroom data.** Invented mean native plant cover in before/after intervention sites is 12%/38%, with reference sites at 28%/40%. Put each synthetic observation into a small table with a column for its unit, survey date or period, uncertainty and collection method. If an input is a judgment or a scenario assumption rather than an observation, label it as such. Draw a diagram showing how each measurement relates to the ecological process. Check dimensions before constructing composite indices: an area in hectares and a conservation score without units cannot be added without an explicit decision rule.

**Analysis assignment.** Calculate observed changes and a difference-in-differences contrast, then assess whether the control is sufficiently comparable. For each alternative, state what is observed, what is assumed and what would require external validation. Give a best-case and a downside scenario, but do not attach unsupported probabilities. Explain which changes in sampling design or stakeholder priorities would change the conclusion. Illustrate your results with a chart that shows raw points alongside summary values; avoid hiding sample size in a visually smooth curve.

**Critical evaluation.** Outline monitoring of nutrient export, vegetation composition and rights impacts that could invalidate the apparent short-run gain. A responsible report names at least two plausible alternative explanations. Propose a stronger monitoring design with a baseline or counterfactual, a biological outcome metric, a record of implementation and a realistic revision trigger. If the intervention entails handling wildlife, exposing locations or changing access, identify independent specialist review, legal permissions and affected decision makers; the invented project grants none of those permissions.

**Submission format.** Produce (1) a one-page causal map, (2) a reproducible calculation sheet, (3) a table separating raw observations from modeled outputs, (4) a comparison of intervention and nonintervention alternatives, and (5) a short decision memo stating uncertainty and who can authorize further work. Peer reviewers should be able to find a changed assumption and recompute the conclusion without modifying undocumented spreadsheet cells. Respond in writing to one challenge from a skeptical reviewer rather than simply repeating the original recommendation.
