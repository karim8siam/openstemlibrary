# Unit 08 — Evidence, adaptive management and integrated planning

> **Book 18 / undergraduate editorial draft**. All numerical cases in this book are invented for classroom use. Independent academic review is pending.

## Core unit inquiry

**Question:** How can a conservation program demonstrate additional impacts and learn when assumptions fail?

**Bounded setting:** comparative impact estimates, monitored decisions and linked objectives. **Fictional running setting:** a multi-year program in a fictional delta landscape. The five lessons share the conceptual aim of following an ecological claim from biological mechanism to measurement, inference and a justifiable decision. A management plan is a sequence of testable causal claims, not merely a list of planned activities. The emphasis is neither pessimism nor optimism; it is disciplined reasoning about actions that influence living systems over time.

An integrated conservation program can fail even when each planned activity looks reasonable. Without a theory of change, a credible counterfactual, monitoring and authority to adapt, results cannot distinguish successful intervention from a favorable climate year. Cost-effectiveness analyses can help choose among interventions but are only as reliable as their biological outcome metrics and scenario assumptions. Long-term records need calibration and provenance so that shifts in equipment are not mistaken for population recovery.

The last unit treats conservation as organized learning under uncertainty. Build a logical chain from threats to intended action, from actions to mechanisms, from mechanisms to observed outcomes and from outcomes to an explicit decision rule. Name what would falsify the model. A plan becomes practical only when responsibilities, rights, funding, monitoring schedule and response to adverse evidence are also clear.

Before starting, create a two-column notebook. In the first column write statements that directly follow from a defined observation; in the second place the wider interpretation that needs extra assumptions. At the end of the unit, record which observation would most change your preferred conservation decision. This exercise is deliberately not a ranking of students' values. Its purpose is to make the relationship between observations, inference and public choices explicit.

## Lesson 8.1 — Counterfactual impact evaluation

**Guiding question.** How can we distinguish a defensible finding about **counterfactual impact evaluation** from a pattern that could arise through an alternative mechanism or a biased sample? ### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

An observed improvement after an intervention does not show the intervention caused it; credible impact inference asks what would have happened without the action. ### A field measurement contract

Use matched controls, before-after-control-impact designs or other defensible causal approaches; examine pre-trends and spillovers. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — comparison across space.** Select at least three independent locations within each management class, describe how sites were chosen and report environmental covariates. Differences between sites may precede the management action; avoid claiming intervention effects from one-time cross-sectional samples. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

A restored marsh gains birds during a wet year, but untreated marshes gain a similar number of birds. This is a fictional case, not a measured local trend. Identify response, suspected driver and an unmeasured common cause. Would the conclusion change if the monitoring method became more sensitive?

**Main interpretive trap.** Before-after charts exaggerate effect when climate or landscape trends change both treated and untreated sites. Name a comparison site and independent indicator that could expose this error. Specify the result that would force revision of the preferred explanation.

### Worked quantitative case: Difference-in-differences effect

**Assumed invented input.** Treatment increases from 16 to 24 and controls from 14 to 18, yielding an additional +4 count units under assumptions.

**Expression:** `(24-16)-(18-14)`. **Calculated answer:** **4 count units**.

Identify the units and denominator. This arithmetic alone supplies no uncertainty interval or causal evidence. Change a central input by 20%; report whether the management interpretation changes and why.

### Evidence appraisal and practical choice

**Scale and transfer.** An effect observed in one patch does not automatically apply to a landscape, and a ten-year forecast is not validated by a two-month trial. Consider spatial heterogeneity, ecological feedbacks, temporal autocorrelation and the potential displacement of pressures. When extending a conclusion, explicitly identify which biological processes are plausibly stable and which depend on new conditions. Poor transportability often matters more than precision within the original sample. Apply this lens to **counterfactual impact evaluation**: identify the actual observation, the larger unsupported inference and the smallest useful independent check. Explain how new evidence might change the next action.

**Uncertainty propagation.** Distinguish variation among sites, error in individual measurements, uncertainty in modeled parameters and lack of knowledge about causal structure. Combining them into a single unsupported percentage hides weakness. Report the difference in changes and limitations of comparison groups rather than only the intervention-site trend. A decision brief must still specify feasible alternatives, baseline and rights-related constraints; no quantitative success probability is implied.

### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

Adaptive management is planned learning under uncertainty, with explicit actions, monitoring, decision triggers and institutional capacity to change course. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? Name one observation that could contradict the explanation and distinguish the measured state from its inferred cause.

### A field measurement contract

Predefine measurable outcomes, safety safeguards, monitoring frequency and response options before implementation. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — repeat observations through time.** Schedule repeated observations during biologically informative seasons and record observer effort, equipment and protocol changes. A trend can reflect a shift in the detection process; retain a calibration subset when methods change. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

A fictional reserve trials two fire-management regimes and adjusts practice when native plant recruitment differs from predictions. This is a fictional case, not a measured local trend. **Main interpretive trap.** Informal improvisation is not adaptive management if hypotheses, monitoring and accountable decisions are absent. Name a comparison site and independent indicator that could expose this error. Specify the result that would force revision of the preferred explanation.

**Assumed invented input.** A hypothetical index 32 versus reference 40; intervention trigger is a value judgment, not an ecological law.

**Expression:** `(40-32)/40*100`. **Calculated answer:** **20 % lower than reference**.

### Evidence appraisal and practical choice

**Field reasoning.** Represent the causal pathway as a set of stages: ecological source condition, exposure or interaction, demographic or community response, observed indicator and management interpretation. Describe which stage the proposed evidence directly addresses. A value measured at the end of a chain is not automatically a direct measurement of its cause. ## Lesson 8.3 — Cost-effectiveness and multi-criteria ranking

**Guiding question.** How can we distinguish a defensible finding about **cost-effectiveness and multi-criteria ranking** from a pattern that could arise through an alternative mechanism or a biased sample? The aim is to explain the ecological idea, define an appropriate quantity, reproduce an elementary numerical example, interrogate a counterargument, and connect the evidence to a legitimate practical decision.

### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

Conservation value cannot be reduced to inexpensive hectares; costs should be related to additional persistence, habitat quality, equity and feasibility under transparent objectives. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? Name one observation that could contradict the explanation and distinguish the measured state from its inferred cause.

### A field measurement contract

Use discounted lifecycle costs, implementation probability, uncertain benefit, ecosystem complementarity and equity constraints across options. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — mechanism-based evidence.** Construct a directed causal diagram with the proposed driver, mediating process, ecological response and confounders. Association can be entirely real yet not caused by the proposed mechanism; test plausible alternatives. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

### Synthetic ecological case and competing interpretation

Two interventions cost USD 100,000 each, but differ in survival benefit, maintenance obligations and tenure risks. This is a fictional case, not a measured local trend. Identify response, suspected driver and an unmeasured common cause. Would the conclusion change if the monitoring method became more sensitive?

**Main interpretive trap.** A single ratio conceals uncertain denominators, values and long-term financing; low initial cost can create high future liabilities. Name a comparison site and independent indicator that could expose this error. Specify the result that would force revision of the preferred explanation.

### Worked quantitative case: Expected gain after execution risk

**Assumed invented input.** Eighty units conditional benefit and 65% assumed execution probability; values require elicitation and sensitivity testing.

**Expression:** `80*.65`. **Calculated answer:** **52 expected outcome units**.

Identify the units and denominator. This arithmetic alone supplies no uncertainty interval or causal evidence. Change a central input by 20%; report whether the management interpretation changes and why.

### Evidence appraisal and practical choice

**Inference and evidence.** Separate the estimand—the quantity the study intends to learn—from the statistic reported by a field method. A sample mean, proportion or index has a denominator and inclusion rule; a demographic forecast adds model assumptions; a management classification adds policy judgment. Ask whether the observation domain is representative of the population for which a recommendation is made. A confidence interval can capture sampling variation while omitting systematic selection bias. Explain how new evidence might change the next action.

**Stakeholder assessment.** Identify who gains, who bears costs, who controls access and who can challenge a decision. Good biodiversity statistics do not supply consent or equitable governance. Present scenario ranges and non-negotiable rights or safety constraints before optimizing portfolios. A decision brief must still specify feasible alternatives, baseline and rights-related constraints; no quantitative success probability is implied.

## Lesson 8.4 — Long-term monitoring and early warning

**Guiding question.** How can we distinguish a defensible finding about **long-term monitoring and early warning** from a pattern that could arise through an alternative mechanism or a biased sample? The aim is to explain the ecological idea, define an appropriate quantity, reproduce an elementary numerical example, interrogate a counterargument, and connect the evidence to a legitimate practical decision.

### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

Long-lived species and delayed ecological processes require observations across time scales; changes in effort, taxonomy or equipment can masquerade as biodiversity trends. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? Name one observation that could contradict the explanation and distinguish the measured state from its inferred cause.

### A field measurement contract

Maintain versioned protocols, permanent sample locations, calibration, open-but-responsible data and predefined alert rules. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — hierarchical evidence.** Separate raw observations from modeled parameters, predictions, classifications and management choices. Model uncertainty often has several layers; numerical output should not hide unmeasured inputs. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

### Synthetic ecological case and competing interpretation

A decade-long acoustic record shows more detections after new microphones replace less sensitive devices. This is a fictional case, not a measured local trend. Identify response, suspected driver and an unmeasured common cause. Would the conclusion change if the monitoring method became more sensitive?

**Main interpretive trap.** A detection increase does not necessarily reflect population growth, and annual reporting can conceal seasonal collapse. Name a comparison site and independent indicator that could expose this error. Specify the result that would force revision of the preferred explanation.

### Worked quantitative case: Observer effort change

**Assumed invented input.** Field monitoring hours move from ninety to one hundred twenty; trend inference requires detectability adjustment.

**Expression:** `(120-90)/90*100`. **Calculated answer:** **33.333 % effort increase**.

Identify the units and denominator. This arithmetic alone supplies no uncertainty interval or causal evidence. Change a central input by 20%; report whether the management interpretation changes and why.

### Evidence appraisal and practical choice

**Decision quality.** An actionable claim should identify a decision maker, a feasible alternative, an indicator that responds on the appropriate time scale and a rule for revisiting the choice. When both options could harm affected people, ecological urgency does not remove the obligation to identify rights and safeguards. Note whether the action prevents a pressure, repairs a process, protects remaining habitat or merely improves knowledge. The intended outcome must match that pathway. Explain how new evidence might change the next action.

**Comparator selection.** Choose untreated or reference sites using ecological similarity rather than ease of access. A valid comparator can differ from a desired restoration state but must represent a plausible alternative trajectory. Preserve calibration overlap and publish change logs alongside indicators. A decision brief must still specify feasible alternatives, baseline and rights-related constraints; no quantitative success probability is implied.

## Lesson 8.5 — Integrated conservation action plan

**Guiding question.** How can we distinguish a defensible finding about **integrated conservation action plan** from a pattern that could arise through an alternative mechanism or a biased sample? The aim is to explain the ecological idea, define an appropriate quantity, reproduce an elementary numerical example, interrogate a counterargument, and connect the evidence to a legitimate practical decision.

### The ecological mechanism and what it predicts

A defensible plan links biological objectives, spatial boundaries, rights, baseline, causal threats, feasible interventions, monitoring, costs and revision triggers in one traceable argument. For this mechanism, identify the biological scale and time interval. Does an observation concern one organism, a local population or regional persistence? Name one observation that could contradict the explanation and distinguish the measured state from its inferred cause.

### A field measurement contract

Assemble stakeholder-defined objectives, alternative actions, evidence ratings, cost projections and explicit decision-making authority. Define the sampling unit, eligibility, survey timing and denominators in advance. Keep auditable observations and appropriately restricted provenance so independent analysts can identify sources of bias.

**Methodological lens — decision-focused study.** Specify the decision that will change if a result is high or low, then choose the least complex credible measurement. Collecting more data is not always useful if the result cannot change an authorized decision. Document how site choice or altered detection could change the measured result.

### Synthetic ecological case and competing interpretation

A fictional delta landscape faces salinity intrusion, fishing pressure and wetland conversion; each driver points to a different response. This is a fictional case, not a measured local trend. Identify response, suspected driver and an unmeasured common cause. Would the conclusion change if the monitoring method became more sensitive?

**Main interpretive trap.** A long catalog of activities is not a strategy without causal logic and a credible counterfactual. Name a comparison site and independent indicator that could expose this error. Specify the result that would force revision of the preferred explanation.

**Assumed invented input.** USD 240,000 fully loaded program cost across sixty hectares of planned action; not an effectiveness measure.

**Expression:** `240000/60`. **Calculated answer:** **4,000 USD per hectare**.

### Evidence appraisal and practical choice

**Research integrity.** Scientific disagreement is most useful when it is attached to a falsifiable assumption. Document the version of the habitat map, taxonomic name, protocol, instrument calibration and analysis rules used in the inference. If a relevant record must be generalized for species protection or cultural confidentiality, explain that limitation without revealing the restricted information. A transparent report can be reproducible without disclosing sensitive coordinates. **Measurement reliability.** State whether the indicator measures abundance, occupancy, area, survival, pressure or a subjective rating. Some indicators can move in opposite directions under the same ecological change. Prioritize a small set of testable actions and revise them using independent outcome monitoring. ## Integrated unit investigation — Delta conservation causal evaluation

**Research and management setting.** A coalition seeks to restore reedbeds, improve fish passage and reduce nest disturbance; untreated sites are exposed to many of the same annual weather shifts. This dataset is fictional and is not sourced from an actual site. Begin by specifying the intended population and the decision that requires evidence. A useful research question should distinguish a state variable, such as breeding occupancy or native habitat condition, from management inputs, such as field labor or expenditure. Decide which indicator could in principle move because the proposed intervention changed an ecological mechanism rather than because the observation process was altered.

**Classroom data.** Synthetic mean breeding-pair counts: treated 16→24, comparison 14→18; effort increases 15% at treated sites. Put each synthetic observation into a small table with a column for its unit, survey date or period, uncertainty and collection method. If an input is a judgment or a scenario assumption rather than an observation, label it as such. Draw a diagram showing how each measurement relates to the ecological process. Check dimensions before constructing composite indices: an area in hectares and a conservation score without units cannot be added without an explicit decision rule.

**Analysis assignment.** Compute difference-in-differences, discuss parallel trends and effort changes, and propose a recalibration of the observed outcome. For each alternative, state what is observed, what is assumed and what would require external validation. Give a best-case and a downside scenario, but do not attach unsupported probabilities. Explain which changes in sampling design or stakeholder priorities would change the conclusion. Illustrate your results with a chart that shows raw points alongside summary values; avoid hiding sample size in a visually smooth curve.

**Critical evaluation.** Specify pre-registered revision thresholds, who approves changes, project lifecycle costs and escalation for adverse impacts. A responsible report names at least two plausible alternative explanations. Propose a stronger monitoring design with a baseline or counterfactual, a biological outcome metric, a record of implementation and a realistic revision trigger. If the intervention entails handling wildlife, exposing locations or changing access, identify independent specialist review, legal permissions and affected decision makers; the invented project grants none of those permissions.

**Submission format.** Produce (1) a one-page causal map, (2) a reproducible calculation sheet, (3) a table separating raw observations from modeled outputs, (4) a comparison of intervention and nonintervention alternatives, and (5) a short decision memo stating uncertainty and who can authorize further work. Peer reviewers should be able to find a changed assumption and recompute the conclusion without modifying undocumented spreadsheet cells. Respond in writing to one challenge from a skeptical reviewer rather than simply repeating the original recommendation.
