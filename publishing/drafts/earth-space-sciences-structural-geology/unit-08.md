# Unit 8 — Integrative structural methods and quantification

## Central question

From structural field notes to defensible geological models. Use structural observations and reproducible geometric tests to evaluate competing interpretations.

## Extended unit perspective

Structural geology uses measurements collected at strongly different scales: micrometre-scale fabrics, metre-scale outcrop geometry and regional lineaments spanning tens of kilometres. A measurement appropriate to one scale may not transfer safely to another. A remotely mapped lineament could represent a road, lithologic boundary or drainage feature; only corroborating geological evidence permits the stronger description of a fault. Vertical exaggeration can transform a modest dip into a dramatic-looking slope even though nothing has changed in the ground. Structural domains should therefore be defined by measurable differences in fabric, not merely by the appearance of a colour-shaded map. Relative chronology from crosscutting relations is powerful, but a reused fault can record several episodes and a poorly exposed intersection can be misread. Transparent work practices—original coordinates, metadata, scripts, unit conversions and diagrams—allow an independent geologist to challenge or replicate the conclusions. This final unit treats uncertainty as part of the scientific output rather than a defect to hide. Students should hold back some observations before model building, test predictions against that reserved information and avoid using classroom models for real engineering or emergency decisions.

### Field and scientific objectives

Describe relevant structural objects with location and orientation, translate them between map and section, construct a physically and chronologically consistent interpretation, and test at least one alternative. The aim is to communicate exactly which information is observed, which result follows by geometric calculation and which regional mechanism is inferred. Avoid interpreting one structural indicator as proof of a single tectonic event where earlier deformation, reactivation or anisotropic rock properties remain plausible.

## Five core lessons and worked cases
## Lesson 8.1 — Section distortion and scale effects

### Mechanism and observational scale

Vertical exaggeration changes drawn slope, apparent dip and perceived fold shape when horizontal and vertical scales differ. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed. 

### Evidence and documentation

Profiles created from the same topography at different exaggerations reveal how dip angles and fault throw appear visually. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with **source coordinates, map projection, plotting exaggeration and alternative models** and illustrate **original field facts, derived geometry and uncertainty-ranked interpretation**. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve. 

### Competing interpretation

A steep-looking contact on a display may reflect plotting convention instead of a steeper real geological plane. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

### Test and scientific protocol

Plot the identical planar contact with no exaggeration and with selected vertical exaggeration, then recover true dip mathematically. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For **section distortion and scale effects**, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error. 

### Worked numerical case 36: Mean section dip after vertical exaggeration

**Invented classroom inputs:** 18° true dip drawn with vertical exaggeration 2.5. Evaluate `math.degrees(math.atan(2.5*math.tan(math.radians(18))))` to obtain **39.0869 degrees**. Write the governing equation separately, substitute the stated values, and check the dimensions and sign convention. For a trigonometric expression identify whether angles are entered in radians or degrees; for percentages identify the reference length or population. 

Repeat the calculation after varying the least certain input. Explain whether the change is important relative to plausible field error. If the formula neglects a deformation component, assumes a planar fault or uses a restricted stress model, identify that assumption rather than interpreting the number as exact. Distinguish geometric separation from true displacement, finite strain from its time rate and stress from material strength whenever those concepts arise in the calculation. 

### Limitation, cross-check and synthesis

Oblique section orientation introduces a second independent apparent-dip effect. Consider the alternative that changes the interpretation most while retaining the measured observations. Make a two-column field record. Put verifiable geometric and material facts in the left column and causal or historical interpretation in the right. For each interpreted event, cite the specific measured feature and its location. An interpretation without such a link is a hypothesis to test rather than a supported result. 

State the horizontal and vertical scales on every geological cross-section. A defensible conclusion therefore has five components: observed feature, ideal relation used, leading interpretation, decisive contrary test and residual uncertainty. Indicate whether each is local to the measured exposure or can legitimately be extended across the mapped domain.

### Practice

1. Sketch **section distortion and scale effects** with orientation or movement arrows and a north or section reference.
2. List three direct observations and identify a plausible rival mechanism.
3. Recalculate case 36 for a revised parameter, retaining meaningful units.
4. Design a two-site or two-orientation test that distinguishes the interpretations.
5. Write a short final paragraph reporting what remains uncertain and which measurement would reduce the uncertainty.

## Integrated investigation 8 — Blind replication of a structural map

A separate student receives original orientation data, an elevation model and a marked subset of candidate lineaments but not the first interpreter’s preferred fault map. Both students must produce independent structural domains and cross-sections. Compare agreement and document differences arising from plotting scales and selection criteria. Numerically correct one exaggerated slope. Create a chronological event graph from veins and fault cuts, mark any unresolved orderings and give a final assessment of confidence without pretending that missing field evidence has been collected.

### Analysis protocol and deliverables

The following integrated investigation uses fictional measurements expressly manufactured to demonstrate methods. It is not a published field survey or a substitute for collecting data. Begin with original measurements and metadata; present interpreted structures in separate layers. Compute at least one simple quantity with its units, make two competing predictions, and decide which additional piece of evidence would be most informative. Report any failure of the data to distinguish models. Submit a map or diagram, annotated calculations, a brief uncertainty table and a paragraph explaining whether the result has any practical limitations. The first panel of the submitted figure should contain observations only. The second may show an inferred geometry, but include a legend differentiating solid observation lines from dashed extrapolations. The third should compare an alternative model and identify the measurement that would select between them. A model should be judged against the data it did **not** use for fitting whenever possible.

### Interpreting the synthetic dataset

Open `datasets/unit-08.csv` from the offline reader. All station names, orientations, distances and categories in this CSV are fictional. Plot the values and report which trend, if any, is robust against removal of two observations. Where the data do not contain enough directional or temporal context to support a tectonic claim, write that limitation explicitly. Do not claim a physical trend is established just because a graphing tool draws a smooth line.

### Independent review

Exchange interpretations with another student. Ask them to identify one term or symbol whose meaning is unclear, one unstated convention and one observation that could disprove the preferred reconstruction. Revise the final diagram to make these items visible. Grade the work on measurement transparency and falsifiability, not on whether every student selected the same visually attractive fault or fold model.
