# Unit 2 — Stress, strain and mechanical behaviour

## Central question

Separate applied forces, observed changes of shape, and the history connecting them. Use structural observations and reproducible geometric tests to evaluate competing interpretations.

## Extended unit perspective

The distinction between motion and deformation prevents many structural interpretations from becoming circular. Two points can translate together by ten metres without any change in the distance between them, and therefore without strain. A material body can rotate without changing its shape, yet lineation orientations may change dramatically in the map reference frame. A strain ellipse captures final distortion but not the unique series of earlier increments that produced it. Stress is different again: it describes force per area acting on internal planes, and cannot be calculated from strain without assumptions about rheology and loading history. The same amount of shortening may form a narrow fault at one temperature and a distributed fold at another, because rock behaviour depends on pressure, fluid conditions, strain rate, lithology, and previous defects. Classroom Mohr circles simplify the stress tensor but remain useful for checking sign conventions and how normal and shear traction change as an imaginary plane rotates. The simple effective-stress relation reduces a normal stress by pore pressure; real pore systems, anisotropic permeability, rate dependence and thermal evolution complicate application. Lab reports must state whether an equation is kinematic, mechanical, empirical or merely a diagnostic ratio. Never claim a numerical stress inferred from fold wavelength alone without an independently justified constitutive model.

### Field and scientific objectives

Describe relevant structural objects with location and orientation, translate them between map and section, construct a physically and chronologically consistent interpretation, and test at least one alternative. The aim is to communicate exactly which information is observed, which result follows by geometric calculation and which regional mechanism is inferred. Avoid interpreting one structural indicator as proof of a single tectonic event where earlier deformation, reactivation or anisotropic rock properties remain plausible.

## Lesson 2.1 — Displacement fields and kinematic reference frames

### Mechanism and observational scale

A displacement vector records change from an initial to a final position in a defined reference frame; rigid translation produces displacement without strain. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed. 

### Evidence and documentation

Matched marker beds, veins, pebbles and survey points constrain relative movements over different time intervals. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with **reference state, principal axes and whether the formula is kinematic or mechanical** and illustrate **measured marker distortion, model stress and assumed material properties**. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve. 

### Competing interpretation

An offset marker might result from differential erosion, stratigraphic lensing or bedding truncation rather than fault slip. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

### Test and scientific protocol

Choose two independent markers and check that their restored geometry yields consistent displacement vectors. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For **displacement fields and kinematic reference frames**, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error. 

### Worked numerical case 6: Marker displacement in two dimensions

**Invented classroom inputs:** orthogonal marker components 12 and 5 m in fixed reference frame. Evaluate `math.hypot(12,5)` to obtain **13 m**. Write the governing equation separately, substitute the stated values, and check the dimensions and sign convention. For a trigonometric expression identify whether angles are entered in radians or degrees; for percentages identify the reference length or population. 

Repeat the calculation after varying the least certain input. Explain whether the change is important relative to plausible field error. If the formula neglects a deformation component, assumes a planar fault or uses a restricted stress model, identify that assumption rather than interpreting the number as exact. Distinguish geometric separation from true displacement, finite strain from its time rate and stress from material strength whenever those concepts arise in the calculation. 

### Limitation, cross-check and synthesis

Absolute reference positions are usually unknown for ancient structures and deformation may be nonuniform. Consider the alternative that changes the interpretation most while retaining the measured observations. Make a two-column field record. Put verifiable geometric and material facts in the left column and causal or historical interpretation in the right. For each interpreted event, cite the specific measured feature and its location. An interpretation without such a link is a hypothesis to test rather than a supported result. 

Explicitly distinguish a displacement vector from a strain measure. A defensible conclusion therefore has five components: observed feature, ideal relation used, leading interpretation, decisive contrary test and residual uncertainty. Indicate whether each is local to the measured exposure or can legitimately be extended across the mapped domain.

## Lesson 2.4 — Stress tensors and principal directions

### Evidence and documentation

## Lesson 2.5 — Failure, friction and pore-pressure effects

### Mechanism and observational scale

On a preexisting cohesion-bearing surface, a simple Coulomb criterion compares shear traction with cohesion plus friction times effective normal stress. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed. 

### Evidence and documentation

Fault-gouge mineralogy, vein fillings and slip surfaces together constrain material and fluid influence on deformation. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with **reference state, principal axes and whether the formula is kinematic or mechanical** and illustrate **measured marker distortion, model stress and assumed material properties**. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve. 

### Competing interpretation

High pore pressure can reduce effective normal stress, but the same fracture orientation may arise under a different total stress state. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

### Test and scientific protocol

Vary pore pressure and coefficient of friction in a simplified calculation; compare the resulting threshold with observed failure only within the assumptions. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For **failure, friction and pore-pressure effects**, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

Repeat the calculation after varying the least certain input. Explain whether the change is important relative to plausible field error. ### Limitation, cross-check and synthesis

Real faults have rate, temperature, mineralogy, roughness and fluid feedbacks absent from a linear criterion. Consider the alternative that changes the interpretation most while retaining the measured observations. List which surfaces or fabrics predate which others using direct cut, fold, overprint or growth relations. Leave pairs unordered where no observation establishes priority. Reactivation means a single surface may occur more than once in the event graph. 

Treat Coulomb calculations as conceptual constraints, never site-specific failure predictions. A defensible conclusion therefore has five components: observed feature, ideal relation used, leading interpretation, decisive contrary test and residual uncertainty. Indicate whether each is local to the measured exposure or can legitimately be extended across the mapped domain.

## Integrated investigation 2 — One rock, multiple deformations

Imagine four initially circular marker populations in a layered rock. Some record finite ellipses, while the entire block has also undergone rigid-body rotation. Show mathematically why an observed shape change does not determine the time sequence of deformation. Compute both engineering and logarithmic strain for one stretched marker. Build a schematic two-dimensional Mohr circle under specified compression-positive principal stresses. Contrast a friction-only failure model with one in which fluid pressure is nonzero, and articulate the unmeasured rheological parameters.

### Analysis protocol and deliverables

The following integrated investigation uses fictional measurements expressly manufactured to demonstrate methods. It is not a published field survey or a substitute for collecting data. Begin with original measurements and metadata; present interpreted structures in separate layers. Compute at least one simple quantity with its units, make two competing predictions, and decide which additional piece of evidence would be most informative. Report any failure of the data to distinguish models. Submit a map or diagram, annotated calculations, a brief uncertainty table and a paragraph explaining whether the result has any practical limitations. The first panel of the submitted figure should contain observations only. The second may show an inferred geometry, but include a legend differentiating solid observation lines from dashed extrapolations. The third should compare an alternative model and identify the measurement that would select between them. A model should be judged against the data it did **not** use for fitting whenever possible.
