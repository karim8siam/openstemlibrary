# Unit 4 — Brittle faults and fault-zone architecture

## Central question

Relate fault surfaces and markers to slip, damage and reactivation. Use structural observations and reproducible geometric tests to evaluate competing interpretations.

## Extended unit perspective

A fault is not identified solely by a crack. The essential distinction is relative displacement across a surface or distributed zone. In field work this requires matched markers and a specified reference direction. Apparent stratigraphic separation observed in an oblique section can differ greatly from the actual slip vector. A rake measured on a fault plane can resolve a net slip into strike-parallel and dip-parallel components, but polished lineations may record just one late episode of movement. Faults also have thickness and internal anatomy: a gouge-rich core can be bordered by a damage zone with variable fracture intensity, permeability and mechanical properties. Some old fault zones are reactivated under new stress conditions and record contradictory slip indicators unless event timing is considered. Simple cartoons of normal and reverse movement aid terminology, but natural faults commonly curve with depth or branch into multiple strands. Slip estimates must therefore distinguish measured offset, geological separation and the amount restored under a model. The best restoration is one that satisfies multiple independent marker matches and stratigraphic ordering, not merely a visually smooth cross-section. Class exercises here use intentionally simple planar geometry; they are not seismic-hazard maps and cannot be used to estimate contemporary operational fault activity.

### Field and scientific objectives

Describe relevant structural objects with location and orientation, translate them between map and section, construct a physically and chronologically consistent interpretation, and test at least one alternative. The aim is to communicate exactly which information is observed, which result follows by geometric calculation and which regional mechanism is inferred. Avoid interpreting one structural indicator as proof of a single tectonic event where earlier deformation, reactivation or anisotropic rock properties remain plausible.

### Limitation, cross-check and synthesis

Fault dip may vary at depth and growth strata may record several phases of motion. Consider the alternative that changes the interpretation most while retaining the measured observations. ### Analysis protocol and deliverables

The following integrated investigation uses fictional measurements expressly manufactured to demonstrate methods. It is not a published field survey or a substitute for collecting data. Begin with original measurements and metadata; present interpreted structures in separate layers. Compute at least one simple quantity with its units, make two competing predictions, and decide which additional piece of evidence would be most informative. Report any failure of the data to distinguish models. Submit a map or diagram, annotated calculations, a brief uncertainty table and a paragraph explaining whether the result has any practical limitations. The first panel of the submitted figure should contain observations only. The second may show an inferred geometry, but include a legend differentiating solid observation lines from dashed extrapolations. The third should compare an alternative model and identify the measurement that would select between them. A model should be judged against the data it did **not** use for fitting whenever possible.
