# Unit 4 — Bijections and Infinite Sets

**Core insight.** A bijection compares size without choosing a preferred enumeration. Infinite sets exhibit behavior impossible for finite sets.

**Running case.** Two repositories enumerate student submissions differently; ask whether they have equally many identifiers, whether every identifier is processed, and whether missing indices can be repaired.

**Restriction.** For infinite sets, cardinal comparison is not the same as inclusion; a proper subset may have equal cardinality.

## Unit orientation: definition, construction, and evidence

An explicit matching is stronger evidence of equinumerosity than the impression that two collections are “both endless.” A bijection includes a rule and a proof of no collisions and no omissions. Its domain and codomain must be named: shifting N forward changes the range, even though the shifted range has the same cardinality.

Dedekind-infinitude exposes a difference between finite and infinite sets. But the background logic matters: without an adequate choice principle, some converse statements about infinite and Dedekind-infinite sets are not provable in ZF alone. A textbook must not silently import a stronger axiom.

Enumerations are algorithms only when the scheduling reaches every intended element and treats duplicates consistently. The enumeration of positive fractions by diagonal level p+q is complete because each pair has a finite level. Infinite grids are easy to draw misleadingly; provide a finite index bound for each target.

**Three questions before working.** (1) What is the explicit mapping? (2) Can two inputs collide, or can an output be missed? (3) Does the proof accidentally import an axiom of choice?

The unit is organized in a deliberate sequence. Start with a definition; derive consequences by matching the definition to its quantifiers; construct a worked finite or symbolic case; and then search for a boundary case. Mathematics offers different kinds of evidence. A direct proof establishes a result for all objects in the stated domain; a counterexample disproves such a result; an example only illustrates it. The accompanying model visualizations are transparent local computations. They can reveal errors and motivate a proof, but cannot establish set-theoretic independence, consistency, or claims about every possible infinite structure.

### Common vocabulary and a formal audit

In this unit, the word *construction* means a legitimate definition of an object in the chosen system, not just an algorithm that prints a plausible string. The running case is **Two repositories enumerate student submissions differently; ask whether they have equally many identifiers, whether every identifier is processed, and whether missing indices can be repaired.** A complete mathematical account begins by stating the source sets and background theory. Next specify what is asserted to exist, what equality means for these objects, and what properties need proof. Finally separate any domain facts collected from outside the mathematics. The last separation matters because an algorithm can be logically correct yet operate on incomplete or incorrectly classified entries.

A quick error audit starts from the restriction: **For infinite sets, cardinal comparison is not the same as inclusion; a proper subset may have equal cardinality.** Try to invent a minimal counterexample to a stronger-sounding but invalid statement. If the counterexample is finite, list each member or ordered pair explicitly. If the statement concerns infinity or axioms, name the exact principle that would be required. Giving the missing hypothesis is a mathematically useful response: it tells the reader how the claim could be repaired without pretending it was already proved.

### Statement and formal objects

**Definition.** Sets A and B are equinumerous, A≈B, when a bijection f:A→B exists. A bijection is injective (one-to-one) and surjective (onto), not just one of these.
