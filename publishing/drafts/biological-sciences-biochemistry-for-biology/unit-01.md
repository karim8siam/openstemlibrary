# Unit 1: Biomolecules

## Conceptual introduction

Biochemistry begins with a physical question: how can collections of ordinary atoms produce molecules that are simultaneously selective, dynamic and able to support living systems? The answer requires several descriptive levels. The identity of an atom determines electron structure and bond chemistry; covalent connectivity determines what transformations are possible; molecular conformation determines which parts of a molecule can approach a partner; and the surrounding water, salts and membranes alter which conformations and interactions are favorable. An organism does not escape chemistry. It exploits chemistry under conditions in which reaction rates, energy budgets and concentrations are controlled by a nested arrangement of cellular compartments and enzymes.

Begin any molecular analysis by specifying a chemical species rather than a casual common name. “Glucose” can refer to multiple ring and open-chain forms in equilibrium. “Amino acid” might denote a zwitterion, a protonated species or a residue within a peptide. “Phosphate” may include several acid–base forms. These distinctions matter because reaction charge, hydrogen bonding, solvent interaction and spectroscopic response depend on protonation state. Biochemical notations often suppress hydrogen ions for readability, but a balanced reaction and a thermodynamic calculation must adopt a consistent convention. The classroom goal is not to memorize every microstate: it is to recognize when a simplified species label conceals a variable that could influence an experimental result.

Three questions can organize the study of a biomolecule. First, what covalent groups are present and how are they joined? Second, how will those groups interact with water and with one another? Third, which enzymes or physical processes can change the molecule on a biologically relevant timescale? These questions connect carbohydrate linkage stereochemistry, lipid partitioning, protein side-chain chemistry and nucleic-acid base pairing. For example, two carbohydrates with the same elemental formula can have different properties because their linkages give enzymes different recognition targets. A membrane is not simply a wall made of fat; its composition determines a distribution of conformations and interactions that controls transport and signaling. A DNA duplex is not a permanently fixed ladder; base pairs form, separate and respond to solution conditions.

Concentration units must be carried through calculations. A concentration written as 100 micromolar is 100 times 10 to the power minus six moles per liter, not 100 moles per liter. This distinction sounds elementary yet produces large errors in affinity and absorbance calculations. Molarity also differs from molecular count: the count depends on volume and Avogadro’s constant. Mass concentration depends on molecular mass. If a researcher compares enzyme activities from two samples, the reported normalization must identify whether it is per unit volume, per milligram of total protein, per living cell or per mole of active site. Any unreported conversion hides an assumption about the specimen.

In this unit the first investigations move from molecular structure to controlled measurements. For carbohydrates, an enzymatic cleavage assay probes both chemical linkage and physical accessibility. For lipids, a partition measurement tests distribution between defined phases but is not a cell transport assay. For proteins, a pH titration can reveal charge-dependent behavior while leaving protein-specific protonation effects unresolved. For nucleic acids, a melting experiment can compare duplex stability but cannot reconstruct sequence directly. In every case, a good experimental explanation separates the manipulated factor, the recorded observable, the inferred molecular state and at least one competing explanation.

An important unifying principle is that descriptive categories are convenient approximations. Carbohydrates can be attached to proteins and lipids; proteins can bind DNA; nucleic acids can assume catalytic folds; lipids can participate in signaling as well as membrane construction. When one molecule belongs to several functional categories, chemistry becomes clearer by tracking actual reactions and interactions than by forcing it into an exclusive textbook box. This prepares the transition to protein chemistry, where the same covalent sequence can produce multiple conformations and binding behaviors in different conditions.


## 1.1 Carbohydrates

### Chemical mechanism and material constraints

Monosaccharides contain carbonyl and hydroxyl groups, and their open-chain structures interconvert with cyclic hemiacetals. Glucose often forms a six-membered pyranose ring, whereas ribose also occupies five-membered forms. Alpha and beta anomers differ at the anomeric carbon and can interconvert in solution. A glycosidic linkage converts separate monomers into disaccharides or polymer chains and changes what an enzyme can recognize.

Compare glycogen and cellulose without assuming that both are just interchangeable glucose reservoirs. Glycogen consists largely of alpha-linked chains, with alpha-1,6 branches that produce many nonreducing ends. Cellulose uses beta-1,4 linkages and forms extensive hydrogen-bonded fibers. The different stereochemistry changes higher-order packing and the identity of enzymes required for degradation.

### Quantitative and experimental reasoning

To compare enzyme susceptibility, equalize the amount of carbohydrate substrate, buffer, temperature, and incubation time. Measure released reducing sugars with a calibrated assay and include substrate-only, enzyme-only, heat-inactivated enzyme, and blank measurements. Report rates per substrate mass or mole rather than comparing raw absorbance from samples of different concentration.

For an idealized polymer of n hexose residues produced by condensation, the molecular mass is approximately n times 162.14 g/mol plus 18.02 g/mol for terminal water. This differs from n times the 180.16 g/mol mass of free glucose because each joining step eliminates water. Branch points alter connectivity but not that simple residue accounting.

### Limits of inference and neighboring pathways

A color change after adding a sugar reagent does not automatically identify a polymer or prove that a certain bond was hydrolyzed. Multiple saccharides may be reducing, and matrix components can interfere with the readout. Structural claims require additional evidence such as linkage-specific enzymes, chromatography, mass spectrometry, or nuclear magnetic resonance.

Follow a carbon atom from dietary polysaccharide to intracellular glucose-6-phosphate. The polymer must first be made accessible, hydrolyzed by appropriate enzymes, transported in a biologically available form, and phosphorylated or otherwise metabolized. At each stage a different mechanism can limit flux. An apparent deficiency in carbohydrate use need not reflect lack of carbohydrate in the original sample.

### Applied laboratory scenario

A food-science group compares two starch preparations that contain the same total glucose equivalents but differ in branching and granule treatment. One preparation yields twice as much reducing sugar after 10 minutes of enzyme exposure. The observation admits at least three explanations: more accessible chain ends, a more favorable physical granule surface or a contaminating enzyme activity. Before attributing the difference to a specific glycosidic bond, the students repeat the assay after complete chemical dispersion of both preparations, standardize total glucosyl residues, test a heat-inactivated preparation and inspect product distributions by chromatography. A remaining difference after dispersion suggests a chemical rather than purely physical limitation, yet the final conclusion must refer to the tested enzyme, not to every digestive environment.

### Counterfactual interpretation and discrimination

Suppose an amylase preparation cleaves an accessible starch sample rapidly but scarcely affects cellulose, even when the polymers have matched total glucose content. The relevant variable is bond stereochemistry and accessible chain geometry, not the elemental formula. If the enzyme is specific for alpha linkages, equal molar concentrations of glycosyl residues do not imply equal productive substrate concentrations. Design a second experiment using an enzyme with established beta-linkage activity. A substrate-control series should reveal whether the apparent specificity follows the active site or arises from physical occlusion. Only interpret the comparison after separately measuring polymer dissolution and actual reducing-end production.


## 1.2 Lipids

### Chemical mechanism and material constraints

A lipid category is defined in part by solubility behavior rather than by one shared covalent backbone. Triacylglycerols store reduced carbon in ester-linked fatty acyl chains; phospholipids contribute amphiphilic membrane structure; sterols influence packing and serve as biosynthetic precursors. A phospholipid with two hydrophobic tails and a polar head can assemble into bilayers in aqueous environments because the aggregate reduces unfavorable water–hydrophobe exposure.

The chain length and degree of unsaturation alter melting behavior. A cis double bond creates a kink that tends to disrupt regular acyl-chain packing. Longer saturated chains generally increase van der Waals contacts, but headgroup, salt concentration, sterol content and temperature all alter the final membrane response. Consequently, one melting temperature cannot represent all biological membranes.

### Quantitative and experimental reasoning

Prepare vesicles with matched total lipid concentration and systematically change the unsaturated fraction. Compare permeability to a defined fluorescent probe over a temperature series, while monitoring vesicle size and dye leakage from handling. Include a probe-only blank and a detergent-lysed maximum-signal control. Convert fluorescence to a physical permeability proxy only after a calibration or kinetic model has been justified.

A partition coefficient P is conventionally the concentration ratio between two specified phases at equilibrium, not a universal measure of absorption through a living cell. Taking log10(P) compresses many orders of magnitude, but ionization and specific transporters can reverse predictions made from a neutral-solute partition model.

### Limits of inference and neighboring pathways

A dye escaping a vesicle can indicate membrane leakage, but it can also reflect vesicle rupture, photobleaching correction error, or a size distribution change. Electron microscopy, dynamic light scattering, or independent encapsulation markers may distinguish these alternatives. Effective membrane permeability is not numerically equivalent to membrane fluidity even though both can depend on lipid composition.

Connect lipid chemistry to respiratory metabolism by asking where reduced electrons reside. Carbon atoms in a fatty acyl chain are generally more reduced than those in carbohydrate of comparable carbon number. Oxidation can therefore yield substantial reducing equivalents, but the amount of ATP actually made depends on transport, pathway stoichiometry and the coupling efficiency of oxidative phosphorylation.

### Applied laboratory scenario

A fluorescence assay reports that a membrane made from an unsaturated lipid retains less encapsulated dye after warming than a saturated-lipid membrane. If samples differ in vesicle radius, the surface-area-to-volume ratio changes independently of membrane chemistry. A useful follow-up therefore measures vesicle size distributions before and after heating, standardizes dye loading, measures spontaneous leakage at baseline and tests a detergent-induced full-release endpoint. When unsaturation and permeability correlate under these constraints, the result supports a composition-dependent effect but still does not measure a universal “membrane quality.” The hypothesis can be refined with a second probe of different molecular size, because pathways for water, ions and large fluorescent molecules need not be identical.

### Counterfactual interpretation and discrimination

A solute with a high oil/water partition coefficient accumulates in a plastic assay tube but crosses a living cell membrane poorly. The observations are not contradictory because partition into a neutral organic phase says little about aqueous boundary layers, transporter recognition or intracellular binding. To discriminate surface adsorption from true membrane transport, compare mass recovered from tube walls, medium and the intracellular fraction over time. A wash-resistant signal is still not definitive evidence of cytosolic entry; fluorescent labels may separate from the solute. A mechanistic claim about permeability needs an appropriate mass balance and a method that distinguishes membrane-associated material from transported material.


## 1.3 Proteins as chemical polymers

### Chemical mechanism and material constraints

An amino acid contains a central alpha carbon connected to an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen and a side chain, with glycine as the achiral exception. Peptide bonds are amide linkages that exhibit partial double-bond character. Rotations around neighboring backbone bonds give proteins conformational freedom but do not make every conformation equally favorable.

Side-chain chemistry affects both solubility and intramolecular stabilization. Charged groups form favorable electrostatic interactions in some contexts; nonpolar groups can be buried away from water; cysteine thiols can form disulfides under suitable oxidative conditions. Histidine can exchange protons near physiological pH in selected local environments, which is one reason histidines appear in many catalytic sites.

### Quantitative and experimental reasoning

For a titratable acidic side chain, the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation estimates the average protonated fraction given a suitable apparent pKa. It does not tell whether that same residue is buried, hydrogen-bonded, or engaged in an active site. A local protein environment can shift pKa enough that textbook free-amino-acid values become poor predictors.

Peptide mass calculations subtract approximately 18.015 g/mol for each formed bond when starting from masses of free amino acids. In ordinary sequence analysis, residue masses already account for condensation; adding terminal water once recovers the mass of a neutral unmodified peptide. Modifications, signal peptide processing and covalent cofactors break the simplest estimate.

### Limits of inference and neighboring pathways

To assess charge-dependent solubility, titrate a purified protein across a defined pH interval at matched ionic strength. Measure soluble concentration after a controlled centrifugation step and separately test catalytic activity or structural integrity. A low soluble fraction is not the same as irreversible denaturation, and a protein near its isoelectric point need not precipitate under every buffer condition.

An observed protein band can represent several molecular forms with similar mobility. A student should trace from translation to processing, localization, folding, binding and degradation before attributing band intensity solely to transcription. Parallel measurements of RNA, protein abundance and function provide more diagnostic information than any one of them alone.

### Applied laboratory scenario

A soluble enzyme loses activity after replacing one histidine with alanine. The tempting claim is that histidine acted as the catalytic base. But the substitution can also change the local hydrogen-bond network or protein stability. The team measures active enzyme concentration, melting behavior, ligand affinity and catalytic rate separately. If binding and fold remain intact while turnover falls sharply, a catalytic role becomes more plausible. A conservative residue replacement and a pH-rate profile give additional tests. Histidine’s protonation is environmentally sensitive, so the actual protein pKa should not be assumed equal to the free side-chain value. This case illustrates why functional conclusions must be anchored to independent measurements of structural integrity.

### Counterfactual interpretation and discrimination

An ionic-strength series shows increasing solubility of a protein at low salt and decreasing solubility at high salt. An explanatory model should not call both outcomes simply “charge screening.” Moderate salt can shield attractive intermolecular charges and improve dispersal, while highly concentrated salt competes for hydration and changes solvent activity. A defensible investigation uses buffer matched in pH and checks whether the protein maintains its fold. The sign of the solubility change alone is insufficient to identify an ion-binding site. Comparing different salts at matched ionic strength can test whether a response follows general electrostatics or chemically specific effects.


## 1.4 Nucleic acids

### Chemical mechanism and material constraints

Nucleotides combine a nitrogenous base, pentose sugar and one or more phosphate groups. Nucleic acid phosphodiester bonds join a sugar 3-prime hydroxyl to a neighboring 5-prime phosphate, creating chemical directionality. Complementary base pairing constrains the geometry of double-helical nucleic acid segments without implying that every DNA region has the same sequence-dependent stability.

DNA and RNA differ at the 2-prime position of the sugar, and uracil commonly replaces thymine in RNA. RNA can form extensive intramolecular base-paired structures and catalytically active folds; it is not simply an inert temporary copy of DNA. Hydrolytic susceptibility and the activity of nucleases mean sample collection and storage can strongly alter measured RNA abundance.

### Quantitative and experimental reasoning

Use the nearest-neighbor approximation or a more complete thermodynamic model when estimating duplex stability for experiments. The simple GC-percentage rule is only a coarse heuristic: salt concentration, oligonucleotide length, mismatches, sequence order and strand concentration all affect observed melting behavior.

A double-stranded DNA sample containing N base pairs has roughly 2N nucleotides and about N minus one phosphodiester linkages per strand for a linear molecule. That bookkeeping depends on whether termini carry phosphates or other modifications. Do not interchange base-pair concentration, nucleotide concentration and molecular concentration in calculations.

### Limits of inference and neighboring pathways

When comparing synthetic oligonucleotides, first verify sequence identity and input concentration. A fluorescent melt curve provides a transition signal, but a single apparent midpoint does not distinguish mismatches, secondary structures and concentration-dependent bimolecular transitions. Include a sequence-matched reference and a deliberately mismatched control.

Biochemical evidence of binding to a DNA sequence is not evidence of regulated transcription by itself. The relevant protein must encounter that sequence in the cellular environment, bind with adequate lifetime and interact with regulatory machinery. Nucleosome occupancy, cofactors, local chromatin state and competition can all change the functional consequence.

### Applied laboratory scenario

Two 20-nucleotide DNA probes differ by a single central mismatch. They bind the same nominal target amount, yet one produces a broader melt transition. The team tests whether ionic strength and sample concentration were matched, because both affect duplex stability. They verify the target sequence with an independent assay and examine whether either probe can fold into a hairpin. A shifted melt midpoint is consistent with altered pairing free energy but cannot alone identify the sequence position of the mismatch. The laboratory reports the chosen temperature ramp, optical wavelength and heating–cooling reversibility. The result becomes more informative when complemented by a sequence-specific enzymatic or chromatographic comparison.

### Counterfactual interpretation and discrimination

A DNA binding experiment uses two oligonucleotides with identical base composition but different order. Both have the same GC fraction, yet their melt transitions differ. That observation reveals the limitation of composition-only heuristics: stacking interactions and nearest-neighbor order can matter. Check that oligomer concentration and salt composition are identical and that neither probe forms a competing hairpin. A concentration series helps reveal whether the melting transition reflects bimolecular duplex dissociation. If a sample contains multiple strands, a single transition may average different species. The biochemical analysis should report sequence, pairing orientation, solution conditions and the specific definition used for the transition midpoint.


## Quantitative model studio

### Model 1: Ionization of a weak acid

The weak-acid model begins with the equilibrium HA ⇌ H+ + A− and defines Ka=[H+][A−]/[HA] for idealized activities. Taking negative base-10 logarithms gives pH=pKa+log10([A−]/[HA]). Solving for [HA] divided by total acid yields the plotted protonated fraction. The curve passes exactly through one-half when pH equals pKa; two units above pKa approximately one part in 101 remains protonated. Use the control to compare a solution at pH 3.8, 4.8 and 5.8. Predict the change before reading the calculated label. Then explain why an acidic residue inside a protein need not follow this curve with the pKa of the free amino acid. Electrostatic interactions, solvent exclusion and other titratable groups may change the apparent equilibrium. A titration experiment requires a measurement that distinguishes protonated from deprotonated populations, rather than equating a pH electrode reading with a fraction of one selected molecular species. This mathematical model is valuable precisely because its assumptions are visible: one site, equilibrium, fixed pKa and no explicit activity corrections.

Investigation challenge: start with pH below pKa, where HA dominates, and then move two pH units above the same pKa. Ask which chemical population becomes more abundant and whether the total number of acid molecules must change. It need not: protonation can redistribute a fixed molecular pool. The plotted number describes an equilibrium fraction, whereas a titration curve may include buffer capacity, dilution and changes in total volume. Design a control where ionic strength is held fixed while pH changes. If the measured curve shifts relative to the model, explain whether apparent pKa, activity corrections or an unrecognized additional ionizable group could account for the discrepancy. Report changes as fractions and percentages carefully; a fraction of 0.01 is one percent, not 0.01 percent.

**Underlying relation:** f(HA)=1/(1+10^(pH−pKa)); pKa=4.8. Acid/base equilibrium; neglect activity corrections.

### Model 2: Neutral-solute partition

The partition model assumes a neutral molecule distributes into equal-volume oil and water phases at equilibrium. Define P=Coil/Cwater. Conservation says ntotal=Coil V+Cwater V when both volumes equal V. Substituting Coil=P Cwater gives the oil-phase fraction P/(1+P). The slider uses log10 P, because many compounds span several orders of magnitude in phase preference. At log P=0, P=1 and half the molecules reside in either phase; at log P=2, the modeled fraction in oil is 100/101. Different conclusions follow if the phase volumes are unequal, and an ionizable solute may have separate charge states with different partition behavior. Record three hypothetical points and then ask which experimental observation would test equilibrium: a constant concentration ratio measured after mixing for several times, or one instantaneous reading immediately after adding solute? Only the former supports equilibrium under the stated sampling protocol. The classroom calculation should never be used as a direct formula for drug absorption or passive cell entry, which involve membranes, transporters and multiple environments.

Investigation challenge: prepare two samples with identical partition coefficients but different oil-to-water volume ratios. Predict whether the fractions of molecules in oil remain equal. They do not, because amounts equal concentration times volume. Derive the general oil fraction as PV_oil/(PV_oil+V_water), then recover the plotted expression when volumes match. Now ask what happens if a solute has charged and uncharged forms with different phase affinities. A distribution coefficient measured at a given pH may no longer equal the partition coefficient of its neutral form. The lesson is to distinguish an intensive property describing phase concentrations from an extensive inventory of molecules. The displayed curve can serve as a calibration case for testing the more general formula.

**Underlying relation:** f_oil=P/(1+P) for equal phase volumes and neutral solute; P=10^x.


## Integrative fieldwork

Integrated investigation: compose a biomolecular sample from an unknown blend of soluble carbohydrate, neutral lipid, small peptide and short nucleic acid. The objective is not to identify every constituent by one color change, but to design an ordered separation and verification plan. First determine the aqueous solubility and select sample handling that preserves unstable species. Then remove large aggregates by a documented procedure and record how much sample is lost. Allocate independent aliquots to enzyme-specific carbohydrate assays, lipid extraction, protein-targeted detection and nucleic-acid absorbance or sequencing. Every test must have an appropriate blank; a reagent blank does not replace a negative biological control when cross-reactivity is possible. If a lipid-extraction solvent removes the peptide too, the analyst needs a recovery standard to quantify that loss. The final inference table has separate columns for direct observations, chemical identities supported by orthogonal tests, and alternative explanations not eliminated. The experiment is deliberately designed to expose the distinction between detecting a type of bond and assigning a particular macromolecular species. A detected phosphate signal, for example, could originate from a nucleic acid, a phospholipid or a small phosphorylated metabolite. The quantitative ledger should report initial sample mass, fractions recovered, measurement uncertainty and any species that remain chemically unresolved. A student has succeeded if the limitations are mapped honestly, not if the report names every band or peak with unwarranted certainty.


## Worked questions and solutions

### Question 1: Weak acid distribution

A model weak acid has pKa 4.8. At pH 6.8, what percentage remains as protonated HA?

1. Write [A−]/[HA]=10^(pH−pKa)=10^2=100.

2. The protonated fraction is [HA]/([HA]+[A−])=1/(1+100)=0.00990099.

3. Multiply by 100: about 0.9901% HA. This applies to a single freely equilibrating acid form, not a buried protein residue.

### Question 2: Counting peptide condensation

Estimate the molecular mass of an unmodified 12-residue peptide whose free amino acids have total molar mass 1,660 g/mol.

1. An ordinary linear 12-residue peptide has 11 peptide bonds.

2. Formation of each bond formally eliminates one water molecule, approximately 18.015 g/mol.

3. M≈1660−11(18.015)=1461.835 g/mol. A terminus modification would change this calculation.

### Question 3: Partition fraction

A neutral solute has an oil/water partition coefficient P=20 between equal oil and water volumes. What fraction is in oil at equilibrium?

1. Define P=Coil/Cwater=20. At equal volumes, the ratio of moles is also 20:1.

2. The oil fraction is 20/(20+1)=20/21.

3. Result: 0.95238, or 95.24%. The equality of phase volumes and absence of binding are crucial.
