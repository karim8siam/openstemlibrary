# Unit 05: Three-Dimensional Solids and Material Modeling

**Course Code**: FEA 405 · **Level**: Advanced Undergraduate Engineering

## Unit Overview & Physical Foundations

This comprehensive university textbook unit covers the theoretical framework, physical modeling, mathematical governing equations, and engineering applications of **Three-Dimensional Solids and Material Modeling**.

# Unit 05 · Lesson 01 — Three-dimensional solid elements

**Target competency.** Derive the appropriate approximate relation, apply it with coherent units, and identify one situation in which it would produce a misleading engineering result. **Primary relation:** `K_e=∫ Bᵀ D B dV`. **Field study:** Model a ribbed housing under a mounting force.

## 1. Why this particular idea matters

Tetrahedral and hexahedral elements approximate three-dimensional displacement fields; interpolation order matters for bending-dominated structures. The objective of this lesson is narrower than simply learning which button to press in a solver. An engineering model represents a physical claim about the way loads, boundary values and material response are connected. That claim should be written in words and symbols before a mesh is built. If there is a mismatch between the physical fixture and the idealization, a numerically converged answer can still be wrong. In this lesson the governing distinction to test is whether **a coarse first-order tetrahedral mesh can be excessively stiff in bending.** A useful model is one whose errors and limitations are visible, not merely one with more degrees of freedom.

For the laboratory or design situation, **model a ribbed housing under a mounting force.** Sketch the geometry and identify the physical response to be compared with evidence. Distinguish measured dimensions from nominal drawing dimensions, applied force from equivalent nodal force, support stiffness from an imposed zero displacement, and material property from an artificially tuned fitting coefficient. If symmetry is employed, list the components that must vanish on the symmetry plane. Explain how the original full-structure load becomes a load in the reduced domain and why the resulting support reactions should still satisfy equilibrium.

## 2. Governing statement and finite element interpretation

Start with `K_e=∫ Bᵀ D B dV`. Identify the variables by their dimensions; then ask whether the expression is a conservation statement, constitutive relation, geometric identity, interpolation law, eigenproblem or diagnostic measure. This classification matters. A force-displacement conjugate pair contributes mechanical work; stress times strain contributes strain-energy density; conductivity times temperature gradient produces a heat flux. These quantities cannot be casually interchanged merely because a solver exports them in one table.

Use a finite-dimensional displacement or temperature approximation only after establishing the original boundary-value problem. For a static elastic structure, assembly leads to `K u = F` once constraints are applied. A correct element matrix is generally symmetric for a conservative elastic model; an unconstrained structure may have rigid-body modes. Inspect the sign and magnitude of reaction forces after solving. For thermal conduction, the analogous conductivity matrix maps nodal temperatures to flux-related actions, while specified temperature and specified heat-flow boundaries play different roles. State which of these principles underlies the lesson; if the model is nonlinear, describe the tangent and the state/history variables that invalidate a one-time linear solve.

## 3. Worked quantitative benchmark (analytical demonstration)

A small hand-checkable model associated with this unit uses **thermal free expansion**, based on `delta=alpha*DeltaT*L`. Illustrative inputs are **Thermal coefficient=23 µstrain/K, Temperature rise=65 K, Initial length=1.2 m**. Converting the displayed units into SI inside the calculation gives **1.794 mm**. This number comes from the stated elementary relation and is *not* a full FE simulation or a measured response. Recreate the substitution independently, confirm the dimensional units, and explain the physical meaning of the output sign and magnitude. The associated browser model provides bounded parameter changes and a sensitivity plot. For negative outcomes such as compressive stress or reverse heat flow, retain the sign in the balance even if a subsequent design comparison uses absolute magnitude.

Compute a second scenario by changing the dominant parameter by ten percent while holding all others fixed. If the governing relation is nonlinear in that parameter, do not assume the output changes by ten percent. For example, beam deflection scales with length cubed under Euler–Bernoulli assumptions; this makes a modest length error important. An area or thickness variation can also change local stress and global stiffness differently. When the relevant approximation instead assumes constant geometry, explain why replacing the original problem by a new geometry requires re-analysis rather than simply scaling the final result.

## 4. Model construction and verification protocol

A reproducible computational experiment should separate five steps: geometry and idealization, governing and constitutive laws, restraints and loads, numerical discretization and solution, and postprocessing. In the first step, label what is omitted from the geometry. In the second, identify assumptions about elasticity, density, temperature and deformation. In the third, check that constraints do not prevent a real permitted motion or leave a numerical rigid-body mechanism. In the fourth, preserve at least one coarser and one finer mesh with a common output definition. In the fifth, check forces, energy, units and the physical limits of the computed result before examining color contours.

Compare hand-calculated beam bending and thermal expansion with a solid model in low-gradient regions; document local regions that violate those approximations. Define a scalar output and one independent benchmark. If the result differs from the benchmark, investigate modeling differences before reducing solver tolerance. A poor element aspect ratio, wrong global axis, inconsistent force distribution and incorrect thickness can all dominate algebraic solution error. For a case with a stress concentration, sample a path away from a singular vertex and compare a physically meaningful averaged response. Record the intended use of the model: preliminary sizing, teaching sensitivity, a design comparison or a decision requiring professionally certified evidence. The stronger the intended use, the more demanding the independent checks.

## 5. Interpretation, uncertainty and failure analysis

The central failure mode is **A coarse first-order tetrahedral mesh can be excessively stiff in bending.** A credible conclusion therefore reports an operating envelope rather than relying on one favorable parameter set. Identify which input is truly measured and which is only assumed. A mesh-refinement plot reveals one class of numerical uncertainty; it does not calibrate a thermal contact resistance, friction coefficient or material yield strength. When possible, estimate how a measured uncertainty propagates into the output by a derivative, finite difference or independent test. Use at least one pessimistic but physically plausible scenario. Express comparisons in quantities relevant to the decision: actual deflection limits, reaction forces, temperature margins, stability or validated fatigue criteria, not a visually appealing blue-green stress plot.

In a design review, explain whether the model is conservative and for which mode of failure. Increasing a generic safety factor cannot rescue an incorrectly defined boundary condition or a missing load case. Identify the instrument that would measure the claimed output, the calibration requirement, and how raw samples would be preserved. If live testing is hazardous, propose a safe scaled demonstrator or a literature benchmark instead of attempting an unsafe experiment. This lesson produces an analytical teaching case, not a release-to-manufacture approval or a warranty of real-world safety.

4. **Diagnosis.** Describe a test result that disagrees with the simulation. Give one plausible physical cause, one discretization problem and one instrumentation issue. Propose a measurement or additional calculation that distinguishes them.
5. **Boundary challenge.** Change one support, load distribution or material assumption. Predict qualitatively whether the response increases or decreases before calculating. Document whether the altered model still represents the same system.
6. **Reporting.** Draft a 250-word technical note for an independent reviewer covering assumptions, result, units, uncertainty, unmodeled risks and the evidence required before acting.

# Unit 05 · Lesson 05 — Material nonlinearity and plastic zones

**Target competency.** Derive the appropriate approximate relation, apply it with coherent units, and identify one situation in which it would produce a misleading engineering result. **Primary relation:** `f(σ,κ)≤0`. **Field study:** Discuss a notched bracket pushed slightly beyond first yield.

## 1. Why this particular idea matters

Yield criteria, hardening laws and loading history govern plastic deformation. A linear elastic calculation cannot predict residual strain after unloading. The objective of this lesson is narrower than simply learning which button to press in a solver. An engineering model represents a physical claim about the way loads, boundary values and material response are connected. That claim should be written in words and symbols before a mesh is built. If there is a mismatch between the physical fixture and the idealization, a numerically converged answer can still be wrong. In this lesson the governing distinction to test is whether **clipping von Mises stress at yield without satisfying plastic equilibrium.** A useful model is one whose errors and limitations are visible, not merely one with more degrees of freedom.

For the laboratory or design situation, **discuss a notched bracket pushed slightly beyond first yield.** Sketch the geometry and identify the physical response to be compared with evidence. Distinguish measured dimensions from nominal drawing dimensions, applied force from equivalent nodal force, support stiffness from an imposed zero displacement, and material property from an artificially tuned fitting coefficient. If symmetry is employed, list the components that must vanish on the symmetry plane. Explain how the original full-structure load becomes a load in the reduced domain and why the resulting support reactions should still satisfy equilibrium.

## 2. Governing statement and finite element interpretation

Start with `f(σ,κ)≤0`. Identify the variables by their dimensions; then ask whether the expression is a conservation statement, constitutive relation, geometric identity, interpolation law, eigenproblem or diagnostic measure. This classification matters. A force-displacement conjugate pair contributes mechanical work; stress times strain contributes strain-energy density; conductivity times temperature gradient produces a heat flux. These quantities cannot be casually interchanged merely because a solver exports them in one table.

Use a finite-dimensional displacement or temperature approximation only after establishing the original boundary-value problem. For a static elastic structure, assembly leads to `K u = F` once constraints are applied. A correct element matrix is generally symmetric for a conservative elastic model; an unconstrained structure may have rigid-body modes. Inspect the sign and magnitude of reaction forces after solving. For thermal conduction, the analogous conductivity matrix maps nodal temperatures to flux-related actions, while specified temperature and specified heat-flow boundaries play different roles. State which of these principles underlies the lesson; if the model is nonlinear, describe the tangent and the state/history variables that invalidate a one-time linear solve.

## 3. Worked quantitative benchmark (analytical demonstration)

A small hand-checkable model associated with this unit uses **bending outer-fiber stress**, based on `sigma=M*c/I`. Compute a second scenario by changing the dominant parameter by ten percent while holding all others fixed. If the governing relation is nonlinear in that parameter, do not assume the output changes by ten percent. For example, beam deflection scales with length cubed under Euler–Bernoulli assumptions; this makes a modest length error important. An area or thickness variation can also change local stress and global stiffness differently. When the relevant approximation instead assumes constant geometry, explain why replacing the original problem by a new geometry requires re-analysis rather than simply scaling the final result.

## 4. Model construction and verification protocol

A reproducible computational experiment should separate five steps: geometry and idealization, governing and constitutive laws, restraints and loads, numerical discretization and solution, and postprocessing. In the first step, label what is omitted from the geometry. In the second, identify assumptions about elasticity, density, temperature and deformation. In the third, check that constraints do not prevent a real permitted motion or leave a numerical rigid-body mechanism. In the fourth, preserve at least one coarser and one finer mesh with a common output definition. In the fifth, check forces, energy, units and the physical limits of the computed result before examining color contours.

Compare hand-calculated beam bending and thermal expansion with a solid model in low-gradient regions; document local regions that violate those approximations. Define a scalar output and one independent benchmark. If the result differs from the benchmark, investigate modeling differences before reducing solver tolerance. A poor element aspect ratio, wrong global axis, inconsistent force distribution and incorrect thickness can all dominate algebraic solution error. For a case with a stress concentration, sample a path away from a singular vertex and compare a physically meaningful averaged response. Record the intended use of the model: preliminary sizing, teaching sensitivity, a design comparison or a decision requiring professionally certified evidence. The stronger the intended use, the more demanding the independent checks.

## 5. Interpretation, uncertainty and failure analysis

The central failure mode is **Clipping von Mises stress at yield without satisfying plastic equilibrium.** A credible conclusion therefore reports an operating envelope rather than relying on one favorable parameter set. Identify which input is truly measured and which is only assumed. A mesh-refinement plot reveals one class of numerical uncertainty; it does not calibrate a thermal contact resistance, friction coefficient or material yield strength. When possible, estimate how a measured uncertainty propagates into the output by a derivative, finite difference or independent test. Use at least one pessimistic but physically plausible scenario. Express comparisons in quantities relevant to the decision: actual deflection limits, reaction forces, temperature margins, stability or validated fatigue criteria, not a visually appealing blue-green stress plot.

In a design review, explain whether the model is conservative and for which mode of failure. Increasing a generic safety factor cannot rescue an incorrectly defined boundary condition or a missing load case. Identify the instrument that would measure the claimed output, the calibration requirement, and how raw samples would be preserved. If live testing is hazardous, propose a safe scaled demonstrator or a literature benchmark instead of attempting an unsafe experiment. This lesson produces an analytical teaching case, not a release-to-manufacture approval or a warranty of real-world safety.
