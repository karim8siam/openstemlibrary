# Unit 1: Materials, services and circular system boundaries

**Guiding question:** What does a sustainable material actually deliver, and over what useful lifetime?

## The system before the material label

A university specifies a new fleet of classroom furniture. Procurement can minimize kilograms, initial carbon, purchase price, maintenance downtime, or replaced chairs, but these objectives do not rank products identically. A product comparison only becomes meaningful after its required service, physical boundaries, time and mandatory safety specifications are written down. The system may contain long-lived inventories, poorly measured return streams, process losses, upstream impacts and financial constraints. The engineering objective is to find the particular intervention that actually improves useful output per resource and reduces meaningful harms. The unit combines an explicit material mechanism with a data and decision workflow, rather than presenting recycling, repair, substitution or efficiency as universally superior.

An evidence-ready project in this unit starts with **service reliability and traceable stock ledger**. Its indispensable records include **count useful delivered services, batch identity, retained stock, exits, returns and actual product failures**, and important counterevidence comes from **customer cohort tracking paired with physical mass records**. Before reading any calculated result, consider **whether added efficiency increases use volume enough to increase total production**. If the answer can be changed by a reasonable assumption about material quality or service life, the proper result is a bounded comparison and a plan to collect missing evidence, not a confident environmental winner.

## Unit project: challenge an appealing sustainability claim

Write an initial one-page decision specification for the case above. State the material in/out boundaries, the functional unit and a physically meaningful reference flow. Identify at least one mass-balance constraint and two competing mechanisms that might explain an observed difference. Then list the measurement owner, instrument or transaction log, geographic and temporal applicability, uncertainty and a specific control. Revisit this specification after each lesson. The five topics form parts of one investigation, not five disconnected sustainable-material slogans.

## Lesson 1.1 — Service-based functional units

### 1. Physics, chemistry and the function actually supplied

The same material quantity cannot be compared fairly when items provide different service durations or performance. A functional unit fixes a quantified function, required quality and time horizon. For example, one thousand safe seating hours and one chair are different denominators if chairs have different repairability and occupancy. The reference flow states how many products, replacements, maintenance events and transport movements are required to satisfy the unit. Standards-based life-cycle comparison must hold the function consistent before modeling emissions or waste.

**Working relationship:** Impact per service = cradle-to-grave impact / verified delivered service units; the numerator and denominator must refer to the same reference flow. Keep the system boundary, physical units and quality specification visible before asserting an avoided impact.

### 2. A real engineering dilemma with rival explanations

**Problem statement:** A supplier claims a lighter chair is automatically greener, although its seat fabric wears out earlier. Compare one chair purchased with 100,000 occupied seat-hours delivered at equal safety and comfort. Propose two rival mechanisms and one observation that differentiates them. Record timing, composition and functional performance before ranking either explanation.

**Constraint that can reverse the answer:** Occupancy, premature failure, repair, and changing requirements make a declared lifetime different from achieved service. Estimate which service, quality or environmental condition could reverse the conclusion.

### 3. Transparent synthetic calculation and sensitivity

The paired interactive card for this lesson is **Burden per achieved service**. Its computational expression is `150/n+3`, with adjustable **Achieved uses per product** in uses. At 11 uses the deterministic output is **16.636364 kg CO₂e/use**; at 31 uses it is **7.8387097 kg CO₂e/use**. The two-point sensitivity is -0.4398827 kg CO₂e/use per uses. These are synthetic teaching calculations, not measurements. Independently verify units and a third input before interpretation.

**Why the numerical output has limits:** Synthetic manufacture 150 kg CO₂e/product, fixed use burden 3 kg CO₂e/use, no returns or failure effects. For a real decision, independently measure the variables that this illustrative formula holds constant.

### 4. Evidence protocol, sampling and falsification

**Specific investigation:** Obtain failure curves and repair histories from multiple settings; independently assess seat strength and user acceptance. Define independent samples, record rejected outputs and capture instrument calibration and field-relevant service conditions.

 Verify both mass-balance closure and functionally qualified output; publish observations that contradict the preferred mechanism.

### 5. Design trade-off and conditions of adoption

**Trade-off:** A heavier maintainable structure may outperform a lighter disposable one if replacement, transport and user demand are significant. Evaluate the trade-off only after minimum safety, quality and legal requirements are satisfied.

### 6. Three guided practice problems

**Question A — reconstruct the physical account.** Starting from “Impact per service = cradle-to-grave impact / verified delivered service units; the numerator and denominator must refer to the same reference flow.”, identify the relevant system, quantities, physical units and hidden assumptions. Draw material or energy arrows, label the conserved quantity and explain at least two potential losses or quality changes. **Solution guidance:** reconcile consistent physical units and include process losses.

**Question B — independent worked sensitivity.** Recalculate 16.636364 kg CO₂e/use from an input of 11 uses; calculate a third point midway between 11 and 31, using the formula rather than copying the interactive output. Explain why a difference of -8.797654 kg CO₂e/use is not automatically a change in environmental benefit or service economics. **Solution guidance:** numerical sensitivity does not validate a site-specific benefit.

**Question C — design a decisive controlled comparison.** For “A supplier claims a lighter chair is automatically greener, although its seat fabric wears out earlier. Compare one chair purchased with 100,000 occupied seat-hours delivered at equal safety and comfort.”, provide a sample plan, at least one suitable control and one evidence source that does not share the same central assumption as the model. ## Lesson 1.2 — Circular stocks and flow mapping

### 1. Physics, chemistry and the function actually supplied

Stocks persist in buildings, vehicles and equipment while material flows enter, circulate and leave those stocks. A system can show high collection rates yet have little closed-loop material return because of losses, exports or accumulation in long-lived assets. Flow accounting requires explicit boundaries and consistent mass bases, including moisture, alloy constituents and products with multiple materials. Marketed recycling rates need a specified denominator: generated, collected, processed, or finally recovered output.

**Working relationship:** Input + opening stock = output + closing stock + accounted losses on a matching time basis. Keep the system boundary, physical units and quality specification visible before asserting an avoided impact.

### 2. A real engineering dilemma with rival explanations

**Problem statement:** A municipality celebrates 90% separate collection of aluminum beverage packaging; a smelter reports much lower qualified metal return. Trace every intermediate mass. Propose two rival mechanisms and one observation that differentiates them. Record timing, composition and functional performance before ranking either explanation.

**Constraint that can reverse the answer:** Informal collection, stockpiles, measurement moisture and discarded process rejects can make apparent recovery greater than reality. Estimate which service, quality or environmental condition could reverse the conclusion.

### 3. Transparent synthetic calculation and sensitivity

These are synthetic teaching calculations, not measurements. Independently verify units and a third input before interpretation.

**Why the numerical output has limits:** Start stock 5000 t; inflow 400 t/y; outflow 260 t/y; no nonlinear demand or stock aging. For a real decision, independently measure the variables that this illustrative formula holds constant.

## Lesson 1.3 — Material intensity and dematerialization

### 1. Physics, chemistry and the function actually supplied

Reducing kilograms per product can reduce extraction but may also change durability, maintenance or energy use. Material intensity should be tied to the unit of service and to the life-cycle stages affected by redesign. Substitution has cascading implications: thin sheet can demand new coatings, laminated joints can prevent disassembly, or lightweight vehicles can have manufacturing penalties offset by operating savings. A sensitivity analysis should show the break-even utilization at which redesign changes net impacts.

**Working relationship:** Total burden = initial manufacture + cumulative operation + replacements + end-of-life over the defined service horizon. Keep the system boundary, physical units and quality specification visible before asserting an avoided impact.

## Lesson 1.4 — Longevity, reliability and repair

### 1. Physics, chemistry and the function actually supplied

A product with a theoretical thirty-year life can deliver far less service if spare parts disappear, firmware support ends or critical components are bonded shut. Repairability depends on access, modularity, part availability, skill, diagnostic documentation and safe reassembly. Reliability is usually a distribution rather than a single mean; replaceability and recoverability must be evaluated for specific failure modes. Repair can retain embodied materials but must be compared with the efficiency and safety of newer alternatives.

**Working relationship:** Service fraction available = uptime / total scheduled use time under a declared maintenance policy. Keep the system boundary, physical units and quality specification visible before asserting an avoided impact.

### 2. A real engineering dilemma with rival explanations

## Lesson 1.5 — Rebound, substitution and absolute demand

### 1. Physics, chemistry and the function actually supplied

Efficiency improvements lower material use per service but can reduce prices and increase overall consumption. The rebound effect is a demand response, not an excuse to ignore efficient design. Absolute material demand is intensity multiplied by activity level; both can change. A recycled-content claim also needs evidence of displacement: recovered material may create an additional low-quality market rather than reduce primary production by one-for-one. Modeling should distinguish physical return, market-mediated substitution and policy constraints.

**Working relationship:** Total mass = material per fulfilled order × number of orders; compare both periods with comparable product mix. Keep the system boundary, physical units and quality specification visible before asserting an avoided impact.

### 2. A real engineering dilemma with rival explanations

**Problem statement:** Packaging becomes 25% lighter but online shipments grow quickly. Determine whether total packaging tonnage falls. Propose two rival mechanisms and one observation that differentiates them. Record timing, composition and functional performance before ranking either explanation.

**Constraint that can reverse the answer:** Assuming constant activity or perfect virgin displacement can overstate net savings. Estimate which service, quality or environmental condition could reverse the conclusion.

## Unit 1 capstone — challenge, calculation, evidence and choice

**Integrated case:** A university specifies a new fleet of classroom furniture. Procurement can minimize kilograms, initial carbon, purchase price, maintenance downtime, or replaced chairs, but these objectives do not rank products identically.

1. **Service definition and counterfactual.** Define the actual useful function and service horizon, identify the current material or process, and explain what product/service alternative is realistically available in the decision location. List safety and legal criteria that must be met before comparing optional environmental benefits.
2. **Mass, energy and quality ledger.** Construct a table of incoming material, recovered output, contaminants, rejects and inventory changes. Add the largest energy and water flows. Any unaccounted balance residual must be visibly labeled instead of silently treated as saleable recovered material.
3. **Two quantitative sensitivities.** Recalculate the unit's two interactive models. Change the default assumptions to two defensible adverse scenarios. Explain what is synthetic and what measurements would be needed to convert the question into a supplier-specific evaluation.
4. **Independent audit.** Design a sampling plan using **service reliability and traceable stock ledger**, recording **count useful delivered services, batch identity, retained stock, exits, returns and actual product failures** and challenging the decision with **customer cohort tracking paired with physical mass records**. Explain how many independent production batches, assets or user cycles are needed before generalization and how the result could be biased by rejected output.
5. **Decision and falsification.** Present a one-page recommendation with a go/no-go pilot, responsible decision-maker, estimated economic and environmental trade-offs, and a measurement that would overturn your recommendation. Specifically challenge **whether added efficiency increases use volume enough to increase total production**. No safety-critical release or environmental certification is authorized by the classroom exercises.

### After the capstone

Discuss whether a reduction in impact *per service* is likely to translate into lower *total* resource use. A more efficient or more recyclable product can still be produced in expanding quantities, and a durable product can still create local harms if its manufacturing is poorly controlled. Document the supply chain and user context in which the conclusion holds. A coherent sustainability argument preserves engineering functionality, mass conservation, traceability and the limits of the available evidence at the same time.
