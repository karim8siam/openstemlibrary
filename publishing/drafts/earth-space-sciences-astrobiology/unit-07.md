# Unit 7: Exoplanets and Remote Biosignature Science

**Guiding research question:** What can a small amount of distant light actually tell us about an unfamiliar planet?

Astrobiology is an inference-driven science. Researchers combine geochemistry, astronomy, biology, geology, statistics and engineering to ask what sorts of environments could support life, what chemical and physical processes could produce complex systems, and which observations can distinguish living processes from abiotic chemistry. The strength of an explanation depends on compatibility with measurements and on explicit testing of alternatives. This unit builds a sequence of five complementary questions rather than presenting an inventory of established answers about extraterrestrial life. There is no generally accepted confirmed detection of life beyond Earth within the scope of the cited background resources, so laboratory analogs and fictional mission examples must never be portrayed as discovered organisms.

The distinction between **necessary conditions**, **sufficient conditions** and **observed evidence** controls the interpretation throughout the unit. A resource such as water may be necessary for terrestrial organisms in a particular scenario while still being insufficient to show that any organism is present. A biosignature candidate can be ambiguous even when multiple measurements are precise. Conversely, the failure to observe an indicator does not establish that a site is uninhabited, especially if the instrument cannot access shielded or chemically altered material. Wherever numerical examples appear, the reader should identify the physical equation, the assumed boundary conditions and the unknown quantities deliberately simplified away. The goal is defensible reasoning, not a probability-of-life calculator.

## Learning objectives

After this unit, students should be able to state a relevant mechanistic hypothesis, define measurable observables and units, organize competing nonbiological explanations, calculate an explicitly idealized numerical example, design a contamination-control or instrument-calibration strategy, explain where a synthetic dataset stops being informative, and write a conclusion bounded by the actual evidence. Students should be able to identify links between the molecular scale and the whole-planet scale without confusing a laboratory success with a proven historical sequence.

## Lessons

### 7.1 Finding planets and selection effects

**Core explanation.** Transits measure dimming associated with orbital geometry; radial velocities constrain minimum masses in many orbital configurations. Detection methods preferentially reveal particular populations, so an observed catalog is not the underlying cosmic population. Uncertainties in the host star propagate into planetary estimates. A concrete measurement plan is: Derive a transit depth and analyze what unknown stellar radius does to the inferred planetary radius. The interpretation should specify instrument limitations, physical context and which rival models make distinct predictions. The purpose is to test a mechanism, not simply to accumulate correlated observations.

**Worked thought experiment.** A periodic dip suggests a small planet crossing an active star. The explanation compatible with biology must identify a causal process, a suitable environment and a route to the observable. A serious alternative is: Starspots, blended binaries and instrumental trends can produce or distort transit-like signals. A useful follow-up should compare a paired geological control and a negative instrument blank, while preserving the original location and sample treatment. If both hypotheses predict the same result at current precision, the outcome remains underdetermined.

**Quantitative example — Approximate transit depth.** Take the fictional inputs **Rp = 0.75 Earth radii; Sun-sized star**. Under the explicitly stated classroom relation **depth = 84 ppm·(Rp/R⊕)²**, the result is **47.25 ppm**. Assumes a Sun-sized star and neglects limb darkening and spots. Vary one measured input, inspect the sensitivity, and distinguish mathematical precision from the error in the physical assumptions. The result is an instructional calculation, not a mission observation or an estimate of the probability that extraterrestrial life exists.

**Exercises and discussion.** (1) State exactly what the hypothetical case measures. (2) Explain how starspots, blended binaries and instrumental trends can produce or distort transit-like signals. (3) Write a control that distinguishes source chemistry from analytical contamination. (4) Recalculate the example after increasing one input by ten percent and justify units. (5) Identify an independent measurement that would discriminate mechanisms. (6) Write a two-sentence conclusion containing both the result and its major uncertainty.

### 7.2 Habitable zones as conditional models

**Core explanation.** The circumstellar habitable zone is typically defined through liquid-water surface climate models subject to atmospheric and geological assumptions. Real planets may have subsurface environments beyond such zones or unsuitable surfaces within them. Orbital eccentricity and stellar history affect the reliability of a single-distance description. A concrete measurement plan is: Estimate incident flux for a range of stellar luminosities and orbital distances without equating a range with life probability. ### 7.3 Atmospheric spectroscopy and retrieval

**Core explanation.** Transmission and emission spectra sample wavelength-dependent opacity, cloud effects and thermal structure, while stellar contamination can modify the observed signal. Retrievals fit assumed models and may be nonunique. Reporting a molecule therefore requires posterior ranges, model choices and instrumental calibration context. ### 7.4 Oxygen, methane and contextual false positives

**Core explanation.** Atmospheric oxygen might be produced by biology but abiotic photolysis, water loss and geochemical factors must be assessed. Methane likewise has biological and geological sources; its abundance alone is not a life signature. Gas combinations can be more informative, but interpreting them demands a complete environmental model. A concrete measurement plan is: Construct coupled production–loss budgets for two gases and inspect how uncertain sinks affect interpretation. The interpretation should specify instrument limitations, physical context and which rival models make distinct predictions. The purpose is to test a mechanism, not simply to accumulate correlated observations.

### 7.5 Biosignature inference and observing strategies

**Core explanation.** Atmospheric life detection benefits from multiple observing epochs, complementary bands, realistic background models and explicit Bayesian or likelihood-based comparisons. A lack of detection can bound only the models accessible to the instrument. Follow-up priorities should maximize discrimination rather than simply repeat the most dramatic spectrum. A concrete measurement plan is: Simulate signal-to-noise scaling with repeat visits and evaluate correlated-noise limitations. The interpretation should specify instrument limitations, physical context and which rival models make distinct predictions. The purpose is to test a mechanism, not simply to accumulate correlated observations.

**Worked thought experiment.** An observatory receives time to observe one target again or several comparison stars. ## Integrated investigation 7: A synthetic evidence review

**Case definition.** A fictional interdisciplinary team is reviewing a proposed astrobiological target related to **exoplanets and remote biosignature science**. Its science lead receives eight columns of instrument-like readings, contextual notes and uncertainty ranges. The numbers are simulated and have deliberately incomplete information. The task is to decide whether the available resources should fund another measurement, whether a proposed life-related explanation is currently distinguishable, and what sample-handling records would be required to rule out a contamination event. The team must not silently upgrade a promising habitability assessment into a claim that organisms have been detected.

**Research workflow, phase one: reconstruct the observation.** Read the unit's synthetic CSV in `datasets/unit-07-synthetic.csv`. Every record should be interpreted as a fictitious spatial or temporal sampling event with a proxy signal and an uncertainty. Plot the raw proxy against sampling index, retain the baseline and calculate a median and interquartile range. Inspect whether signal varies smoothly with the contextual index or whether a small number of points dominate the result. Check for impossible physical values and missing units before attempting any fitting. If you choose to normalize, retain the original so another reader can reproduce the transformation. A nonzero proxy reading is not itself a biosignature because the proxy is intentionally not tied to a unique biological mechanism.

**Research workflow, phase two: compare mechanisms.** Construct three possible origins for the pattern: one biology-compatible pathway, a plausible inorganic reaction or transport process, and an instrumental artifact. The biological interpretation must identify how energy, matter and relevant environmental stability could support it. The abiotic candidate should identify specific minerals, radiation conditions or chemical pathways rather than the vague claim that 'nature could do it.' The instrumental candidate should identify how data handling could introduce a trend. For each explanation state one observation it uniquely predicts and one that it shares with the alternatives. Investigate whether a proposed test is truly independent. Repeating the same assay on samples prepared in the same batch might increase precision but not reveal preparation contamination.

**Research workflow, phase three: evaluate uncertainty.** Distinguish counting noise, calibration drift, incomplete spatial sampling, alteration after deposition and structural uncertainty in the chosen mechanism. The first two may be amenable to repeated measurements; the latter three require better design or a different observable. Report an interval or a plausible range when numerical assumptions permit, but do not assign empirical probabilities to the existence of life from invented classroom inputs. A negative result should explain the measurement detection threshold and the range of biological scenarios that the assay might miss. A positive result should describe alternate mechanisms, including routes that make organics, gases, textures or isotope patterns without living processes.

**Research workflow, phase four: propose the next instrument.** The team can choose one enhanced measurement, but must justify its effect on discrimination rather than on aesthetic resolution. For example, a spatially resolved mineral–organic association might distinguish transport histories, whereas a high-precision repeated spectrum could still be ambiguous if multiple species overlap. Design calibration standards, positive controls, negative blanks and records of transfer between instruments. Specify which data and code versions will be released so that independent investigators can attempt to reproduce the result. If destructive sample preparation is required, explain what sample fraction is retained and how archived material is linked to the original geological context.

**Deliverable.** Submit an 800–1,200 word fictional study report consisting of an abstract, instrument and data description, three mechanistic hypotheses, one worked idealized calculation, uncertainty table, proposed discriminating follow-up and public-facing conclusion. The public version must not use 'life discovered' language unless the defined evidence threshold has been independently satisfied; nothing in this synthetic exercise satisfies that standard. A strong report may conclude that the proposed biological interpretation is unresolved. Such a conclusion is valuable if it identifies a feasible measurement that could resolve uncertainty.

## Knowledge check

1. Explain a distinction between a necessary environmental condition and a life-specific indicator.
2. Describe why an organic molecule or water observation cannot independently demonstrate extraterrestrial life.
3. Identify two ways different sensors can share one source of systematic error.
4. Explain how an abiotic production pathway can change the meaning of a candidate biosignature.
5. Distinguish a detection limit from evidence of absence.
6. Provide a physically defined next test and the result that would falsify your preferred interpretation.

## Teaching-model interpretation

The two sliders in this unit operate on simple equations with explicitly specified parameters. Use them to discover the mathematical behavior of the equations, **not** to predict whether or where life exists. Change the input through the full displayed range, inspect whether the output responds monotonically and identify where the simplifications become unrealistic. A model that reproduces its own intended equation has passed only a software correctness check. Physical validation against independent observations and literature is a separate scientific activity. Take particular care when the displayed result is labeled probability: its prior odds and conditional assumptions are invented, not estimated from alien biospheres.

## Unit-specific advanced analysis: Exoplanets and Remote Biosignature Science

A fictional exoplanet orbits a small active star and produces a modest transit depth. The transit is repeated over four observing campaigns, each with a different noise floor. Estimate the planet radius only after stating an assumed stellar radius, and show why stellar-radius uncertainty propagates directly into planetary size. A weak spectral feature can be fit either by a molecule in the planetary atmosphere or by stellar heterogeneity coupled to processing choices. Propose a control observation that would characterize the star separately, then describe how alternative atmospheric compositions should be compared using the same raw data. Do not interpret a molecule from a single band as proof of life. Explain why a nominally habitable-zone orbit says little about persistent water without assumptions about pressure, composition, cloud feedbacks and evolutionary history. End with an observing plan prioritized for discrimination rather than headline appeal.
