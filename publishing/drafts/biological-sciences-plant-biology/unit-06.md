# Unit 06: Growth, Hormones and Development

**Level:** Undergraduate plant biology · **Scope:** theory, evidence, data and experimental interpretation.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, learners should be able to explain the four core topics, defend a prediction with a mechanistic model, identify measurement units, and evaluate at least one confounding variable.

### 6.1 Auxin and tropisms

**Core framework.** This section develops polar auxin transport, elongation and differential growth. In plant biology, structure and process cannot be separated: the same tissue changes its behavior with development, water status and environmental history. Start with observable features, specify what is measured, and only then choose a mechanism that could explain those observations. For auxin and tropisms, distinguish a descriptive pattern from a causal explanation and state which comparison could falsify the proposed mechanism.

**Mechanistic reasoning.** A phototropic response depends on signal integration and tissue context, not light direction alone. Connect this claim to three levels: molecules and membranes, cells and tissues, and the whole organism within its habitat. Identify the driving gradient or regulatory signal, where the resistance lies, and the time-scale of the response. This prevents a common mistake: interpreting a correlation as evidence that a single molecular route is sufficient to produce an organism-level result.

**Worked scientific workflow.** Place seedlings under unilateral light and quantify curvature against a dark control. Record the experimental unit, the treatment, a matched control, a measured response and potential confounders. Collect baseline information first. Repeated measurements from one individual are not independent biological replicates. Present uncertainty and an alternative explanation even when the predicted pattern occurs.

**Interpretation and limits.** A measured effect may be altered by species identity, genotype, developmental phase, growth conditions or instrument calibration. Report the measurement's physical units and whether the result is a rate, a concentration, a flux or a probability. Do not extrapolate the qualitative teaching model into an agronomic forecast. A scientifically persuasive account connects the observation to a testable mechanism while keeping alternative explanations visible.

**Practice prompts.** (1) Draw a causal diagram for auxin and tropisms. (2) Name two controls that would distinguish an environmental response from an ontogenetic effect. (3) Explain why biological replication and instrument calibration answer different questions. (4) Specify an outcome that would make you revise the initial hypothesis.

## Worked quantitative problems

### Problem 6.1 — controlled proportional model

A teaching experiment has a measured baseline of **12.8 units** and a fractional change of **0.4**. The task is to find the final value using the explicit assumption of proportional response. This is an arithmetic exercise, not a biological law.

1. **Define:** baseline = 12.8 units; fractional increase = 0.4; both must refer to the same observable and experimental time interval.
2. **Compute:** final value = baseline × (1 + fractional increase) = 12.8 × 1.40 = **17.920 units**.
3. **Evaluate:** the calculation assumes a linear proportional intervention without saturation. If biological feedback or resource limits operate, the measured final value can differ substantially. A valid test needs an independent control and biological replicates.

## Advanced investigation: growth control, signaling, and developmental timing

### Plant hormones are context-dependent regulators

Auxin patterns and polarized transport contribute to axis formation, tropisms, and organ development, but auxin concentration alone cannot predict the response of every tissue. Cytokinins often influence cell division and shoot-related developmental programs. Gibberellins can promote elongation and aspects of germination; abscisic acid participates in dormancy and drought responses; ethylene can regulate ripening and stress-associated development. These summaries are not one-hormone–one-effect rules. Tissue sensitivity, receptor expression, cross-talk, and developmental age alter outcomes.

Phototropism occurs when asymmetric growth redirects a shoot toward the light under appropriate conditions; it is not necessarily due to one side receiving more total energy for photosynthesis. Gravitropism involves directional sensing and altered growth in roots or shoots. Time-lapse image analysis can measure curvature, but a curvature angle is distinct from elongation rate. A short hypocotyl under one light quality cannot independently identify the concentration of a particular hormone.

### Development connects genetic programs to environment

Photoperiodic flowering pathways respond to the relative timing of light and darkness through circadian-regulated perception in many species. Vernalization is a prolonged cold-associated acquisition of flowering competence in certain plants. Some species require one cue, both cues, or neither. A plant's flowering date also depends on age, nutrition, temperature, and genotype; classifying all plants as strictly short-day or long-day ignores nonphotoperiodic forms and quantitative responses.

### Quantitative case: relative growth rate

A hypothetical group of independent plants has mean dry mass of 2.0 g at the start and 3.2 g after 14 days. Under an exponential-growth approximation, relative growth rate RGR = [ln(3.2) − ln(2.0)]/14 = ln(1.6)/14 ≈ 0.0336 day⁻¹. If the plants show delayed or sigmoidal growth, one interval cannot establish a constant RGR. Since destructive harvests cannot measure the same plant's dry mass twice, independent randomized cohorts may be necessary and uncertainty must be derived from replicate data.

**Experimental design.** Randomize seedlings among control and light-direction treatments; quantify stem curvature from standardized time-lapse images; independently record stem extension, photon flux, and temperature. For flowering, use a factorial photoperiod-by-temperature design while holding seedling age constant. Measure time to flowering and proportion flowering rather than collapsing both outcomes into a single ambiguous 'growth score.'

**Diagnostic questions.** Why can the same auxin treatment stimulate one organ and inhibit another? What distinguishes dormancy release from seed viability? How can a circadian clock cause different responses to equal-duration illumination delivered at different times? Why does final height fail to identify the mechanism controlling elongation?

## Practicum 6: Reproduction and developmental transitions

### The question and why it matters

**System:** Pollen germination and tube elongation. The central aim of this investigation is to move from a descriptive observation to a reproducible biological measurement. Experimental interpretations are strongest when the instrument output, the biological quantity of interest, and the causal claim are kept distinct. A quantitative value is useful only with its definition, units, assumptions and uncertainty. Many textbook diagrams intentionally omit these constraints; laboratory investigation is where those omissions become visible.

### Experimental design

Collect fresh pollen from a defined plant genotype and developmental stage. Prepare an in-vitro germination medium and establish a pilot to identify appropriate sugar, calcium and boron concentrations for the species. Score germination using a predefined minimum tube-length criterion to avoid post hoc threshold changes. Select microscope fields randomly and record whether tubes are damaged or folded. Replicate across flowers and individuals; hundreds of grains on one microscope slide are not hundreds of independent plants.

Before collecting data, write a hypothesis with a directional prediction, define an observation window, and select a primary outcome. Predefine exclusion criteria for damaged material and log departures from the protocol. Plan at least three truly independent biological replicates for a small teaching pilot, while recognizing that a formal sample-size calculation depends on expected effect and variability. Random allocation mitigates positional bias but cannot fix systematic instrument calibration error. Mask sample identifiers during image scoring if practical; if blinding is impossible, use automated measurements and a predeclared scoring rubric to reduce subjective decisions.

### Worked quantitative interpretation

In two media, 42 of 70 and 56 of 80 pollen grains germinate. Estimated proportions are 0.60 and 0.70, so the difference is 0.10 (ten percentage points), not a 10% relative increase. Relative improvement compared with the first medium is 0.10/0.60 ≈ 16.7%. Sampling dependence and plant-to-plant variation require careful treatment before an inferential statement.

A reproducible report should retain raw readings alongside any normalized value. If repeated values appear, show their distribution and the source of variability before constructing confidence intervals. Converting a measured number into a larger biological conclusion requires an explicit assumption chain. For example, a bulk gravimetric measurement does not resolve cell-level transport; a count of responsive cells does not measure pathway activation; an averaged population response does not characterize every individual. These distinctions prevent overstating the meaning of the calculated estimate.

### Mistake to identify and repair

Pollen grains that germinate rapidly must lead to more seeds: not proven. Fertilization, compatibility, ovule availability, resource allocation, and abortion determine realized seed set. Lab pollen performance is not a direct field-reproductive-success assay.

The repair is to formulate two competing explanations and state a discriminating measurement. Draw a causal graph with the manipulated exposure, the proposed mediator, the measured endpoint, and at least one confounder. Separating a mechanistic hypothesis from its observable consequences is essential because several mechanisms can predict the same direction of change. When no available measurement separates the explanations, report the result as descriptive rather than mechanistically decisive.

### Data record and quality controls

Record a unique replicate identifier; species, genotype and source where applicable; pretreatment growth conditions; measurement date; treatment assignment; instrument resolution; sample handling; and the raw endpoint. Use SI-compatible units and explicitly document any conversion. If a balance reads to 0.01 g, reporting a derived mass to six decimal places falsely suggests precision. Verify blank controls, out-of-range samples, and response saturation before averaging. If an instrument drifts over a session, record standards at both the beginning and end instead of simply assuming constancy. Archive sample exclusions with reasons, including exclusions made after seeing a result.

Distinguish **technical replication**, where one biological sample is measured repeatedly, from **biological replication**, where independently sampled individuals, populations, or experimental units are compared. Use the structure of the intervention to identify the correct unit for statistical analysis: if an entire container receives the treatment, the container is not interchangeable with each plant inside it. A design with many observations but few independent treated units can produce precise-looking yet misleading uncertainty estimates.

## Quantitative research clinic: Hormone interactions and factorial design

Plant hormones are signals in networks, not independent switches. The direction and size of an auxin response depend on tissue, dose, developmental state and interaction with other signals. Gibberellins can promote growth in some contexts, while abscisic acid is important for drought responses and dormancy; neither hormone should be described as universally activating or suppressing all growth processes. A factorial experiment is useful because it can reveal interactions missed by one-factor-at-a-time testing.

**Worked two-factor design.** Suppose mean hypocotyl length in a teaching dataset is 8 mm without either treatment, 11 mm with treatment A alone, 9 mm with B alone and 15 mm with both. The additive prediction for the combined treatment is 8+(11−8)+(9−8)=12 mm. The observed combined mean is 3 mm above this additive prediction. That arithmetic describes an interaction contrast, but whether the effect is distinguishable from noise depends on biological replicate variance and design. Do not infer a receptor-level mechanism from four means.

**Experimental controls.** Deliver solvent-only controls, document application position and concentration, and account for unequal germination or initial seedling size. Dose–response relationships can be nonlinear, so interpreting two doses as a universal trend is risky. Randomize pot positions or rotate them when directional illumination creates gradients. If all treated seedlings occupy one tray, tray environment may be confounded with the hormone treatment. Biological independence belongs to the unit that received the intervention.

**Mechanistic follow-up.** Compare the phenotype in two distinct tissues, sample transcript or reporter responses when appropriate, and test whether a response persists if another pathway is perturbed. Temporal resolution matters because gene-expression changes can precede measurable elongation. A useful lab report explicitly states which data show an association, which show an interaction, and which additional interventions would be required to identify pathway order.
