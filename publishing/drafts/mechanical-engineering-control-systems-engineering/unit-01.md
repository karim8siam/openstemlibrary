# U01-L01 — Control objectives, signals, and feedback

**Mechanical Engineering · Control Systems Engineering · Unit 1: Modeling, Feedback, and Control Architecture.**  This is an unreviewed instructional chapter; none of its calculations alone justifies commissioning machinery.

## What you should be able to do

Explain **control objectives, signals, and feedback** without confusing measured variables, hidden dynamics and desired behavior; write the main equation and derive its assumptions; reproduce the numerical illustration; diagnose at least two ways the model may mislead; and propose a practical, limited-risk verification experiment.

## Physical interpretation

Reference r, measured output y_m, actual output y, error e=r−y_m, command u and disturbance d describe different physical channels. Negative feedback uses an error signal but does not magically correct unmeasured variables. A speed controller must distinguish commanded speed from encoder measurement and distinguish load torque from motor current.

**System context.** A conveyor speed loop must reject varying load torque without accelerating the belt abruptly. The objective in this lesson is not an abstract good-looking response: Identify the plant, sensor, actuator, disturbances and task before writing a controller equation. Establish whether the lesson concerns the plant itself, the mathematical input/output map, an estimator, a controller, or a test method. Those roles cannot be interchanged. A controller parameter may improve the command-response plot and simultaneously create unacceptable actuator current, mechanical strain or sensor-noise transmission.

## Derivation and use

Use a signal-and-energy diagram before a block diagram. Draw the motor, drive, load, encoder and computer; attach units to every arrow. A measurement can be delayed, noisy or biased, which means the error includes instrument effects.

**Dimensional and sign audit.** Before substituting values, define an input and output in physical units, choose a positive direction, identify zero or equilibrium conditions, and make sure both sides of any differential or algebraic equation have the same dimensions. For control objectives, signals, and feedback, changing coordinates or the chosen sensor location can reverse the sign or modify the apparent dynamics. A formula for ideal negative feedback must not be used when actual wiring implements positive feedback. Explicitly distinguish a continuous-time differential equation from a discrete update at sample index k.

## Worked numerical illustration

At a 1500 rpm command and 1440 rpm encoder reading the displayed error is 60 rpm. If the encoder has a +20 rpm bias, actual speed is about 1420 rpm and the true tracking difference is about 80 rpm.

**Reproduction protocol.** Write the givens and units in a table, compute any derived time scales or gains, substitute into the displayed model and calculate the result with more precision than required for the final reported value. Compare against a limiting case: zero disturbance, zero controller gain, vanishing sample time, or a stationary equilibrium as appropriate. If a calculation predicts infinite control force, negative dissipation, or motion through a hard stop, that is an indication to revisit assumptions, not a valid operating instruction.

## What the calculation does not establish

When the commanded quantity is position rather than speed, the sensor and actuator limits require a different architecture.

This limitation matters particularly for **control objectives, signals, and feedback**. The simplified example contains no manufacturing tolerances, no equipment-specific safety certification, and no direct evidence about the chosen hardware. Extra physics—sensor lag, actuator limits, cross-coupling, higher modes, friction, temperature drift or sampling delay—must be assessed according to the application. A model can be mathematically stable while the experiment violates the permitted range of current, speed, force or temperature.

## Practical reasoning and validation

1. **Define the measurement.** For this lesson, identify the smallest set of sensors that can distinguish the predicted mechanism from an alternative explanation. Record calibration, range, resolution, sampling rate and timestamps. Do not use commanded actuator signals as though they were measured actuator output.

2. **Plan excitation.** Choose a reversible stimulus small enough to respect the independent safety limits in the case: A conveyor speed loop must reject varying load torque without accelerating the belt abruptly. A step, ramp, chirp, frequency sweep or deliberate small load perturbation may be appropriate, but there is no universal safe test amplitude. Start with the smallest informative test.

3. **Predict first.** Use the selected model to forecast the direction, scale and timing of at least one observation. Include what would refute the prediction. Predictions recorded before an experiment reduce the temptation to tune the story after seeing the result.

4. **Collect independent evidence.** Compare the predicted and measured quantities at more than one operating condition, including a held-out condition not used to fit parameters. Report transients, steady behavior and the actuator command on separate channels. Record uncertainty and do not hide residual patterns by averaging them away.

5. **Revise and document.** If the data disagree, consider model order, omitted delay, calibration bias, nonlinearity or incorrect sign before raising controller gain. Save the model version, parameter ranges, test date, and outstanding safety questions.

## Guided problems

**Problem A — definitions.** Draw the complete signal path for a system that must demonstrate control objectives, signals, and feedback. Mark the reference, measured output, actual output, process disturbance and actuator constraint. State which quantities can be directly measured and which must be estimated.

**Problem B — quantitative reproduction.** Rework the example in this lesson using SI units, then change one numerical input by 10%. Report the result and a dimensionless sensitivity ratio. Explain whether a first-order approximation is appropriate.

**Problem C — alternate assumption.** Repeat the reasoning after adding either finite sample time, sensor bias or actuator saturation. Identify precisely which mathematical step is no longer valid and whether the expected effect is on stability, steady-state error or transient response.

**Problem D — experiment design.** Write a one-page safe bench-test protocol covering instrument checks, recorded variables, stimulus limits, shutdown logic and criteria for rejecting a fitted model. You must distinguish acceptance of a numerical model from approval to operate equipment.

**Problem E — peer challenge.** Argue against a proposed design that optimizes only one metric (such as settling time). Name at least two competing metrics and explain why evidence from the running case might change the preferred design.

## Worked answer path (not a substitute for a full design)

For A, write a block diagram with sensor noise entering the measurement channel and the disturbance entering the correct physical location; this prevents confusion between feedback error and actual tracking error. For B, compute the baseline result, recompute at 1.10 times the chosen parameter, and use sensitivity approximately (Δoutput/output)/(Δparameter/parameter), describing sign and any nonlinear dependence. For C, identify the changed assumption first; do not silently reuse the nominal transfer function. For D, report a test trace and a safety boundary independently. For E, make the tradeoff explicit rather than calling the fastest response automatically best.

## Reflection and next step

Separate the equation derived under assumptions from the test that could challenge it. Identify a single observation that would make you question the conclusion about **control objectives, signals, and feedback**.
