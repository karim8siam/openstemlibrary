# Unit 1: Earth Systems, Environmental Evidence and Responsibility

**Guiding question:** How do connected physical systems and unequal exposure turn a geological change into a public decision?

Environmental geoscience connects observation to decisions about land, water, air, ecosystems and people. In this unit, consider the fictional teaching setting of **a rapidly growing river city built on floodplain sediment**. Each lesson explains a mechanism, demonstrates a transparent numerical screen and develops an observation plan capable of finding evidence *against* a favored interpretation. No case is a real contamination event and no value should be treated as a regulatory limit.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit students should be able to (a) diagram relevant stocks, flows, feedbacks and boundaries; (b) identify at least two mechanisms compatible with incomplete data; (c) compute the unit-consistent screen and recognize its simplifying assumptions; (d) design a measured contrast or control; (e) discuss the difference between evidence for a process and the ethical choice of response; and (f) prepare a short decision note with uncertainty, stakeholder impacts and specific follow-up actions.

## Five lessons


### 1.1 Systems boundaries and stocks

**Core mechanism and governing scale.** An environmental system has boundaries chosen for a question, rather than borders drawn by nature. A municipal aquifer, a river reach and the overlying atmosphere exchange water and chemicals; a stock can remain nearly constant even while large opposing fluxes pass through it. A model that tracks only a stock may miss an accelerating throughput or a delayed downstream effect. This is a physical claim about a set of processes, not a description of a single visible feature. To use it rigorously, distinguish the quantity being measured from a proxy and draw an explicit spatial and temporal boundary. If the relevant mass or energy can cross that boundary, the investigator must consider transfers as well as any material produced or consumed internally. A strong explanation predicts how a change in forcing would alter at least two independently observable consequences. Identify those predictions before seeing the sample data, so an initially appealing interpretation can genuinely be rejected.

**Site investigation.** Map a catchment, shallow aquifer and city water supply as separate compartments. Mark imported water, recharge, pumping, wastewater return and evaporation as directed fluxes, not interchangeable totals. Write the site description in a way that another geoscientist can reconstruct the comparison. Record location, elevation or depth, coordinate system, local time, sampling interval, instrument settings, quality-control flags and any reason observations were excluded. A sample label should distinguish a point from a mixture of points, and a laboratory result should identify the phase analyzed: filtered water, whole water, sediment, porewater or another material. For a process that varies after rainfall, one visit gives a snapshot rather than a stable baseline. Use pre-event and post-event observations where the mechanism makes a temporal prediction.

**Working with competing explanations.** A monthly water-balance estimate can conceal daily storms and seasonal pumping. An apparently closed budget may actually be missing subsurface leakage, trade in bottled water or human changes to drainage. Formulate two alternative mechanisms that could plausibly produce the observation, then specify a measurement that would discriminate them. Merely collecting more measurements of the same ambiguous quantity may increase precision without increasing explanatory power. Test whether the apparent change can arise from sensor drift, dilution, sample composition, changed boundary conditions or an unrelated common driver. A transparent analysis reports which explanations were evaluated and which remain unresolved. Numerical agreement between a model and data is not a sufficient test if the model parameters were selected using those same data.

**Decision interpretation.** Before approving new wells, disclose the model boundary and at least one measurement outside it. Compare decisions using broad and narrow catchment boundaries. State the practical action that could change under the result, then calculate how an error in the parameter would affect that action. For a preliminary screening study, use conservative but explicit uncertainty bounds rather than claim exact compliance. Distinguish a threshold from a causal mechanism: exceeding a screening value may justify follow-up, while remaining below it does not automatically prove no ecological or human-health impact. Prioritize measures that interrupt a credible exposure pathway if an urgent hazard is identified, and refer regulatory or public-health determinations to competent authorities.

**Worked quantitative screen (1.1).** *balance.* Use the wholly invented parameters **P=900 mm, ET=500 mm, Q=280 mm**. The teaching expression is **P − ET − Q**; substitute consistently converted units to obtain **900 − 500 − 280 = 120**, or approximately **120 mm storage residual**. The arithmetic illustrates the chosen model; it should not be mistaken for a field observation or a calibrated regulatory prediction. Ask whether doubling one input would double the output. If it would not, explain the nonlinearity. Name one missing parameter that could reverse a site decision despite this seemingly precise result.

### 1.3 Hazard, exposure and vulnerability

**Core mechanism and governing scale.** Hazard describes a potentially damaging physical event or process, not a prediction of harm to a particular population. Exposure concerns assets or people in the affected location; vulnerability includes susceptibility and limits on capacity to anticipate, cope and recover. The same flood depth can produce very different outcomes where building elevations, services and mobility differ. This is a physical claim about a set of processes, not a description of a single visible feature. A causal diagram should distinguish source, transport, intake, baseline conditions and confounders such as occupation or smoking. A mechanistic pathway is stronger when multiple observations in space and time agree with physical transport constraints. This is a physical claim about a set of processes, not a description of a single visible feature. ## Integrated investigation 1: River-city planning and buried water connections

**Research brief.** Propose an aquifer–stormwater–river cross-section that includes a leaking sewer, a pumping well, an informal settlement and an upstream industrial parcel. Work with the accompanying `datasets/unit-01-synthetic.csv` file. The data are deliberately synthetic and not observations from a real study. Students must not use the outputs to locate actual pollution or certify safety. Before drawing a graph, write down the spatial and temporal extent of the question, the target population of inference, and the causal alternative being examined. If these differ across competing hypotheses, explicitly state the difference rather than changing the question after plotting the data.

**Initial scenario evidence.** In the synthetic city, the dry-season well head falls while the river conductivity rises. A newly built drain can transport sediment across a land boundary while a subsurface clay lens limits some pathways. An appropriate notebook records the stated evidence separately from new calculations and speculative explanations. Construct a table with source, transport medium, receptor, measurement, unit, time stamp and quality flag. Do not automatically interpolate through a gap. Describe what direct observations could distinguish spatial gradients from a measurement artifact. Think carefully about whether measurements made on the same day are independent: weather, laboratory batches and access constraints can introduce common disturbances.

**Hypothesis comparison.** Competing interpretations are aquifer depletion, seasonal river chemistry, a measurement offset and changed drainage connectivity. Their predictions differ by depth, timing and water-chemistry co-variation. Express each hypothesis as a short process chain, from cause to transport or transformation and then to observable consequence. For each chain, describe a finding that would weaken it even if the original observation remained true. This is more demanding than simply asking students to find supporting evidence. Write a four-row evidence matrix containing current observations, ambiguous clues, missing observations and tests capable of discrimination. Use a mixture of field observations and existing institutional records rather than putting the entire burden on the laboratory assay.

**Measurement design.** Select a reference location, a likely impact location and at least one location that tests the suspected transport direction. Plan a repeat period long enough to capture the dominant forcing, with a predeclared justification for the number and location of samples. Document the measurement unit, calibration procedure, field duplicate, transport method, chain-of-custody practice when needed and appropriate data-handling permissions. Explain what a non-detect means in relation to the reporting limit. If the accessible sampling locations omit vulnerable groups, state how that limitation affects the conclusion rather than quietly treating data absence as exposure absence.

**Quantitative analysis.** Read the CSV and calculate the mean, range and a simple temporal contrast for one indicator. Plot indicator against the forcing variable and inspect outliers on the original scale before considering transformation. Report the result with units and avoid inferring causation from one correlation coefficient. Test sensitivity to removal of one observation, alternative grouping of sites and plausible systematic error. If a variable is coded as a toy index, keep it clearly labeled as such; there is no physical unit hiding behind a derived ranking. A good report includes a notebook where a reader can reproduce every number without proprietary data access.

**Uncertainty and decision.** Compare exposure reduction by alternative drinking-water supply, surface-drain repair and reduced pumping. Which action protects people while mechanism studies continue? List which uncertainties are reducible through new measurements and which depend on social values or future land-use choices. Produce at least two recommendations: a near-term low-regret action and a longer-term investigation or remediation option. For each, state the measurable trigger that would alter the recommendation and the organization that must act. An unexplained rank ordering is weaker than a transparent comparison across environment, health, fairness, time, cost and implementability. If a real-world hazard were suspected, qualified professionals and local authorities would have to replace these toy values with regulatory procedures.

**Deliverables and review rubric.** Submit a labeled site diagram; a one-page hypothesis table; a reproducible calculation file; a figure whose axis labels include units; a paragraph describing alternative mechanisms; a decision table with at least three choices; and an evidence-quality note. Award 20% for physically plausible mechanisms, 20% for transparent data provenance, 20% for unit-consistent arithmetic and uncertainty checks, 20% for discriminating experiment design and 20% for decision clarity and treatment of affected stakeholders. A polished figure alone receives no substitute credit for missing science.




## Unit 1 consolidation and peer critique

Construct a two-page dossier for **a rapidly growing river city built on floodplain sediment**. Start with a map or cross-section of the physical environment, then list stocks, fluxes or forces that operate on multiple time scales. Use one instrument observation, one derived physical quantity, one sensitivity test and one clearly identified decision assumption. Organize claims by evidence quality and specify what additional field or laboratory result would change the conclusion. A scientifically defensible exercise may conclude that the available fictional evidence is insufficient for strong attribution.

For peer review, exchange dossiers and ask a partner to locate unsupported causal leaps, missing mass-balance terms, numerical unit mistakes and hidden stakeholder costs. The reviewer should mark every model parameter as measured, calibrated, hypothesized or fixed for teaching. In a revision note, explain what changed and what remains unknown. Do not substitute an unverified AI summary or a single plotted trend for this explicit review process.

**Mastery test:** Define the core process in one paragraph, show one correct calculation with units, draw two plausible mechanisms capable of producing the same initial observation and give a measurement that separates them. Make a separate recommendation that identifies who benefits or bears costs; explicitly label which part is scientific inference and which part is a value-based decision.
