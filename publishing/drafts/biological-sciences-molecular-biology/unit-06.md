# Unit 6: Translation

## Unit overview

Translation uses RNA sequence as a template but depends on much more than matching triplets to amino acids. tRNA charging, codon recognition, ribosome function, initiation and elongation are active biochemical processes. Subcellular targeting and folding mean that a synthesized chain is not necessarily a functional mature protein. Changes in protein abundance can result from altered synthesis or degradation, and a high ribosome density could reflect slow elongation rather than high output. The molecular interpretation must therefore use at least two separate readouts whenever translational control is claimed.

## Learning goals

Understand the causal molecular sequence for four named processes; derive and interpret simplified quantitative statements; identify measurement controls; separate experimental observation from molecular inference; and explain the limits of the idealized simulation models.

## Unit synthesis

The translation system must translate a sequence, choose a start, preserve a frame and deliver a mature product to an appropriate location. A working ribosome changes physical position, but that movement alone does not show final protein function. A protein could be correctly sequenced yet misfolded, degraded or localized to the wrong compartment. Draw one secreted-protein trajectory from transcription through RNA export, cytosolic translation initiation, signal recognition and ER-associated processing, then compare it with a soluble cytosolic enzyme. Interpret what a loss of fluorescence could mean at three stages: less translation, accelerated protein degradation and impaired fluorophore maturation. To identify translational control specifically, pair an RNA measurement with a rate-sensitive nascent-chain measurement; steady protein abundance is a delayed composite.

## Unit-specific integrated assessment

To evaluate translational reasoning, provide equal steady-state messenger RNA counts from two experimental conditions but different amounts of mature protein. Students must consider initiation frequency, ribosome elongation, cotranslational degradation, targeting and post-translational stability. A pulse of labeled amino acid would report synthesis more directly than a final fluorescent-protein measurement, but isotope labeling has its own recovery and temporal assumptions. Include a short coding sequence with a one-base insertion and ask students to find the shifted reading frame before predicting possible stop codons. A strong solution records what is known at the DNA, RNA, ribosome and protein levels separately. The aim is to connect decoding chemistry with functional maturation and to show why a translation-rate estimate does not alone predict a cell's final enzyme activity.

## 6.1 Genetic code and reading frames

### Mechanistic foundation

Ribosomes interpret mRNA triplets as codons in a defined reading frame. Initiation establishes the frame, and most codons specify one amino acid while three canonical stop codons signal termination in the standard code. Some organisms and organelles use deviations; recoding events such as selenocysteine incorporation require specialized context. Frame shifts alter downstream triplet grouping and often create premature stop codons. Protein sequence is not determined by mRNA alone when RNA editing or recoding occurs.

The starting claim is that ribosomes interpret mRNA triplets as codons in a defined reading frame. In an active cell, production and consumption often occur simultaneously. For genetic code and reading frames, the distinction is especially consequential because protein sequence is not determined by mRNA alone when RNA editing or recoding occurs.

A fourth condition should deliberately test an alternative explanation.

### Discriminating causes and ruling out artifacts

An uninterrupted open reading frame does not prove translation; initiation signals, RNA accessibility and ribosome engagement must be considered. Build two alternative hypotheses that could mimic the predicted observation.

A meaningful negative result needs equal scrutiny. Establish the dynamic range with positive and negative controls.

### Specific molecular investigation

A one-base deletion within a coding region changes the codon grouping downstream of the deletion. To reason about the protein effect, mark the original start codon and group nucleotides into triples before and after the deletion. Do not merely count the total number of bases. The resulting frame may encounter a premature stop, but the protein endpoint also depends on whether the mutant RNA is translated or subjected to surveillance. A reporter that produces little fluorescence may indicate reduced RNA abundance as well as a truncated protein. Verify the DNA change and independently measure mature RNA before concluding that a particular peptide sequence was produced.

## 6.2 Ribosome mechanics

### Mechanistic foundation

The ribosome has decoding and peptidyl-transferase functions and moves along mRNA while tRNAs cycle through binding states. Peptide synthesis involves delivery of charged tRNA, decoding, peptide-bond formation and translocation. Elongation can pause at difficult sequences or structural regions. The ribosome is a molecular machine whose rate depends on codon context, tRNA availability, energy supply and stress.

### Specific molecular investigation

Ribosome footprints accumulate upstream of a highly structured RNA segment after a treatment. A simple interpretation of high ribosome density as enhanced translation is vulnerable because pausing can increase density while reducing completed protein output. A pulse-label assay for nascent polypeptides and a protein accumulation time course would separate occupancy from completion. Match the treatment to an RNA abundance measurement, as a transcript increase can also elevate footprint counts. If footprint lengths shift, altered nuclease behavior or sample preparation may contribute. Ribosome profiling is a powerful readout of protected positions, but its reconstruction requires assumptions about initiation, elongation and recovery.

## 6.3 Protein targeting and maturation

### Mechanistic foundation

Translation can occur on free cytosolic ribosomes or at membrane-associated ribosomes directed by nascent signal peptides. Secretory and membrane proteins may enter endoplasmic reticulum pathways co-translationally; nuclear, mitochondrial and peroxisomal targeting generally use distinct sequence and transport machinery. After synthesis, proteins can require folding, cofactors, proteolytic processing or post-translational modification.

## 6.4 Translational control

### Mechanistic foundation

Cells modulate initiation factors, upstream open reading frames, RNA structures, codon-dependent elongation and RNA-binding proteins to reshape protein synthesis rapidly. Stress-associated mechanisms can broadly reduce translation while allowing particular messages to be translated preferentially. Ribosome loading reflects multiple processes and can increase because elongation is stalled rather than because more protein is completed.

## Fully worked questions

All numerical inputs are constructed teaching examples. Where a model is conditional, that condition is part of the solution.

### Problem 1: Translation traversal time

A 420-amino-acid protein is translated at an ideal constant elongation rate of 14 amino acids per second. Estimate a single ribosome elongation time.

**Worked reasoning**

1. Time≈420/14=30 seconds, ignoring initiation and termination overhead.
2. If codon pauses or cotranslational folding slow progression, actual times differ.
3. Protein output per mRNA also depends on how frequently ribosomes initiate.

### Problem 2: Polypeptide sequence length

A coding DNA sequence contains 1002 nucleotides including a terminal stop codon and no introns. How many amino-acid residues are encoded in the standard code?

**Worked reasoning**

1. 1002/3=334 codons total.
2. One is a stop codon that does not specify an amino acid, so the expected polypeptide length is 333 residues.
3. This assumes the start is in the annotated frame and there is no unusual recoding.

### Problem 3: Translational repression

An illustrative repression law is output=1/[1+(R/K)^2]. For R=12 and K=6 in matching concentration units, find output fraction.

**Worked reasoning**

1. R/K=2, square=4, so relative output=1/(1+4)=0.20.
2. This corresponds to 20% of the modeled unrepressed value.
3. A Hill-type fit is phenomenological and cannot tell whether the molecular step affected is initiation, elongation or RNA stability.

## Simulations and assumptions

The two simulation cards below are local, offline-capable numerical thought experiments. They are parameterized formula demonstrations, not mechanistic claims about a real cell type. Inspect the stated assumptions first, then vary an input, compare limiting cases and reset the model to its default.

### Model focus: Ribosome elongation traversal

At a constant translation elongation speed v in amino acids per second, a ribosome producing a peptide of n amino acids has a traversal approximation t=n/v. Four hundred twenty residues at fourteen residues per second require approximately thirty seconds. Doubling the protein length doubles this ideal traversal time, whereas doubling the speed halves it. Initiation frequency, codon-specific pauses, availability of charged tRNA, co-translational folding and termination are absent from the equation. A ribosome-occupancy experiment that shows more footprints on a message is not enough to conclude that protein output has increased. A complementary nascent-chain or short-pulse proteomics measurement would better isolate the synthesis rate.

### Model application and sensitivity test

A peptide with length 420 amino acids requires thirty seconds of ideal elongation at fourteen residues per second. The same peptide would require sixty seconds at seven residues per second. This is a traversal time for one ribosome, not the interval between completed proteins at steady state. If multiple ribosomes initiate at staggered times on one mRNA, production may continue at a faster rate than one product per traversal time, subject to ribosome spacing and initiation constraints. Now imagine a treatment causes ribosome density to increase while elongation speed decreases. Both effects can occur together without an increase in completed protein. Test the hypothesis using an assay sensitive to newly synthesized protein and a method reporting ribosome occupancy, measured at the same time. Protein maturation, turnover and secretory traffic create additional delays between synthesis and a functional output. An ideal speed formula is useful precisely because it separates these effects.

### Model focus: Hill-form translation repression

A Hill-style normalized response for a translational repressor can be written R=1/[1+(x/K)^2], where x is repressor abundance and K marks the half-response point. The displayed percentage is 100R. When x=0 the modeled output is fully unrepressed; at x=K it is fifty percent; and at x=2K it is twenty percent. The exponent two describes the steepness of a chosen empirical curve. It does not establish that two molecules bind cooperatively to a specific site. Direct interactions, RNA degradation, translation initiation and elongation can produce superficially similar concentration-response curves. Combine a site-mutant reporter with native RNA and protein measurements to locate the actual regulated step.

### Model application and sensitivity test

Compare the Hill-style repression curve at zero repressor, at the parameter K and at three times K. The output percentages are one hundred, fifty and ten. The square-law curve becomes steep near the midpoint but never predicts a negative output. That observation is mathematical, not direct evidence of molecular cooperativity. Imagine introducing a microRNA mimic that lowers reporter fluorescence strongly while the reporter RNA itself also declines. It would be premature to say the entire effect acts at ribosome initiation. Construct a native-target rescue in which a predicted binding site is changed without broadly changing RNA structure, and measure RNA disappearance separately. A second reporter lacking the binding site should serve as a specific negative comparator. If global translation is suppressed by the perturbation, a site-specific interpretation may fail. The simulator describes a response law; identifying the regulated biochemical reaction requires additional evidence.
