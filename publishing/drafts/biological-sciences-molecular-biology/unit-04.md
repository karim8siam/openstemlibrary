# Unit 4: Transcription

## Unit overview

Transcription is not a single instantaneous event. Promoter recognition, initiation, elongation, RNA processing and termination have different kinetics and may respond differently to regulators. Regulatory binding provides an input state, but RNA synthesis is an output that must be measured separately. In bacteria, the relative spatial proximity of transcription and translation provides possibilities not typical of the same arrangement in a eukaryotic nucleus. In eukaryotes, chromatin and distal elements can influence access and recruitment. Identify the intermediate state each experimental method measures rather than collapsing every assay into gene expression.

## Learning goals

Understand the causal molecular sequence for four named processes; derive and interpret simplified quantitative statements; identify measurement controls; separate experimental observation from molecular inference; and explain the limits of the idealized simulation models.

## Unit synthesis

Gene expression is often used as an umbrella phrase even when investigators measured only one stage. Construct a timeline with promoter occupancy, polymerase recruitment, nascent RNA, mature RNA and accumulated protein. A change in mature RNA may arise from altered synthesis or turnover; a change in protein may arise from translation or degradation; an unchanged occupancy measurement is compatible with several downstream regulatory changes. Choose assays that make those possibilities distinguishable. A useful model comparison holds promoter occupancy constant while changing RNA decay. The mature RNA may shift despite no difference in initiation; the inverse experiment changes initiation while compensating with decay to keep the RNA pool stable. These thought experiments show why separate molecular states must be quantified before selecting a regulatory mechanism.

## Unit-specific integrated assessment

A useful integrated transcription problem begins with a promoter that binds its regulator strongly yet produces few transcripts in one cell type. The regulator's binding curve and the RNA-production curve are plotted separately. Learners should propose tests for missing polymerase recruitment, promoter-incompatible chromatin, insufficient activating partners, or premature termination. The assessment then introduces an RNA with an unusually long measured half-life. Even modest initiation can support a sizable RNA pool when loss is slow. Ask for a time-course that distinguishes slow initiation from slow decay, and require consistent units for production and loss. The final report should state why a high steady-state RNA level cannot be read as a high transcriptional initiation rate without a separate measure of RNA kinetics. A precise causal model makes distinct predictions for nascent and mature RNA rather than using expression as an unspecified outcome.

## 4.1 Promoters and initiation

### Mechanistic foundation

RNA polymerase begins RNA production near promoters after initiation factors or bacterial sigma-factor mechanisms help identify appropriate DNA sequences. Promoter accessibility, sequence recognition and regulatory complexes jointly influence the frequency of initiation. An occupancy model can illustrate how binding changes with free regulator concentration, but binding alone need not yield productive polymerase initiation. Direction is determined by the template and promoter configuration; distinct genes may be transcribed from opposite genomic strands.

A fourth condition should deliberately test an alternative explanation.

### Quantitative thinking without false precision

### Discriminating causes and ruling out artifacts

A strong occupancy signal cannot establish elongation competence, and promoter consensus scores are not universal quantitative predictors of expression. Build two alternative hypotheses that could mimic the predicted observation.

A meaningful negative result needs equal scrutiny. Establish the dynamic range with positive and negative controls.

### Specific molecular investigation

A repressor-binding assay shows a strong increase in occupancy when free ligand concentration rises from 6 to 18 nanomolar. Using a single-site dissociation constant of 6 nanomolar predicts a shift from half to three-quarter occupancy. A transcription reporter might nonetheless remain unchanged if the repressor binds a nonfunctional site or polymerase initiation is buffered. Place the same motif in two promoter contexts, measure binding independently, and quantify nascent RNA rather than relying only on mature steady-state signal. A nonbinding motif mutant can show whether the measured occupancy is sequence-specific. Different promoter architectures mean identical biochemical binding can produce distinct transcription outcomes.

## 4.2 RNA polymerase elongation

### Mechanistic foundation

During elongation, polymerase traverses a DNA template and adds complementary ribonucleotides to a growing RNA chain. The enzyme can pause, backtrack and coordinate with RNA processing. Elongation rate varies with sequence, chromatin context and cellular conditions. In bacteria, coupled translation may affect transcription dynamics. Transcription-associated DNA supercoiling and its resolution influence both local progression and neighboring genomic processes.

### Specific molecular investigation

A cell line expresses a 15-kilobase transcription unit. A constant polymerase speed of 45 nucleotides per second yields a theoretical traversal time of about 5.6 minutes. If mature RNA does not peak for thirty minutes, that delay does not contradict the traversal estimate: transcript processing, termination, export, and RNA degradation may matter. Pulse-label nascent RNA in regions near the start and end, using early time points to estimate the delay between their appearance. Chromatin-associated RNA can help isolate transcription from export. A uniform constant-speed model should be rejected if different gene segments show systematic pausing or if stress changes the polymerase's residence time.

## 4.3 Termination mechanisms

### Mechanistic foundation

Transcription termination separates newly synthesized RNA from the DNA-associated transcription machinery. Bacteria can use intrinsic terminator structures or factor-dependent processes; eukaryotic RNA polymerases have distinct termination mechanisms tied to transcript class and RNA processing. Read-through beyond a terminator can change downstream regulation or RNA structure. A high termination fraction can coexist with measurable rare read-through events.

### Stepwise reasoning from molecular events to an observation

A useful molecular explanation begins by identifying the actual substrate and product in **termination mechanisms**, rather than merely naming a molecule associated with the phenomenon. The starting claim is that transcription termination separates newly synthesized RNA from the DNA-associated transcription machinery. In an active cell, production and consumption often occur simultaneously. For termination mechanisms, the distinction is especially consequential because a high termination fraction can coexist with measurable rare read-through events.



### Study design: choosing what to observe

Place reporters on either side of a candidate terminator and use matched control promoters. Sequence RNA ends to distinguish true termination sites from RNA degradation products.

A fourth condition should deliberately test an alternative explanation.

Start analysis with the limiting cases of an idealized model. When writing about termination mechanisms, state where idealization ends and empirical validation must begin.

### Discriminating causes and ruling out artifacts

Avoid assuming all transcript ends originate at a programmed termination site; cleavage, degradation and library preparation also produce ends. Build two alternative hypotheses that could mimic the predicted observation.

A meaningful negative result needs equal scrutiny. Establish the dynamic range with positive and negative controls.

### Specific molecular investigation

A terminator is inserted between two reporters, and the downstream reporter becomes dimmer. Before assigning termination efficiency, determine whether the inserted sequence also changes downstream translation, RNA stability or the promoter architecture. Map transcript 3-prime ends and look for RNA species that cross the candidate boundary. A second terminator variant with a disrupted hairpin can provide a targeted control for one proposed structure-dependent mechanism. Detection of rare read-through transcripts is informative even when most RNA ends upstream. Termination is a distribution of molecular outcomes; the absence of a downstream fluorescence signal need not mean that zero polymerases progressed across the sequence.

## 4.4 Transcriptional regulators

### Mechanistic foundation

Activators, repressors, architectural proteins and signaling-dependent factors reshape transcription through combinations of occupancy, cooperativity and access to polymerase machinery. Enhancers may act at a distance in eukaryotic genomes and cooperate with promoter-proximal elements. A transcription factor can act differently in different cell types because its partners and chromatin environment differ. Rapid transcriptional bursts create single-cell variability even when averaged measurements look smooth.

### Discriminating causes and ruling out artifacts

A meaningful negative result needs equal scrutiny. Establish the dynamic range with positive and negative controls.

### Specific molecular investigation

A steroid-responsive transcription factor binds near a candidate enhancer only in one cell type, while a second cell type carries the same DNA motif but shows no transcriptional response. The difference could reflect chromatin accessibility, missing coregulators or a distinct promoter contact. To separate these, compare factor occupancy, open-chromatin measurement, nascent transcription and protein partners in both contexts. Deleting a candidate enhancer might affect more than one promoter and can perturb local architecture. Rescue with an ectopic enhancer may not restore native genomic context. A valid mechanistic explanation identifies the necessary cellular partners rather than treating a binding motif as a complete regulatory program.

## Fully worked questions

All numerical inputs are constructed teaching examples. Where a model is conditional, that condition is part of the solution.

### Problem 1: Promoter occupancy

A single-site repressor has dissociation constant Kd=6 nM and free nuclear concentration 18 nM. Calculate its equilibrium occupancy.

**Worked reasoning**

1. For noncooperative binding, θ=L/(Kd+L)=18/(6+18)=0.75.
2. Thus 75% of sites are occupied in the simple equilibrium model.
3. It does not imply that transcription is reduced by 75%, because polymerase access and cooperative partners influence output.

### Problem 2: Transcript accumulation

Nascent RNA is made at 25 molecules per hour and removed with first-order k=0.5 h⁻¹, starting from zero. Estimate mean RNA count after 4 hours.

**Worked reasoning**

1. Solve dM/dt=s−kM: M(t)=(s/k)(1−e^(−kt)).
2. Here s/k=50 molecules and kt=2, so M(4)=50×(1−e^(−2))≈43.23 molecules.
3. The steady-state mean is 50 molecules.
4. A deterministic mean does not describe burst fluctuations in individual cells.

### Problem 3: Polymerase traversal time

An RNA polymerase crosses 15 kilobases of template at 45 nt/s in the constant-speed approximation. Estimate elongation time.

**Worked reasoning**

1. Convert 15 kb to 15,000 nt, then t=15000/45=333.3 s or 5.56 min.
2. This is traversal, not time until a mature exported mRNA appears; pausing, processing, termination and export may extend that interval.
3. Check the numerical units and domain against the quantity in polymerase traversal time; interpret the result only under the assumptions stated in the question.

## Simulations and assumptions

The two simulation cards below are local, offline-capable numerical thought experiments. They are parameterized formula demonstrations, not mechanistic claims about a real cell type. Inspect the stated assumptions first, then vary an input, compare limiting cases and reset the model to its default.

### Model focus: Single-site promoter occupancy

A receptor or DNA-binding factor with free concentration L and one independent binding site with dissociation constant Kd has equilibrium occupancy θ=L/(Kd+L). L and Kd must be measured in matching concentration units. At L=Kd the predicted occupancy is one-half; at L three times Kd it is three-quarters. The shape is saturating rather than linear. Affinity changes can shift the concentration response, yet transcription output may remain stable due to other promoter factors. The simulation reports percent occupancy, not polymerase activity or relative RNA production. A biological comparison should measure binding and nascent RNA separately, and a binding-defective motif can help test whether the factor acts at the proposed site.

### Model application and sensitivity test

Choose three free-regulator concentrations in the occupancy simulator: zero, one Kd and nine Kd. The predicted occupied fractions are zero, fifty and ninety percent. The model predicts smooth saturation because adding ever more regulator yields diminishing occupancy increases near the top of the binding curve. Suppose an RNA reporter changes abruptly over the same concentration interval. This discrepancy can suggest cooperative regulation, feedback, a threshold downstream of binding, or a separate factor that becomes limiting. A promoter without the regulator motif can test sequence dependence, while a factor-specific binding assay tests target engagement. Measure total protein and free nuclear concentration separately; the two quantities can differ due to sequestration. Avoid adjusting Kd merely to force agreement with an RNA output, because Kd describes a binding equilibrium and the reporter is the result of an entire biochemical pathway. Distinct model layers require distinct parameter measurements.

### Model focus: RNA production and decay

A deterministic RNA accumulation model combines constant synthesis s with first-order loss kM: dM/dt=s−kM. Starting from M(0)=0, the solution is M(t)=(s/k)[1−exp(−kt)]. The simulation fixes s at twenty-five RNA molecules per hour. For k=0.5 h⁻¹ and t=4 h, the mean prediction is about 43.2 molecules, approaching a steady-state mean of fifty molecules. Lower k raises the steady-state abundance but usually lengthens the approach to that new level. Single-cell measurements may be broadly distributed when transcription occurs in bursts, so this smooth mean curve cannot stand in for a cell-level time trace. Test it with a time-course that measures nascent RNA and mature RNA, and inspect whether synthesis really remains constant after induction.

### Model application and sensitivity test

Investigate the transient and steady-state effects of changing RNA loss rate while keeping synthesis fixed at twenty-five molecules per hour. At k=0.5 per hour the steady-state mean is fifty; at k=0.25 per hour it rises to one hundred, but the approach becomes slower. At one hour after induction, neither condition has reached its ultimate plateau. Record RNA counts at several early and late times and compare the models based on the entire time course, not one endpoint. The derivative at the very beginning is approximately the synthesis rate when M starts at zero, independent of k, which provides a useful limiting check. If transcription starts in bursts or one subpopulation remains silent, a single-cell dataset may have a variance much greater than this deterministic model captures. Extend the experiment by measuring nascent transcription separately from mature RNA and applying a pulse-chase strategy to test the removal constant. Different stages must not be conflated.
