# Unit 7: Gene regulation

## Unit overview

Regulatory genes do not act in isolation. DNA packaging changes the accessibility of regulatory sequence; RNA and protein products can feed back on their own production; environmental inputs alter the operating point of a network. The same circuit may show different transient and steady-state behavior. The unit uses small mathematical response curves as hypotheses and emphasizes the experiments needed to distinguish regulation at DNA accessibility, transcription initiation, RNA processing or translation. Readers should separate a persistent expression state from proof of multigenerational epigenetic inheritance.

## Learning goals

Understand the causal molecular sequence for four named processes; derive and interpret simplified quantitative statements; identify measurement controls; separate experimental observation from molecular inference; and explain the limits of the idealized simulation models.

## Unit synthesis

Gene-regulatory systems connect physical DNA packaging with dynamic networks and environmentally dependent decisions. The question is not whether a histone mark is associated with an active gene but whether a controlled perturbation of the mark changes the proposed causal pathway. A small circuit may be represented by a negative-feedback equation whose final output is easy to calculate, yet this equation does not specify the timescale on which regulation responds. Design a time-course after an environmental pulse and predict how an open-loop and a feedback-containing variant might differ. Distinguish direct perturbation effects from altered cell proportions when the whole population is sampled. To discuss epigenetic memory, define how many divisions the state must persist and whether the underlying DNA sequence remains unchanged.

## Unit-specific integrated assessment

For gene regulation, compare two abstract circuits that reach the same equilibrium reporter value but respond differently to a short stimulus. One contains negative feedback and returns toward baseline; the other has a delayed response without feedback. Learners should sketch trajectories rather than infer dynamics from steady-state endpoints. Next ask how altered chromatin accessibility could modify the input to both circuits and why an accessibility signal would not prove a causal enhancer. Add a population measurement containing two cell states to test whether an average may shift through changes in state proportions alone. The assessment ends by defining a minimal study for persistent regulatory memory across cell divisions, specifying what must be tracked and how to exclude a DNA-sequence change. It is a test of causal interpretation across time, not memorization of epigenetic terminology.

## 7.1 Chromatin and nucleosomes

### Mechanistic foundation

Eukaryotic DNA is wrapped around histone-containing nucleosomes and organized through additional interactions. Nucleosome occupancy affects the accessibility of promoters and regulatory elements, but histones also carry reversible covalent modifications associated with regulatory states. Histone variants and remodeler complexes diversify local chromatin behavior. Chromatin states are context-dependent and dynamic, not simply permanently open or closed.

The starting claim is that eukaryotic DNA is wrapped around histone-containing nucleosomes and organized through additional interactions. In an active cell, production and consumption often occur simultaneously. For chromatin and nucleosomes, the distinction is especially consequential because chromatin states are context-dependent and dynamic, not simply permanently open or closed.

A fourth condition should deliberately test an alternative explanation.

### Quantitative thinking without false precision

For chromatin and nucleosomes, distinguishing the number of molecules from the proportion of molecules in a state is often essential.

Start analysis with the limiting cases of an idealized model. When writing about chromatin and nucleosomes, state where idealization ends and empirical validation must begin.

### Discriminating causes and ruling out artifacts

Accessibility signals arise from a mixture of cell states and sequence biases. A population peak need not represent an open site in every cell. Build two alternative hypotheses that could mimic the predicted observation.

A meaningful negative result needs equal scrutiny. Establish the dynamic range with positive and negative controls.

### Specific molecular investigation

A chromatin-accessibility peak appears upstream of a gene that responds rapidly to a differentiation signal. It is tempting to call the peak an enhancer, but accessibility alone does not establish transcriptional function or target-gene identity. Sequence motifs, perturbation of the interval, and nascent RNA changes provide complementary evidence. A nucleosome may transiently occlude the site even if population profiling reports broad accessibility. Differential cell composition can also create an apparent peak without any individual cell opening its chromatin. The best experiment tracks cell state and locus-specific perturbation while avoiding assumptions about whether a peak regulates the nearest gene.

### Specific molecular investigation

Methylation near a promoter correlates with lower expression in a comparison of cell types. A causal account must consider that silencing might recruit methylation rather than methylation initiating silencing. Targeted methylation editing can test one direction, but off-target action and changing transcription-factor binding complicate interpretation. Measure the same locus through cell divisions to distinguish a transient perturbation from a persistently maintained state. Gene-body and promoter methylation can have different correlations, so the location of the modified cytosine matters. Epigenetic inheritance requires a carefully defined heritability criterion rather than a single endpoint correlation.

## 7.3 Regulatory networks and feedback

### Mechanistic foundation

Gene products regulate one another through activation, repression and interactions that form feedback and feed-forward motifs. Negative feedback often stabilizes response or reduces sensitivity; positive feedback can amplify differences and generate threshold-like behavior. Networks contain delays, nonlinearities and noise, so stability is not determined by one arrow on a pathway diagram. Graphical motifs provide hypotheses that must be connected to kinetic parameters.

### Specific molecular investigation

## 7.4 Environmental gene responses

### Mechanistic foundation

Temperature, nutrient supply, mechanical signals, toxins and cell–cell contacts alter transcription and translation through sensing pathways. A stress-response transcription factor may change many genes, but the functional outcome depends on the tissue, duration and dose. Adaptation can return an output toward baseline despite continued stimulus. Single-cell expression patterns can reveal subpopulations that are obscured in averages.

## Fully worked questions

All numerical inputs are constructed teaching examples. Where a model is conditional, that condition is part of the solution.

### Problem 1: Nucleosome accessibility

A sigmoid access proxy is A=1/(1+exp[−s(x−x0)]), with s=2 and x−x0=0.7. Find A.

**Worked reasoning**

1. Exponent argument =−2×0.7=−1.4, so A=1/(1+e^(−1.4))≈0.8022.
2. This is a mathematical switching curve; it is not a measured probability of a nucleosome moving off a site without independent calibration.
3. Check the numerical units and domain against the quantity in nucleosome accessibility; interpret the result only under the assumptions stated in the question.

### Problem 2: Fold repression

A normalized reporter reads 240 units in control cells and 80 units after a specific regulator is activated. Calculate remaining fraction and fold repression.

**Worked reasoning**

1. Remaining fraction=80/240=1/3≈0.333.
2. The ratio control/treatment=240/80=3 gives threefold repression.
3. Include background subtraction and controls for viability, cell number and reporter protein half-life before attributing the response directly to transcription.

### Problem 3: Feedback steady state

An ideal feedback law satisfies x=u/(1+ax) with input u=12 and coefficient a=0.25. Find the nonnegative steady state.

**Worked reasoning**

1. Rearrange ax²+x−u=0.
2. Insert a=0.25 and u=12: 0.25x²+x−12=0 or x²+4x−48=0.
3. Positive solution x=(−4+√208)/2≈5.211.
4. The negative mathematical root is incompatible with a nonnegative concentration-like output.
5. Stability and transient response require a dynamical model.

## Simulations and assumptions

The two simulation cards below are local, offline-capable numerical thought experiments. They are parameterized formula demonstrations, not mechanistic claims about a real cell type. Inspect the stated assumptions first, then vary an input, compare limiting cases and reset the model to its default.

### Model focus: Sigmoid chromatin access proxy

A logistic proxy for chromatin accessibility is A(x)=1/[1+exp(−sx)], where x is an abstract regulatory-state drive and s is a positive steepness coefficient. At x=0, A=0.5 for any permitted s; positive x raises the proxy and negative x lowers it. A larger s produces a sharper mathematical transition centered at zero. The curve is not a direct physical measurement of nucleosome occupancy, histone modification or enhancer activity. Chromatin profiling is influenced by sequence preference, population composition and sampling. A scientifically useful interpretation starts by verifying whether an experimentally perturbed locus shows the predicted direction and timing of change and then checking whether the associated transcript responds.

### Model application and sensitivity test

The logistic model is most sensitive to the state variable around its midpoint. At zero drive, changing steepness does not move the output from fifty percent; at a positive drive, increasing steepness makes the modeled fraction rise toward one hundred percent. For a negative drive, the same change moves it toward zero. Plot two curves with different steepness and check that they cross at the midpoint. A real accessibility assay may not have a fifty-percent physical baseline, so mapping the model output directly onto sequencing reads is unjustified. To test a candidate chromatin regulator, quantify accessibility changes at the edited locus and compare them with nascent transcription. Include neighboring genomic regions as controls and account for changes in the mixture of cell types. Distinguish a transition in the model state from a measured shift in nucleosome occupation; the curve itself only asserts a convenient nonlinear mathematical relationship.

### Model focus: Negative-feedback steady state

For the toy negative-feedback algebraic relation x=u/(1+ax), rearrangement gives ax²+x−u=0. The nonnegative solution can be written in a numerically stable form x=2u/[1+sqrt(1+4au)]. If a approaches zero, x approaches u. Increasing a while holding positive u fixed reduces the equilibrium output, although the curve provides no trajectory or response time. At input u=12 and coefficient a=0.25, the positive root is approximately 5.21. The graph is a steady-state sensitivity model; it cannot tell whether a network oscillates, adapts or exhibits hysteresis after a disturbance. Those behaviors require a dynamical equation, the relevant molecular timescales and actual perturbation data.

### Model application and sensitivity test

For negative-feedback steady state, use the equation ax²+x−u=0 with a=0.25 and u=12. Verify x≈5.21 by substitution: 5.21/(1+0.25×5.21) is not the correct test, whereas 12/(1+0.25×5.21) is approximately 5.21. If the feedback coefficient is set to zero, the output rises to the input value twelve, matching the algebraic limit. Doubling the input does not generally double steady output when feedback is present. Now introduce a hypothetical molecular delay between sensing and repression. This delay can change transient overshoot or even stability while leaving the algebraic equilibrium unchanged. To identify that behavior, record a stimulus-response time series and compare independently estimated kinetic parameters. An endpoint dose-response curve cannot distinguish fast from slow regulation when both converge to a similar final state. Steady-state and dynamic models address different questions.
