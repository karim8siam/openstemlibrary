# Unit 2: Chromosomes

## Unit overview

A chromosome is a physical carrier of hereditary information, and its different properties—centromere count, DNA mass, homologous sets and sequence content—must not be collapsed into one number. Reproduction, cell division and somatic variation introduce different routes to altered dosage. Cytogenetic descriptions often concern preparations enriched for a particular cell-cycle stage; population statements require considering tissues, selection and mosaicism. Connect each observed rearrangement to the gamete and cellular outcomes it could plausibly generate.

## Learning goals

Explain mechanisms without conflating genotype, molecular state and observable phenotype; formulate explicit conditional probabilities; solve distinct numerical examples with appropriate denominators; evaluate experimental controls; challenge an alternative interpretation; and inspect the assumptions behind each interactive model.

## 2.1 Karyotypes and ploidy

### Mechanistic foundation: Karyotypes and ploidy

Chromosomes are counted by centromeres rather than DNA mass. A replicated chromosome has two chromatids but remains one chromosome until sister chromatids separate. Ploidy describes homologous chromosome-set number, while C-value denotes DNA quantity. Human somatic reference cells are usually diploid, but cell-cycle stage, cell type and lineage can produce other configurations. Cytogenetic interpretation should therefore label cell-cycle stage and tissue rather than treating a microscopy field as an invariant genome.

For **karyotypes and ploidy**, a model written without its inheritance assumptions is not a complete explanation.

### A quantitative example with a defined denominator

A human G2 reference cell has 46 chromosomes and approximately 4C DNA, not 92 chromosomes, before chromatid separation.

## 2.2 Aneuploidy and nondisjunction

### Mechanistic foundation: Aneuploidy and nondisjunction

Nondisjunction can occur at either meiotic division. Homologs fail to segregate in meiosis I, whereas sister chromatids fail to separate in meiosis II. These errors create gametes with missing or extra chromosomes whose effects depend on chromosome and organism. Viability filters observed outcomes; the proportion of abnormal live births is not the same as the formation rate of abnormal gametes. Mitotic nondisjunction after fertilization can generate mosaic cell lineages.

For **aneuploidy and nondisjunction**, a model written without its inheritance assumptions is not a complete explanation.

### A quantitative example with a defined denominator

If a hypothetical error gives two normal gametes, one n+1 and one n−1, then the conditional abnormal fraction is 2/4, not the probability in a clinical population.

## 2.3 Copy number and dosage

### Mechanistic foundation: Copy number and dosage

Copy-number variation alters the number of copies of a DNA segment, sometimes including dosage-sensitive genes. Transcript production does not always scale linearly with copy number because feedback, dosage compensation, allele-specific regulation and cellular composition can intervene. A heterozygous deletion may reduce the number of intact templates without producing a predictable half-abundance protein outcome. Structural context also matters: a duplicated gene may be disrupted or placed next to a new regulatory element.

For **copy number and dosage**, a model written without its inheritance assumptions is not a complete explanation.

### A quantitative example with a defined denominator

For a toy dosage index D=c/2, a cell with c=3 intact locus copies has D=1.5 relative to a two-copy reference; this is not automatically 1.5-fold protein output.

## 2.4 Structural chromosome changes

### Mechanistic foundation: Structural chromosome changes

Inversions reverse orientation within a chromosome, translocations relocate sequence, deletions remove DNA, and duplications add it. A balanced rearrangement may preserve most sequence quantity but still disrupt regulatory architecture, genes or meiotic pairing. A rearrangement can suppress recovery of recombinant products in specific crosses, not because crossing over ceased but because certain recombinant chromatid products are inviable. Distinguish the cytogenetic arrangement from the transmitted gametes and offspring.

For **structural chromosome changes**, a model written without its inheritance assumptions is not a complete explanation.

## Advanced studio: tracking chromosome states through divisions

### Chromosome count is not DNA quantity

A eukaryotic chromosome is defined operationally as a centromere-associated chromosomal entity during ordinary cell-cycle accounting. This definition prevents a common counting error. A reference human somatic cell in G1 has 46 chromosomes with one chromatid each and DNA amount 2C. After S phase, the cell still has 46 chromosomes but possesses 92 sister chromatids and approximately 4C DNA. The exact biochemical mass depends on genome composition and cell type, but the doubling concept is robust in an ordinary replication cycle. During anaphase, separated chromatids become independent daughter chromosomes, briefly changing the chromosome count within the undivided cell. After cytokinesis, each ordinary diploid daughter returns to its characteristic G1 state. A chromosome number written without the cell-cycle context can therefore mislead even when the microscope counting was technically correct.

Ploidy asks a different question: how many homologous chromosome sets are present. A haploid gamete carries one set; a typical diploid somatic cell carries two. An aneuploid genome has an abnormal number of individual chromosomes without necessarily carrying an entire extra set, whereas polyploidy involves complete chromosome sets. These categories matter because the effect of changing one chromosome is often different from scaling an entire genome. In plants, whole-genome duplication can produce successful lineages; many dosage imbalances among particular human autosomes are poorly tolerated. A classroom schematic cannot infer viability from chromosome count alone.

### Separating meiosis-I and meiosis-II mistakes

Follow one homologous chromosome pair through meiosis. In meiosis I, homologs should be sent to opposite daughter cells. If both homologs travel to one pole, the products of the ensuing second division have altered chromosome count for the affected pair. In meiosis II, sister chromatids separate within each of the two cells produced by meiosis I. If separation fails in only one of those divisions, two final products may remain ordinary for the affected chromosome while the other two carry too many or too few copies. These outcomes are *conditional on a specified error* and therefore must not be confused with the probability that such an error occurs in an arbitrary reproductive event.

Imagine testing a hypothetical model in which meiosis-I nondisjunction occurs in 3% of meioses. If meiosis I is normal, meiosis-II nondisjunction occurs in 2% of the remaining meioses. Suppose every first-division error yields four abnormal gametes, while a second-division error yields two abnormal gametes out of four. The expected abnormal-gamete fraction is 0.03 + 0.97×0.02×0.5 = 0.0397. This does not incorporate which chromosome is affected, sex-specific biology, variable survival or ascertainment. It is a transparent mixture of conditional branches, not a clinical rate estimate. The interactive nondisjunction model implements exactly this hypothetical tree.

### Structural rearrangement is not merely a count

A balanced reciprocal translocation may exchange segments without changing total DNA quantity. Nevertheless, segregation of the rearranged chromosomes in meiosis can generate unbalanced gametes with duplicated and deleted regions. When a cytogenetic report says balanced, it describes copy-number balance at the resolution of the method; it does not guarantee that no gene was interrupted. An inversion similarly changes sequence order and the geometry of meiotic pairing. A crossover inside an inversion loop can produce recombinant chromatids whose survival differs by inversion type and organism. An absence of recombinant offspring may therefore reflect selection against products rather than a complete suppression of crossing-over events.

Consider a deletion spanning three genes and a separate duplication spanning the same physical coordinates. A naive dosage calculation might use a normalized index of 0.5 for one remaining copy or 1.5 for three copies in a two-copy reference. This index is DNA dosage only. It says nothing certain about which transcript isoforms are present, whether compensating regulatory feedback operates or whether the altered structural junction changes enhancer contact. To move from copy number to function, acquire gene-specific RNA data, protein abundance and a biologically relevant assay. An assay measuring aggregate DNA fluorescence is not sufficient to identify all those mechanisms.

### Designing a chromosome investigation

A sensible investigation compares microscopy, DNA-content measurement and locus-specific molecular assays. Use karyotyping or an equivalent cytogenetic method to describe large-scale structure, a validated genome-scale assay to assess segments, and targeted breakpoint evidence for a suspected inversion or translocation. Ensure that results refer to the same biological sample and report the fraction of cells showing each configuration. If 20 cells are counted and a variant arrangement appears in six, report six out of twenty with a sampling limitation; do not pretend the fraction is exact across a whole organism. When sequencing detects multiple lineages, avoid forcing the entire tissue into a single uniform karyotype. The final scientific product is a map of observed states and uncertainty, not a deterministic label.

## Further conceptual check: assay resolution and mosaics

Different chromosome methods reveal different kinds of structure. A conventional stained metaphase spread can identify many large number or morphology changes, while targeted fluorescence probes detect selected DNA regions in the cells examined. Genome sequencing can provide much finer breakpoint evidence but may struggle to reconstruct long repeats or balanced arrangements without a suitable strategy. A negative result from one assay means only that its target and sensitivity failed to detect a change; it does not establish that the entire genome has ordinary structure at every scale.

To interpret a hypothetical mosaic sample, suppose a selected tissue contains six cells with a particular altered signal among twenty independently scored cells. The observed sample proportion is 0.30. This is a sample estimate, not the fraction of every tissue in an organism. A binomial standard error would be sqrt(0.3×0.7/20), approximately 0.102, under idealized independent random sampling. Shared lineage and selective culture can make this formula optimistic. Collecting another tissue may answer a different biological question from simply counting more cells in the first preparation. Document the origin and culture history of cells before extending the inference.

## Fully worked questions

All examples below are hypothetical and contain the necessary model assumptions. Solutions are provided to make reasoning inspectable rather than turning a genetics inference into a memorized ratio.

### Problem 1: Chromosomes versus chromatids

A reference diploid human G2 cell has replicated DNA but has not separated sister chromatids. How many chromosomes and chromatids does it contain?

**Worked solution**

1. Chromosome number before anaphase is defined by centromere-bearing replicated chromosomes, not the number of DNA molecules.
2. There are 46 chromosomes in this reference diploid cell and each has two sister chromatids, yielding 46×2=92 chromatids.
3. DNA mass is approximately 4C relative to a haploid reference. The answer is a reference-cell example and excludes chromosomal variants and endoreduplication.

### Problem 2: Meiosis-II error

In a toy single meiosis-II nondisjunction event with normal meiosis I, two gametes are normal and two are aneuploid. What fraction is abnormal?

**Worked solution**

1. State the conditional setup: the error is known to have occurred in the one modeled division; the other division proceeds normally.
2. Out of four final products, two carry atypical copy number for the affected chromosome.
3. The conditional fraction is 2/4=0.5. This does not estimate how frequently meiosis-II errors occur in real organisms.

### Problem 3: Copy-number normalization

A toy assay reports three intact locus copies in a cell compared with two copies at the reference state. Estimate relative DNA dosage.

**Worked solution**

1. Define normalized locus dosage as copies in sample divided by copies in matched reference.
2. Substitute 3/2=1.5, giving 150% of the reference DNA copy number.
3. Do not convert this value directly into protein abundance without a regulatory and expression assay.
