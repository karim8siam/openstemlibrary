## Central research question

**How do fossil shells and skeletons reconstruct ancient marine communities and their environmental gradients?**

## Interpreting paleontological evidence

The unit builds a connected argument rather than a specimen catalogue. Its key themes are **shell morphology, carbonate skeletons, marine zonation, bioturbation and ichnology, time averaging in community reconstructions**. Every conclusion begins with a documented fossil, trace or associated rock unit and proceeds through a set of explicit assumptions. A fossil is not a direct recording of a living ecosystem: death, transport, burial, chemistry, uplift, erosion, collecting and taxonomic recognition all filter the evidence before it reaches a classroom. The scientific challenge is to separate the signal of past life from the processes that determined which signals survived. When several explanations fit the observations, the appropriate result is a constrained set of hypotheses rather than unwarranted certainty.

Imagine investigating the following setting: A fabricated shallow-to-deep transect has brachiopods, bivalves, echinoderm ossicles and microfossils; students distinguish habitat from transport. The first responsibility is to document material provenance, field associations and the sedimentary architecture. Then define what constitutes an independent measurement: a specimen, a shell valve, a fossil bed, a cataloged collection or a stratigraphic sample. An apparent increase in individuals can be created by longer time averaging or preservation of more skeletal fragments, and an apparent loss can arise when the rock containing a former habitat is absent. A robust field notebook therefore records negative searches, changed collecting effort, restricted access and preparation decisions alongside spectacular observations.

The chronology of a fossil occurrence is tied to the geological unit, and may depend on radiometric constraints, magnetostratigraphic information or biostratigraphic comparisons; no one method should be assumed to work in every sedimentary setting. The 2026/06 International Chronostratigraphic Chart is a dated reference, not a promise that numerical boundary ages will never change. Treat the classification of a species, the assignment of an age and the identification of a depositional setting as separately revisable judgments. An assessment that clearly states what is known, what is uncertain and what would decide among alternatives is more scientifically useful than a seemingly exhaustive list of names.

## Integrated investigation: an evidence-to-inference audit

**Research brief.** A fabricated shallow-to-deep transect has brachiopods, bivalves, echinoderm ossicles and microfossils; students distinguish habitat from transport. Design a student investigation that tests whether an observed difference reflects ancient biology or the way rocks and specimens were sampled. Begin with a map, stratigraphic table or measured section; create unique IDs, lithology fields, preservation grades, sample dates and collector identifiers. Make a distinction between information recorded directly from fossil material and information inferred by a model, and mark fictional values as synthetic at every stage.

### Recommended workflow

1. Define a focused question and mutually distinguishable predictions before any calculation. Identify the comparison units and the temporal resolution.
2. Audit original observations, including unknown and unusable specimens. Document facies changes and the risk of reworking or time averaging.
3. Examine at least two admissible analysis choices and preserve the alternative results alongside the selected result.
4. Use the provided synthetic dataset as a calculation exercise, not as evidence about a real outcrop.
5. Report a figure, an uncertainty statement, an explicit limitation, and an observation that would cause revision.
6. Record collection permissions and archive metadata, including any reason not to publish exact locality coordinates.

### Reporting and interpretation

A final report should distinguish the statement “we observed this character on this preserved element” from “this animal used this behavior” or “this group diversified at this time.” Explain the inferential bridge that connects each stage. A statistical trend in specimen counts requires assumptions about collection effort, taxonomic consistency and geological availability before it supports an evolutionary claim. Conversely, a well-preserved fossil with excellent anatomical detail can be weak evidence for local abundance if its origin or geological setting is poorly documented. A transparent limitations paragraph is an essential result, not a concession.

## Practical simulation notes

Two self-contained browser models accompany this unit: **Microfossil relative abundance** and **Two-class Simpson diversity**. Each changes one explicitly described parameter and computes a toy result using the expression provided in MODELS.json. Record values near the minimum, midpoint and maximum, and explain why the output should not be treated as a field-validated biological estimate.
