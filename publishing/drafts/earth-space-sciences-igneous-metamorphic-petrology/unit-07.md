# Unit 7 — Microtextures and quantitative metamorphic petrology

## Central question

Link thin-section evidence with models and explicit uncertainty.

## Unit outcomes and perspective

Practice petrographic description, mineral chemistry, compositional plots, pressure–temperature estimates and mass balance. The central approach is to separate measurement, interpretation, and uncertainty before assigning any historical meaning to a rock. Petrography identifies minerals, textures and overprinting relationships; geochemistry expresses chemical patterns; field mapping constrains geometry and chronology; quantitative models test whether a proposed history is physically plausible. No one method can normally reconstruct the entire history of a complex terrain. The unit therefore uses deliberately simplified calculations alongside explicit alternatives and checks.

## Prior knowledge and the logic of the lesson sequence

Recall that composition is not the same as mineral assemblage: two rocks can have similar bulk chemistry yet different minerals after differing thermal or pressure histories. Revisit the distinction between an intensive variable such as temperature or pressure and an extensive quantity such as mass or volume. When sketching diagrams, annotate axis scales and units and include the assumptions that make a boundary meaningful. For any result, ask whether equilibrium, a closed reservoir or ideal geometry was used. These assumptions should be stated before applying equations to natural rocks.

## Evidence ladder for this unit

Begin with repeatable descriptions of a specimen or synthetic dataset. Next ask which process could account for those descriptions under plausible Earth conditions. Finally test a process model against a competing explanation. Even a good process model does not automatically identify a unique geographic origin. A strong field interpretation shows where each independent line of evidence enters, what could overturn it and which uncertainty is irreducible without new sampling. Use contextual evidence from the surrounding stratigraphy and structures to prevent a microscope-scale feature from being overinterpreted.

### Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

### Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Garnet spiral trails, straight internal fabrics and truncation relationships can indicate growth and deformation overlap. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

### Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

A single apparent spiral in two-dimensional section may reflect cutting orientation rather than rotation. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Inspect sections at several orientations, compare multiple grains and test alternative three-dimensional geometries. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Use two samples at contrasting spatial scales where possible: a local thin section and a regional outcrop pattern. Agreement across scales is more informative than repeated estimates of a single proxy.
