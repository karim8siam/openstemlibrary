# Unit 1: Thermodynamic origins of transformations

**Guiding question:** Which phases can lower free energy, and why may equilibrium be delayed?

## A physical orientation for this unit

A hypothetical alloy cools through a two-phase field but looks single phase under a low-resolution optical microscope. The laboratory must decide whether this reflects equilibrium solubility, a nucleation delay or insufficient spatial resolution. A complete analysis separates the phase identities, the relevant conserved quantities, the imposed temperature–time path, and the measurable response. It is not enough to state that 'a new phase formed': the experiment must establish what is new, where it appeared, whether composition changed and whether the rate is constrained by diffusion, interface kinetics or transformation strain. This unit emphasizes **phase stability**, using a **equilibrium phase fraction** to test plausible mechanisms instead of treating a suggestive curve as proof.

Establish mass balance and phase identities using a chosen binary tie line; calculate Gibbs preference separately from interface penalties; outline a temperature–time schedule and predict what diffraction and chemistry maps should show. Before analyzing each lesson, prepare a ledger of observations, assumed boundary conditions and competing interpretations. Distinguish thermodynamic possibility from a nucleation pathway, a transformation rate and eventual property evolution. Include the thermal program and a practical measurement time resolution. Repeat a calculation at two values of a chosen input; the sensitivity often exposes whether the dominant uncertainty lies in material constants, microstructure or instrumentation.

## Vocabulary and conservation checkpoints

State the initial constituent, product state, composition basis and time origin. A phase fraction may be on a mass, volume, mole or diffraction-intensity basis, and those scales cannot be exchanged without a conversion model. State precisely whether each coefficient is a measured value, an empirical correlation, a thermodynamic state function or a synthetic teaching assumption. For this unit, pay particular attention to **chemical-potential balance** and to possible confusion from **temperature-dependent bulk free energy**. A suitable cross-check would be **a lattice identification check**.

## Lesson 1.1: Phases, components and structural identity

### Concept, boundary conditions and physical meaning

A phase is a region with uniform intensive properties and structural description at a scale of observation. A change from face-centered-cubic to body-centered-cubic symmetry can be a phase transformation even if composition is unchanged. In two-phase alloys the matrix and precipitate have distinguishable lattice structures, compositions or orders. Grain boundaries within one phase are not separate phases merely because orientations differ.

**Working relationship.** Phase identification requires structural evidence plus independent chemical or thermodynamic context. Read the terms in this relationship as quantities attached to a specific state, geometry or path. Do not assume that a convenient equation has proved the physical mechanism. For **phases, components and structural identity**, define the initial state and possible product before assigning a fraction, temperature or rate. A bulk average can conceal a sharp local concentration field, different lattice orientations or particles too small for the selected imaging method. When temperature or composition is not uniform, a single numerical parameter is at best a spatially weighted approximation; every reported number should state which part of the specimen it represents.

## Lesson 1.2: Gibbs energy, stability and metastability

### Concept, boundary conditions and physical meaning

At fixed pressure and temperature stable equilibrium minimizes an appropriate Gibbs energy subject to overall mass conservation. A metastable state can persist when a barrier separates local and global minima. The sign of a bulk free-energy difference supplies a driving force but does not directly specify transformation speed. If phases carry different compositions, chemical potentials—not simply plotted energy heights—govern exchange.

## Lesson 1.3: Chemical potential and partitioning

### Concept, boundary conditions and physical meaning

In multicomponent systems chemical potential is the partial molar Gibbs energy and expresses the cost of transferring an infinitesimal amount of a component. Two coexisting phases in full equilibrium have equal chemical potentials for every transferable species. Partition coefficients reflect phase compositions and depend on temperature and other alloying elements; they are not immutable constants. Mass conservation closes the equilibrium calculation.

**Working relationship.** f_beta=(c0-c_alpha)/(c_beta-c_alpha) for a binary tie line on a consistent composition basis. ## Lesson 1.4: Phase diagrams and invariant reactions

### Concept, boundary conditions and physical meaning

A binary phase diagram expresses equilibrium regions as functions of composition and temperature for a specified pressure. A tie line links compositions of phases in coexistence; the lever rule applies to fractions in an equilibrated two-phase region, not to arbitrary nonequilibrium micrographs. Eutectic liquid transforms to two solids, whereas eutectoid solid transforms to two other solids. Peritectic reactions involve liquid and one solid forming another solid.

**Working relationship.** The lever rule is a mass-balance relation only at the temperature and phase compositions specified by a tie line. ## Lesson 1.5: Driving forces along a thermal path

### Concept, boundary conditions and physical meaning

Cooling or heating moves the system through regions of thermodynamic preference, but the driving force is a difference in Gibbs energies evaluated at the actual temperature and composition. Near a first-order equilibrium transition an entropy discontinuity leads to an approximate linear dependence on undercooling. Path, nucleation sites and composition gradients can create hysteresis between cooling and reheating. Thermodynamic reversibility does not imply microstructural reversibility.

**Working relationship.** |Delta G_m|≈L_m (Delta T/T_eq), close to a first-order transition with approximately constant latent heat. Read the terms in this relationship as quantities attached to a specific state, geometry or path. Do not assume that a convenient equation has proved the physical mechanism. ## Integrated investigation: A metastable binary alloy

### Scientific problem and hypotheses

A hypothetical alloy cools through a two-phase field but looks single phase under a low-resolution optical microscope. The laboratory must decide whether this reflects equilibrium solubility, a nucleation delay or insufficient spatial resolution. Define a primary observable and a rival mechanism. Hypothesis A should predict the observed conversion from the intended transformation; hypothesis B should explain a similar gross signal through a competing event. Name the observations needed to prefer one. A theory that can be made to fit every possible thermal or structural trace is not adequately discriminating. Describe a negative control, a second spatial sampling location, and an independent physical modality capable of challenging the initial phase identification.

### Mathematical framework

Establish mass balance and phase identities using a chosen binary tie line; calculate Gibbs preference separately from interface penalties; outline a temperature–time schedule and predict what diffraction and chemistry maps should show. Distinguish equilibrium state functions from kinetic rate parameters, then write the relevant conservation law. If a temperature is specified, record whether it is a furnace set point, actual specimen center, interface point or surface reading. If a phase fraction is inferred, identify the normalization and its endpoint calibration. Compare output at two values using the unit's interactive models; report the numerical change, dimension and range of acceptable inputs. Where a constitutive relation is approximate, propagate rather than conceal parameter uncertainty.

A rigorous explanation should include what would happen at slow cooling compared with quenching, and explain the conditions under which both trajectories would arrive at similar or different microstructures. Include whether substitutional diffusion, interstitial transport, cooperative lattice change, or interface movement supplies the controlling timescale. If the sample contains gradients, explain how a local transformation result might differ between edge and core. The comparison should be phrased as testable predicted signatures, not a guessed dramatic micrograph.

### Measurement and validation sequence

Require two solute maps at different scales and diffraction with a reference specimen; track thermal history and explicitly state uncertainty in composition and lattice assignment. First capture the untreated material's structure and chemistry. Then document time-temperature history using two relevant temperature locations. Finally collect phase-sensitive and morphology-sensitive data at several interrupted processing states. Specify whether each observation is destructive, whether thermal cycling alters the sample, and how replicate variance will be measured. Blind a second analyst to the proposed mechanism and ask them to calculate a phase fraction or kinetic parameter from the primary traces. Record disagreements before revising interpretation.

### Claim boundaries and report requirements

The equilibrium diagram is a boundary condition, not a kinetic movie; a single end-state measurement cannot prove the route by which the state was reached. A polished final report must include: starting microstructure; phase definitions; a reaction or chemical-potential argument; a checked worked estimate; a temperature–time diagram annotated with sensor location; synthetic model graphs clearly marked as simulated; a second-method validation plan; and a concise list of conditions that would make the engineering conclusion unsafe or invalid. Numerical compatibility is a necessary implementation check but is not sufficient evidence of mechanism or service suitability. Explain precisely what further data would turn the exercise into a candidate processing procedure.
