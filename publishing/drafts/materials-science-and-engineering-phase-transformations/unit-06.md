# Unit 6: Precipitation, ordering and coarsening

**Guiding question:** How can tiny secondary phases strengthen or degrade an alloy?

## A physical orientation for this unit

A fictional precipitation-hardened alloy reaches its largest hardness at intermediate aging time but becomes vulnerable to one environmental exposure. A complete analysis separates the phase identities, the relevant conserved quantities, the imposed temperature–time path, and the measurable response. It is not enough to state that 'a new phase formed': the experiment must establish what is new, where it appeared, whether composition changed and whether the rate is constrained by diffusion, interface kinetics or transformation strain. This unit emphasizes **particle-strength evolution**, using a **a supersaturated matrix and precipitates** to test plausible mechanisms instead of treating a suggestive curve as proof.

Use a coarsening model to infer particle scale changes, compare bypass and cutting hypotheses, then define a constrained optimization across strength and corrosion. Before analyzing each lesson, prepare a ledger of observations, assumed boundary conditions and competing interpretations. Distinguish thermodynamic possibility from a nucleation pathway, a transformation rate and eventual property evolution. Include the thermal program and a practical measurement time resolution. Repeat a calculation at two values of a chosen input; the sensitivity often exposes whether the dominant uncertainty lies in material constants, microstructure or instrumentation.

## Lesson 6.1: Solution treatment, quenching and supersaturation

### Concept, boundary conditions and physical meaning

Solution heat treatment seeks a sufficiently uniform high-temperature solid solution before a quench retains excess solute beyond the equilibrium room-temperature solubility. The retained state is metastable and can decompose during later aging. Incomplete dissolution, incipient melting and quench-induced residual stress place practical limits on schedules. Quench speed must be high enough for the intended alloy yet not so severe that distortion dominates.

**Working relationship.** Supersaturation is measured relative to the equilibrium solubility at the later temperature, not relative to total alloy concentration. Read the terms in this relationship as quantities attached to a specific state, geometry or path. Do not assume that a convenient equation has proved the physical mechanism. For **solution treatment, quenching and supersaturation**, define the initial state and possible product before assigning a fraction, temperature or rate. A bulk average can conceal a sharp local concentration field, different lattice orientations or particles too small for the selected imaging method. When temperature or composition is not uniform, a single numerical parameter is at best a spatially weighted approximation; every reported number should state which part of the specimen it represents.

## Lesson 6.2: Early clustering, GP zones and metastable intermediates

### Concept, boundary conditions and physical meaning

Clustering and Guinier–Preston zones can form before equilibrium precipitates, depending on the alloy system. Metastable sequences may reduce overall free energy more quickly because interfacial or strain costs differ from the equilibrium endpoint. Diffraction, scattering and atom-probe techniques can access different aspects of this evolution. Calling every cluster an equilibrium phase misrepresents structural order and chemical partitioning.

**Working relationship.** A metastable pathway minimizes kinetic barriers as well as thermodynamic driving force; sequence is conditional on processing history. ## Lesson 6.3: Coherent cutting versus dislocation bypass

### Concept, boundary conditions and physical meaning

Small coherent particles can strengthen by forcing dislocations to shear ordered, strained or modulus-mismatched regions. With particle growth or loss of coherency, dislocations may bypass obstacles through bowing and leave Orowan loops. Strength depends on volume fraction, interparticle spacing, shape and order, not merely the count of particles per image. The transition from cutting to bypassing can explain a peak-aged condition.

**Working relationship.** Orowan-like bypass stress scales approximately with G b/lambda times geometry and logarithmic factors. ## Lesson 6.4: Ostwald ripening and coarsening

### Concept, boundary conditions and physical meaning

At fixed total secondary-phase volume fraction, high-curvature small particles can have higher chemical potential than larger particles. Material diffusion from small to large particles lowers total interface area. Classical Lifshitz–Slyozov–Wagner theory for dilute diffusion-controlled coarsening predicts a characteristic radius cubed increasing linearly with time under restrictive conditions. Real particles can coalesce, transform or be constrained by elasticity.

**Working relationship.** R³-R0³=Kt in a simplified dilute diffusion-controlled coarsening regime. Read the terms in this relationship as quantities attached to a specific state, geometry or path. Do not assume that a convenient equation has proved the physical mechanism. ## Lesson 6.5: Overaging, property changes and failure cases

### Concept, boundary conditions and physical meaning

Peak strength occurs where the precipitation state is effective at impeding slip; longer aging can coarsen particles, reduce obstacle density or alter coherency, so strength may fall. Corrosion performance, toughness, creep strength and thermal conductivity do not necessarily peak at the same aging condition. Material specifications therefore use windows of composition and thermal processing tied to intended service conditions.

**Working relationship.** A time–temperature–property map requires independently calibrated properties; fitted peak time is not an intrinsic universal constant. Read the terms in this relationship as quantities attached to a specific state, geometry or path. Do not assume that a convenient equation has proved the physical mechanism. ## Integrated investigation: An age-hardened alloy with competing requirements

### Scientific problem and hypotheses

A fictional precipitation-hardened alloy reaches its largest hardness at intermediate aging time but becomes vulnerable to one environmental exposure. Define a primary observable and a rival mechanism. Hypothesis A should predict the observed conversion from the intended transformation; hypothesis B should explain a similar gross signal through a competing event. Name the observations needed to prefer one. A theory that can be made to fit every possible thermal or structural trace is not adequately discriminating. Describe a negative control, a second spatial sampling location, and an independent physical modality capable of challenging the initial phase identification.

### Mathematical framework

Use a coarsening model to infer particle scale changes, compare bypass and cutting hypotheses, then define a constrained optimization across strength and corrosion. Distinguish equilibrium state functions from kinetic rate parameters, then write the relevant conservation law. If a temperature is specified, record whether it is a furnace set point, actual specimen center, interface point or surface reading. If a phase fraction is inferred, identify the normalization and its endpoint calibration. Compare output at two values using the unit's interactive models; report the numerical change, dimension and range of acceptable inputs. Where a constitutive relation is approximate, propagate rather than conceal parameter uncertainty.

A rigorous explanation should include what would happen at slow cooling compared with quenching, and explain the conditions under which both trajectories would arrive at similar or different microstructures. Include whether substitutional diffusion, interstitial transport, cooperative lattice change, or interface movement supplies the controlling timescale. If the sample contains gradients, explain how a local transformation result might differ between edge and core. The comparison should be phrased as testable predicted signatures, not a guessed dramatic micrograph.

### Measurement and validation sequence

Measure particle sizes using stereological rules, independent tensile properties and environmental exposure on held-out samples. First capture the untreated material's structure and chemistry. Then document time-temperature history using two relevant temperature locations. Finally collect phase-sensitive and morphology-sensitive data at several interrupted processing states. Specify whether each observation is destructive, whether thermal cycling alters the sample, and how replicate variance will be measured. Blind a second analyst to the proposed mechanism and ask them to calculate a phase fraction or kinetic parameter from the primary traces. Record disagreements before revising interpretation.

### Claim boundaries and report requirements

Peak hardness cannot be a stand-in for toughness, corrosion resistance or assured service life. A polished final report must include: starting microstructure; phase definitions; a reaction or chemical-potential argument; a checked worked estimate; a temperature–time diagram annotated with sensor location; synthetic model graphs clearly marked as simulated; a second-method validation plan; and a concise list of conditions that would make the engineering conclusion unsafe or invalid. Numerical compatibility is a necessary implementation check but is not sufficient evidence of mechanism or service suitability. Explain precisely what further data would turn the exercise into a candidate processing procedure.
