# Unit 8 — Mineral assemblages and responsible applications

## Purpose and starting assumptions

Connect mineralogical evidence to petrology, weathering, resources and responsible decisions. Mineral identities are useful only when placed in textural and spatial context. Geological interpretation requires attention to alteration, selection bias and the difference between presence, grade and commercial recoverability. This unit connects five distinct questions rather than memorizing five disconnected lists of features. The repeated aim is to move from an observation, through a process explanation, to a testable mineralogical inference. Each topic includes at least one case in which several different explanations fit the initial evidence, so that the method teaches disciplined uncertainty rather than an automatic answer key.

## A minimum practical workflow

Before naming any mineral, record a specimen code, scale, provenance status, preservation state, and whether the observation came from a single grain or a mixed aggregate. State sample preparation and instrument configuration where relevant. Identify what data would falsify the leading interpretation and whether collecting those data is reasonable and safe. Place a label of synthetic instructional data on every constructed table or reading. Never present a model-generated number as a measurement of a real mine, outcrop or economic resource.

## Learning outcome

Prepare an auditable final mineralogical investigation with alternatives, uncertainty and reporting boundaries. Students should explain how the five lessons constrain one another, complete the worked calculations with units, and defend a conclusion that reflects—not exaggerates—the strength of the data. Include a clear distinction between observational precision, formula correctness and geological accuracy. The practical exercises are intended for supervised or non-destructive study; they are not permissions for hazardous laboratory work or environmental decisions.

## Lesson 36: Mineral assemblages in igneous rocks

### Mechanism and scientific meaning

Crystallization sequence and melt composition shape igneous mineral assemblages, but actual rocks can contain accumulated crystals, xenocrysts, inherited cores and late alteration. Explain the mechanism at its appropriate scale: an atomic site, an ordered crystal, an individual grain, or a composite hand specimen. It matters whether the predicted feature belongs to a pure phase or reflects altered material surrounding it. A credible identification must distinguish the preferred phase from plausible alternatives, not merely find a familiar attribute.

### What can be observed?

Textures, mineral chemistry, phase relations and whole-rock mass balance together test igneous origins. Document instrument or test settings and the specimen identifier alongside the result; a number without measurement context can be misleading. If the phase is small, intergrown or altered, question whether the observation volume is appropriately matched to the mineral grain. Describe a feature first, then separately provide the inference that it implies.

### Case analysis with a rival explanation

**Fictional setting:** A basalt contains olivine crystals with unexpectedly Mg-rich cores and fine reaction rims.

**Different causal accounts:** Early growth, crystal accumulation, magma mixing or disequilibrium entrainment are competing scenarios.

**A proposed discrimination:** Map zoning, compare rim-melt equilibria and examine population-level grain textures.

If an observation seems to favor one account, ask what independent result would actually contradict that favored account. State whether a negative result would be decisive or might reflect detection limits, orientation or sample heterogeneity. Where the tests cannot discriminate the candidates, mark the identity unresolved rather than claiming a mineral name with unsupported certainty.

### Limitation and consequence

A simplified Bowen series is a teaching framework, not an exact thermal history of every magma. Mineralogy provides constraints on magmatic processes when integrated with petrology and geochemistry. Describe the exact claim that is supported and the more ambitious claim that must remain provisional. A result from a selected grain need not represent the host rock; a bulk assay need not identify crystal structure; and a crystallographic result need not determine geological history by itself.

### Worked quantitative example 36

**Fictional problem:** Modal estimate gives 22% olivine, 34% pyroxene and 44% plagioclase. Compute olivine+pyroxene fraction.

**Inputs:** ol = 22, px = 34. Use `ol+px` to obtain **56 %**.

**Physical interpretation:** Counting uncertainty and classification of mixed grains remain. A mathematical solution is useful only after its units, input precision and model domain are checked. Try changing one input by 10 percent and determine whether the answer varies linearly, inversely or nonlinearly; explain how the changed input would be measured in practice. ## Integrative investigation 8: An integrated mineral map and decision memo

### Research problem and scope

Teams receive a hypothetical collection of 12 sites containing igneous, metamorphic and sedimentary hand specimens. Mineralogical notes are purposely inconsistent: some sites report detailed crystal chemistry, while others supply only grain colors and rock names. Develop a minimum reporting standard and mark which site-level comparisons are invalid without more information. Propose independent confirmation tests for one ambiguous quartz-rich sandstone, one garnet-bearing metamorphic sample and one mafic assemblage with possible alteration. Do not assign a unique tectonic environment from one mineral; use stratigraphy, geochemistry, structural observations and regional data when available.

### Synthetic analysis and interpretation

The final decision memo separates (a) the inventory of observed mineral phases, (b) a geological reconstruction with alternatives, (c) resource or environmental questions that remain open, and (d) recommended low-impact follow-up. For a fictional ore body calculate contained metal from mass and head grade, explicitly refusing to label it recoverable reserve. Provide an uncertainty table ranking sampling heterogeneity, phase identification, analytical accuracy, model assumptions and economic or environmental unknowns. A blind second-team reanalysis becomes the final quality test. If disagreements remain, the group records the unresolved scope rather than creating a falsely definitive consensus.

### Practical workflow, phase 1 — sampling and the question

Start with the decision that the study is supposed to support: Prepare an auditable final mineralogical investigation with alternatives, uncertainty and reporting boundaries. Make a numbered sampling plan rather than collecting data opportunistically. For each proposed specimen list selection criteria, sampling location or teaching provenance, predicted heterogeneity, the expected spatial scale of the relevant mineral property, and how the sample will be preserved. Selection effects are part of the result. If the question concerns a trace phase, one homogenized bulk measurement can destroy the very spatial context needed to locate the phase; if the question concerns average composition, selecting only the most interesting-looking grain can severely bias the answer. Explain how the objective determines whether you prioritize hand specimen, polished section, thin section, powder, or in-situ analysis.

### Phase 2 — measurement, calibration and reproducibility

Prepare a record of instrument or classroom method, date, operator, settings, standards, detection limits and data-processing steps. Put raw observations in one table and proposed mineral names in another. Record replicates rather than silently combining them; when results disagree, ask whether the difference is repeatability, spatial heterogeneity, true chemical zoning or changing ambient conditions. The fictional dataset in `datasets/unit-08.csv` demonstrates the mechanics of a tidy table without pretending to represent a geological location. Choose one column as a measurement proxy, sketch it against sample index, and identify whether its pattern justifies a mechanistic inference. An upward trend in arbitrary proxy numbers is not evidence of a geological reaction unless calibration, spatial metadata, and a physically meaningful causal model exist.

### Phase 3 — competing explanatory models

Write a first hypothesis using the principal mechanism of this unit: Connect mineralogical evidence to petrology, weathering, resources and responsible decisions. Then write a genuinely different hypothesis consistent with the same initial observations, motivated by this difficulty: Mineral identities are useful only when placed in textural and spatial context. Geological interpretation requires attention to alteration, selection bias and the difference between presence, grade and commercial recoverability. Predict three distinct outcomes under each model. A useful discriminating observation is not merely “more data”; it is a measurement whose possible results change the relative plausibility of the competing models. Look for possible confounding by sample alteration, orientation, unrecognized intergrowths, selection effects, data reduction, or unknown provenance. If the rival models yield identical predictions at the available scale, report non-identifiability rather than assigning a fabricated confidence percentage.

### Phase 4 — quantitative reasoning with the five lesson calculations

Use the worked problems from lessons 36–40 as transparent reference calculations. For each, reproduce the numeric answer and unit, then change one input and compute a sensitivity ratio. Record where a method assumes a pure single mineral, a cubic structure, a fixed section thickness, homogeneous mixing, or other simplifications. A reasonable mathematical curve is not a calibrated natural-system model. Review whether an expression is dimensionally consistent and whether it predicts nonsensical behavior near a boundary. If so, state the domain of validity and what physical effects the equation leaves out. Keep the numerical assumptions adjacent to the output chart to prevent reuse outside their teaching context.

### Phase 5 — reporting and oral defense

Create a short abstract that states the question and the bounded conclusion. Attach specimen metadata, a simple observation matrix, a calculation table, a list of candidate phases, the diagnostic evidence, two remaining uncertainties and a prioritized next measurement. Provide raw measurements or enough metadata for an independent reanalysis; use sample identifiers that link photographs to laboratory measurements. Ask a second group to read the data without seeing the original conclusion and record whether they reach the same phase family, a different species, or a justified unresolved answer. In oral defense, identify the strongest contradicting observation and explain what would make you revise your preferred explanation. If practical environmental, engineering or economic consequences arise, clearly refer those judgments to qualified professionals and independently validated measurements.

### Laboratory questions

1. Which of the proposed tests is both least destructive and most likely to exclude a strong competitor? Justify the tradeoff.
2. What is the main difference between analytical precision, phase identification accuracy and geological representativeness in this specific case?
3. How could a small inclusion, alteration rim or unfavorable crystallographic orientation change the apparent result without changing the core mineral identity?
4. Which inference is robust to losing one of the observations? Which collapses if a single measurement proves wrong?
5. State a conclusion under three scenarios: predicted data, unexpectedly contradictory data and measurements below detection. Do not force a species name when evidence is ambiguous.

## Unit 8 synthesis and transferable habits

A mineralogical investigation should end with a table of claims rather than an unqualified list of mineral names. Distinguish observations made directly by a person or instrument from derived values computed with a model. List each candidate identification together with supporting and contradicting properties. For every contested phase, write down the independent measurement that would change the answer and whether that test is feasible, safe and appropriately calibrated. Each step should preserve the ability to revisit the original evidence when instruments or classifications improve.

Compare the five lessons by placing their central quantities on a scale ladder: atomic or unit-cell dimensions, crystalline domains, grains, hand specimens and whole-rock assemblages. A model valid on one rung cannot automatically be applied to another. Where phases have the same elements but different structures, elemental assays must be complemented by phase-sensitive measurements. Where a crystal is chemically zoned, one bulk analysis may erase the growth sequence. Where an aggregate has pores or inclusions, mass-volume density reflects the composite rather than a pure end member.

For a short written exam, choose two of the unit's computations, recreate both results, and evaluate the meaning of a plausible measurement uncertainty. Report units and assumptions before providing a final rounded value. Then construct a fresh hypothetical sample that could fool a student who learned only the most visible property. Explain why your preferred test actually discriminates between the candidates. This synthesis is intended to improve scientific reasoning rather than encourage another memorized identification rule.
