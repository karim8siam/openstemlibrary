# Unit 01: Evolutionary evidence: the historical record

> How fossils, homology, molecules and geography jointly constrain descent with modification.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, the reader should be able to investigate evolutionary evidence: the historical record, interpret a relevant quantitative model, design a realistic test that controls for alternative explanations, and state the principal limits of the inference. An intellectually satisfactory answer can remain uncertain when the data do not discriminate among mechanisms.

## Integrative foundation

### Evidence is strongest when sources of error are different

An evolutionary reconstruction is not simply a compelling illustration. It is an inference that becomes more convincing when lines of evidence based on distinct observations make compatible predictions. A fossil gives direct evidence that a particular anatomical combination existed by a particular time, subject to correct identification and stratigraphy. A sequence alignment records similarity and difference among sampled molecules, subject to identification of orthology and defensible alignment. A distribution map reflects present occurrence and survey effort, while geology can independently constrain possible routes of dispersal. The strongest historical explanations survive comparisons among these different kinds of evidence rather than forcing each dataset into one preconceived story.

Treat the distinction between *observation*, *inference* and *assumption* as the central practical skill of this unit. Observations might include the measured position of a fossil layer, a counted number of sequence differences or a field record with coordinates. Inferences include placement of a fossil on an evolutionary tree or estimation of a lineage divergence time. Assumptions include the substitution-rate model used for a clock, the completeness of a survey, and the way a morphological character is coded. A clear research report separates the three so readers can evaluate how strongly the evidence bears on the conclusion.

### Constructing a testable historical explanation

Imagine a plant lineage presently distributed across two distant mountain ranges. One story proposes that a once continuous range was split by a new lowland region. Another proposes that seeds crossed the lowland after the split. Both explanations can be plausible when written in ordinary language. To discriminate them, collect several independently inherited DNA loci from broad samples of both ranges, inspect the phylogenetic relationships, search for fossils with documented locations, and estimate dates with explicit uncertainty. An inferred split much younger than the barrier is consistent with later dispersal, although time estimates alone rarely settle the question. A split of compatible age also does not prove vicariance; continued dispersal may have occurred.

Character coding can change the result just as strongly as sample size. For a fossil skull, coding 'large eye' without defining measurement and developmental stage creates a subjective character. A normalized orbital diameter with a clear landmark and uncertainty is more reproducible. Yet a quantitative character can still be evolutionarily misleading when its distribution is shaped by ecological convergence. Fossil age provides temporal information, not a shortcut around phylogenetic analysis.

### From classroom uncertainty to scientific reporting

For every evidence claim, report the taxon or population sampling, the feature measured, the alternative hypotheses, and the source of dating or tree uncertainty. Illustrative classroom values must be labeled synthetic; they are useful for learning arithmetic but cannot serve as field evidence. A pattern that agrees with a proposed history provides support only if rival explanations have been examined. A new fossil may refine an age minimum without rewriting every relationship, and a new genome may reveal hybridization that makes a single bifurcating tree incomplete. This is normal scientific revision rather than a defect in evolutionary theory.

A useful final figure for this unit has three panels: observations on a geological time axis, a rooted phylogenetic hypothesis with confidence annotations, and a map showing samples and survey effort. Use different visual encodings for direct evidence, inferred states and hypothetical routes. This prevents a visually appealing reconstruction from quietly treating uncertain arrows as documented fact.

## Focused studies and worked problems

### 1.1 Fossils as time-ordered evidence

#### Core idea and causal mechanism

Fossils document extinct forms, their anatomical combinations, and minimum dates for the lineages they represent; stratigraphic succession provides independent evidence that biodiversity has changed through geological time.

Sediments accumulate under diverse conditions; mineral replacement or exceptional preservation can retain biological detail while radiometric dating constrains suitable layers or nearby volcanic material. A fossil's age and its position on a phylogeny are related but different claims.

#### Example in biological context

A series of early tetrapod fossils shows combinations of fish-associated fins and weight-bearing appendage features. That mosaic is more informative than an alleged single missing link, because branches may have existed simultaneously.

#### Evidence and a workable test

Build an age table from independently dated horizons, code visible features before consulting inferred tree placements, and distinguish minimum lineage age from exact origination. Compare preservational environments because hard parts and marine animals are preferentially represented.

**Investigation prompt.** Make a two-column record of chronologically grounded observations and historically inferred events. Mark minimum or maximum ages as constraints instead of exact event dates. Reexamine the proposed ordering when geological uncertainties overlap.

#### Where the inference could fail

A gap in a fossil sequence is not evidence that no organism lived during the interval. Reworked fossils, uncertain horizon assignments, and incomplete skeletons can distort the temporal argument. Never assume one sampled specimen was the direct ancestor of a later species.

A historical claim is strengthened when it predicts an observation not used to formulate it: a new region, another stratigraphic interval or a different molecular locus. Explain which corroborating observation would actually be independent.

#### Apply and question

Explain why an undated bone resembling a modern animal has less chronological value than a well-provenanced fossil with several diagnostic characters. State which observation would revise your placement and which would merely increase dating uncertainty.

Fossil interpretations connect to molecular clock calibrations: an oldest *known* fossil provides a defensible minimum for some divergence, but rarely the exact age. The uncertainty should propagate through downstream inferences.

If two species occur in adjacent layers, what additional phylogenetic evidence would be needed before suggesting ancestor-descendant relationship? Design a diagram that shows both observed strata and inferred evolutionary branching. Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

#### Quantitative case 1.1: Fossil interval duration

**Assumed data (synthetic).** An illustrative horizon spans 390 to 375 million years before present.

**Calculation.** Subtract the younger age from the older age to find elapsed deposition interval. Substituting the stipulated values into `390-375` gives **15 million years**. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

**Interpretation.** The 15-million-year interval is a dating interval, not a generation-by-generation estimate or proof of when a lineage first appeared.

**Model limitation.** If age uncertainties overlap, the exact elapsed time may be less well determined than the displayed integer suggests.

**Try next.** How would the inferred duration change if the second horizon were reassigned to 372 million years? Show the derivation and distinguish the numerical result from any proposed biological cause.


### 1.2 Homology, analogy and structural history

#### Core idea and causal mechanism

Homology is similarity inherited from a shared ancestor at a specified level of organization; analogy concerns independently evolved similar functions. A structure can be homologous as a limb while its specialized use as a wing evolves separately.

Developmental origin, topological position, detailed anatomical correspondence, and independent phylogenies inform historical inference. Selection can reshape homologous structures so strongly that their current functions diverge, while physical constraints can make unrelated organisms converge.

#### Example in biological context

The forelimb skeleton in mammals is homologous despite modifications for running, digging, gliding or swimming. Insect wings and vertebrate wings are not homologous as wings, although conserved molecular regulators may participate in very different appendages.

#### Evidence and a workable test

Prepare a character matrix that separates skeletal position, tissue origin, joint arrangement and function. Map each feature onto a tree inferred independently from genomic loci; report when the two evidence streams agree and when homoplasy is plausible.



#### Where the inference could fail

Similarity alone is not sufficient for shared ancestry, and genes reused in development do not prove entire organs share one origin. The level of comparison matters: genes, cell types, limb bones and whole wings answer distinct historical questions.



#### Apply and question

Choose two visually similar traits and predict which developmental observations would support convergence. Then choose two visibly different traits and identify a positional correspondence that could support homology.

Misclassifying analogy as homology can produce false evolutionary trees and mislead translation from model organisms to biomedical mechanisms.

Can one feature simultaneously show homology and analogy when compared at different levels? Explain using a wing, skeletal element and aerodynamic function without assuming any organism is more evolved. Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

#### Quantitative case 1.2: Homologous-character sampling

**Assumed data (synthetic).** Of 18 scored anatomical characters, 12 are confidently comparable across sampled taxa.

**Calculation.** Calculate the fraction of unambiguously comparable features. Substituting the stipulated values into `100*12/18` gives **66.6667 percent**. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

**Interpretation.** Two-thirds comparability does not measure phylogenetic confidence; informative derived states and correct coding matter more than raw overlap.

**Model limitation.** If the six ambiguous characters are systematically associated with one clade, the remaining dataset can still be biased.

**Try next.** Why would identical character percentages from two different datasets not imply equal tree support? Show the derivation and distinguish the numerical result from any proposed biological cause.


### 1.3 Molecular sequence evidence and shared descent

#### Core idea and causal mechanism

DNA and protein sequences provide many partially independent heritable characters for reconstructing relationships. Shared derived substitutions can support common ancestry even when ecological similarity is weak.

Mutations accumulate through replication and repair; recombination mixes genomic regions, and gene duplication creates paralogs. Comparing orthologous sequences with sound alignments reduces—but does not eliminate—errors arising from hidden gene history.

#### Example in biological context

A mitochondrial marker might group three sampled bird populations differently from several nuclear loci because their histories can differ through introgression or incomplete lineage sorting. More characters do not rescue an analysis that compares nonorthologous genes.

#### Evidence and a workable test

Align homologous loci, inspect ambiguous sites, estimate a model-based tree and assess branch stability with suitable resampling. Compare trees across loci instead of reporting a concatenated result as the only possible species history.



#### Where the inference could fail

Sequence similarity can be elevated by conservation, low complexity or shared compositional bias; gene trees are not automatically species trees. Missing data and uneven taxon sampling can create apparently confident but wrong relationships.



#### Apply and question

Analyze why a 98 percent match over a very short region can be less informative than a lower similarity across a validated longer ortholog. Explain what an outgroup contributes and what it cannot guarantee.

Molecular evidence supports evolutionary epidemiology, conservation genetics and identification of cryptic biodiversity, provided reference samples and provenance are reliable.

Suppose two genes favor incompatible relationships. What would you check before concluding that evolution is unresolvable? Propose tests of alignment quality, duplication, introgression and sampling bias. Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

#### Quantitative case 1.3: Pairwise DNA mismatch fraction

**Assumed data (synthetic).** A carefully aligned synthetic locus contains 18 differences over 900 unambiguous sites.

**Calculation.** Normalize mismatches by all scored sites. Substituting the stipulated values into `100*18/900` gives **2 percent**. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

**Interpretation.** The two-percent mismatch is an observed statistic rather than a molecular-clock age or species boundary.

**Model limitation.** Alignment errors, substitution saturation and inheritance history affect what the fraction means.

**Try next.** How could two alignments with the same mismatch percentage imply different histories? Show the derivation and distinguish the numerical result from any proposed biological cause.


### 1.4 Biogeography, dispersal and vicariance

#### Core idea and causal mechanism

Geographic distributions record a mixture of ancestry, movement, isolation, extinction and ecological opportunity. Islands and separated landmasses allow researchers to compare historical and ecological explanations.

A geological barrier can split an existing population through vicariance; alternatively propagules or migrants may colonize a new region and diverge. Both processes are modified by habitat filtering and the chance of detection.

#### Example in biological context

Related island birds may vary in beak geometry and food use; resemblance does not imply that adaptation alone explains colonization order. Southern-hemisphere lineages also illustrate how plate history may contribute without mechanically determining every distribution.

#### Evidence and a workable test

Compare branching order, plausible dispersal abilities, geological event dates and fossil locations. A credible historical reconstruction should test whether inferred divergence times overlap geological scenarios rather than equating every split with one tectonic event.



#### Where the inference could fail

An absence record is affected by sampling, habitat availability and local extinction. Present-day coastlines can be misleading proxies for ancient geography; rafting, winds and human movement may bypass expected barriers.



#### Apply and question

Create two rival historical scenarios for a species found on distant islands: single colonization followed by spread versus multiple independent colonizations. Identify the sampling that would discriminate them.

Biogeography helps predict invasive-species spread and set conservation units, but models built on equilibrium distributions may fail under rapid land-use change.

How would a newly found older fossil on a previously ignored continent alter a dispersal narrative? Distinguish a revised minimum age from a complete reversal of the inferred tree. Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

#### Quantitative case 1.4: Island survey detection

**Assumed data (synthetic).** True patch occupancy is assumed 0.65 and per-visit detection in an occupied patch is 0.72.

**Calculation.** Under a simplified single-visit model, multiply occupancy by conditional detection. Substituting the stipulated values into `0.65*0.72` gives **0.468 observed fraction**. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

**Interpretation.** The expected observed occupied fraction is lower than true occupancy because some present populations are missed.

**Model limitation.** Detection may depend on weather, observers and animal behavior, invalidating the constant-rate assumption.

**Try next.** What happens to observed occupancy when repeat visits improve effective detection? Show the derivation and distinguish the numerical result from any proposed biological cause.


### 1.5 Evolutionary reasoning and scientific misconceptions

#### Core idea and causal mechanism

Evolution describes changes in inherited population characteristics and diversification through descent with modification; it does not assert inevitable progress, purposeful mutation or individual genetic transformation by need.

Selection changes representation of heritable variants through differential reproduction, whereas drift changes allele frequencies by sampling chance. Individuals develop and survive; populations evolve across generations. Mutational origin, selection and inheritance must not be conflated.

#### Example in biological context

Bacteria may acquire antibiotic resistance through mutation or transferable genes before or during exposure, after which treatment changes relative survival. Exposure need not direct the mutation toward a useful outcome.

#### Evidence and a workable test

Translate a vague statement such as 'the organism evolved to survive' into measurable variables: initial variants, trait-dependent reproductive success, frequencies across time, population boundaries and alternative mechanisms.



#### Where the inference could fail

Not every trait is adaptive; constraints, drift, shared ancestry and correlated responses matter. 'Survival of the fittest' is circular unless fitness is measured independently through reproductive contribution under stated environmental conditions.



#### Apply and question

Rewrite three misleading sentences using population-level language. For each rewrite, give a feasible observation that could disprove the proposed evolutionary explanation.

Clear reasoning is essential when discussing resistance, crop breeding or biodiversity loss because verbal shortcuts can create mistaken interventions.

Could a trait become more common even when it reduces survival? Identify a combination of reproductive advantages or sampling circumstances that could explain the trend. Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

#### Quantitative case 1.5: Allele-frequency change versus individual change

**Assumed data (synthetic).** A population allele frequency increases from 0.20 to 0.28 between generations.

**Calculation.** Subtract starting allele frequency from later frequency and report percentage points. Substituting the stipulated values into `100*(0.28-0.20)` gives **8 percentage points**. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

**Interpretation.** The result is an evolutionary change in gene-copy representation; it does not tell us which selective or stochastic process caused it.

**Model limitation.** Temporal samples must represent comparable breeding populations.

**Try next.** What genetic data would separate a migration pulse from differential reproductive success? Show the derivation and distinguish the numerical result from any proposed biological cause.


## Integrated investigation 1: Triangulating an island lineage history

**Research aim.** Compare two hypothetical explanations for three related island populations: split of a widespread ancestor as sea level changed, versus repeated colonization by rafting or flight. The investigation is designed for an introductory undergraduate seminar or computational laboratory; its specimens, dates, counts and measurements are hypothetical. Do not represent any result as a published empirical discovery.

### Materials, assumptions and ethical boundaries

A fossil occurrence table with 12 dated observations and independently generated short orthologous alignments for 15 contemporary specimens. Include coordinates, survey years and explicit missing-data codes. Use a spreadsheet or local script to create tables and plots, recording pseudorandom seeds if stochastic simulations are used. Collect no personal genetic data. Do not culture pathogens, collect protected wildlife, disturb habitats or undertake medical tests. Where the real-world analog requires permits, trained staff or ethical approval, use simulation or openly available teaching data instead.

### Step-by-step procedure

1. For **triangulating an island lineage history**, define biological units, sampling frame, time coordinate and outcome variables. Write the competing hypotheses as distinct predictions: Compare two hypothetical explanations for three related island populations: split of a widespread ancestor as sea level changed, versus repeated colonization by rafting or flight. A generic statement that 'evolution occurs' is not a testable outcome.
2. Assemble the synthetic input table and verify totals. For counts, check whether each observation represents an individual, gene copy, fossil occurrence or independent lineage. For traits, specify scale and units. Leave missing observations explicitly missing rather than silently substituting zero.
3. Produce a first visualization with individual values visible when practical. Include replicates, not only a pooled mean. Label assumptions, treatment groups and what uncertainty is shown; error bars without a stated definition are ambiguous.
4. Recalculate the outcome after changing one defensible assumption: sampling effort, effective size, migration, branch rate or measurement error, as appropriate. The change in conclusion is a sensitivity result, not a failure of the exercise.
5. Write a short report separating observation, model, interpretation and limitation. Include a figure caption, a reproducibility note and one proposed independent test.

### Expected reasoning, not prescribed results

If an older fossil occurs on the proposed source island, its age can revise a minimum occupancy date but cannot alone establish a successful dispersal route. In this synthetic scenario, a simple map and a proposed tree disagree about dispersal direction when the oldest material is in a poorly sampled area. Resist presenting a single calculated value as the only outcome consistent with the mechanism. Explain what a failed prediction would require you to reconsider: an incorrect method, an unrealistic assumption or a genuinely unsupported biological hypothesis. Negative results are informative when sampling was capable of detecting the predicted difference.

### Troubleshooting the inference

Ask whether the same story could be produced by convergence, selective preservation or a different geographic sampling scheme. If the result rests on absence, quantify the effort that would have been required to detect the missing organism. Pseudoreplication is a major threat when multiple measurements from one biological unit are treated as independent organisms or populations. Similarly, sampling several fossils from one well-exposed outcrop may not represent equal coverage across geological intervals. Identify the true replication unit and justify the comparator. If simulations use the same seed or parameter configuration, discuss whether the apparently repeated outcome is truly independent.

### Submission and further questions

Submit a hypothesis table, methods summary, results figure with labeled axes, numerical worked example, explanation of assumptions and a 150-word limitations statement. How might discovery probability and geological reconstruction create the observed disagreement? For extension, describe one observation that would distinguish the leading hypothesis from a realistic competing mechanism, and state how much uncertainty would remain even if that observation agreed with the prediction.
