# Unit 07: Life-history and social evolution

> Reproductive timing, aging, sexual selection, cooperation and dispersal.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, the reader should be able to investigate life-history and social evolution, interpret a relevant quantitative model, design a realistic test that controls for alternative explanations, and state the principal limits of the inference. An intellectually satisfactory answer can remain uncertain when the data do not discriminate among mechanisms.

## Integrative foundation

### Fitness lives across an entire life cycle

Evolutionary accounts of reproduction cannot be reduced to an adult's offspring count in one season. Survival before maturity, timing of reproduction, offspring survival and future opportunities all affect expected contributions to future generations. Demographic tables organize survival l_x and fecundity m_x by age or stage. Their sum R0=Σl_xm_x gives lifetime expected production of the appropriately defined class of offspring in a stable environment, but two populations with equal R0 can grow at different calendar rates if one reproduces earlier. Life-history comparisons must therefore align age units, breeding definitions and life stages.

Trade-offs arise because growth, defense, maintenance and reproduction can draw on shared resources or introduce distinct risks. Yet an observed inverse association between two traits may be caused by ecological heterogeneity rather than an evolved allocation strategy. Individuals with high resource acquisition can exhibit both greater growth and higher reproduction, masking a real internal trade-off. Experiments and longitudinal measurements distinguish resource acquisition from allocation more effectively than one cross-sectional photograph of adults.

### Interpreting aging through selection

The force of selection often weakens at ages reached by fewer individuals or occurring after much reproduction. This does not mean that aging is always an adaptation or that repair systems deliberately fail. Antagonistic pleiotropy describes genes whose beneficial early effects may be associated with later costs, while mutation accumulation concerns variants whose effects occur late enough to be weakly selected against. Physiological mechanisms of damage and repair are separate proximate questions. Both approaches are needed to understand aging patterns across species.

Social interactions complicate a purely individual reproduction count. In some species a nonbreeding or post-reproductive individual may affect the survival or reproduction of relatives. Such effects can change inclusive fitness, but they must be measured and not assumed from family relationships alone. The simplified inequality rb>c is informative when benefit and cost are expressed in comparable fitness terms and relatedness represents suitable genetic assortment. It does not imply that every behavior benefiting a relative evolved by kin selection.

### Sexual selection and movement ecology

Mating success may depend on signals, competition and compatibility; it can favor conspicuous traits even when predators impose survival costs. Researchers should separate mating observations from genetically assigned parentage, and control for differences in territory, size and age. These principles also apply to migration and dispersal: physically moving to another patch is not the same as successful establishment, and establishment is not necessarily gene flow unless reproduction follows.

Life histories are coherent strategies only in a statistical evolutionary sense: they are sets of correlated traits shaped by selection, ancestry and chance, not conscious plans of organisms. A scientific explanation should identify age-specific benefits, costs and environmental variability, then test whether the implied reproductive consequences appear in data.

## Focused studies and worked problems

### 7.1 Age at reproduction and life-history allocation

#### Core idea and causal mechanism

Life histories involve trade-offs among growth, maintenance, reproduction and survival. Selection can favor different schedules depending on age-specific survival and fecundity, rather than maximizing lifespan alone.

Organisms allocate limited energy and time; breeding early may raise immediate offspring production but reduce later growth or survival. Density, predation and environmental predictability change the payoff of these schedules.

#### Example in biological context

Annual plants invest strongly in one reproductive season; many perennial organisms distribute reproduction across years. The classification is not a simple statement that one strategy is inherently better.

#### Evidence and a workable test

Use age-specific survival l_x and fecundity m_x to compute lifetime replacement R0=Σ l_x m_x, while noting that generation time also matters for growth rate.

**Investigation prompt.** Begin with survival before reproduction and extend through age-specific offspring production and recruitment. Avoid using a single adult observation as a proxy for lifetime fitness.

#### Where the inference could fail

A high R0 does not necessarily imply higher population growth when schedules differ. Data taken only from surviving adults may miss early mortality and bias demographic conclusions.

If cooperation is involved, separate direct fitness benefits from effects mediated through relatives. Document the assumed relatedness and the ecological circumstances that produce the estimated costs and benefits.

#### Apply and question

Construct two age-specific fecundity schedules with equal lifetime output but different timing. Predict which would grow faster under stable conditions with positive growth.

Conservation decisions for long-lived species often hinge on adult survival, while short-lived organisms may respond differently to juvenile survival changes.

Why can earlier maturation evolve even if it produces smaller adults? Discuss predation risk, reproductive timing and future survival probabilities. Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

#### Quantitative case 7.1: Net reproductive output

**Assumed data (synthetic).** A cohort has two reproductive ages with l1=0.7, m1=2 and l2=0.4, m2=3.

**Calculation.** Compute R0 as the sum of survival times fecundity across ages. Substituting the stipulated values into `0.7*2+0.4*3` gives **2.6 expected offspring per newborn**. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

**Interpretation.** The result 2.6 predicts replacement only under consistent counting of sex, birth cohort and life cycle.

**Model limitation.** Generation time is essential for converting replacement to annual growth.

**Try next.** Construct a different schedule with the same R0 but later reproduction. Show the derivation and distinguish the numerical result from any proposed biological cause.


### 7.2 Senescence and antagonistic pleiotropy

#### Core idea and causal mechanism

Aging patterns evolve in the context of how strongly selection acts at different ages. Late-acting harms may persist when early reproduction is protected or enhanced.

Mutation accumulation and antagonistic pleiotropy provide distinct routes to age-related decline, while physiological damage, repair and environmental hazards determine proximate mechanisms. Not all species exhibit identical senescence trajectories.

#### Example in biological context

A genotype boosting early reproduction could reduce later maintenance under certain resource constraints. The observation is consistent with a trade-off but requires genetic and demographic evidence.

#### Evidence and a workable test

Measure age-specific survival and reproduction in genetically controlled lines, apply cross-environment tests and consider selective disappearance of frail individuals from older cohorts.



#### Where the inference could fail

Correlation between age and mortality is not proof of a particular evolutionary theory of aging. Laboratory populations can differ strongly from wild hazards, and lifespan alone hides reproductive schedules.



#### Apply and question

Predict what would happen to selection on late-life survival if extrinsic mortality rises before most individuals reach old age. Discuss assumptions and counterexamples.

Evolutionary insights can frame comparative gerontology but do not justify direct clinical anti-aging claims for humans.

How might strong grandparental or kin effects alter the fitness consequences of survival after direct reproduction? State what would need to be measured. Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

#### Quantitative case 7.2: Early versus late fitness benefit

**Assumed data (synthetic).** A toy allele produces an early-life benefit worth 0.25 offspring and late-life cost worth 0.08 offspring at the relevant survival probabilities.

**Calculation.** Calculate net expected reproductive benefit. Substituting the stipulated values into `0.25-0.08` gives **0.17 offspring-equivalents**. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

**Interpretation.** The positive 0.17 is consistent with selection favoring the allele in this stylized example despite a later harm.

**Model limitation.** Real pleiotropy may affect relatives and multiple ages, changing the fitness currency.

**Try next.** How would increased survival into late age alter the relative importance of the cost? Show the derivation and distinguish the numerical result from any proposed biological cause.


### 7.3 Sexual selection, signals and mating systems

#### Core idea and causal mechanism

Traits can evolve because they affect mating or fertilization success, sometimes despite costs to survival. Sexual selection is a component of natural selection, not a separate non-evolutionary force.

Mate choice, same-sex competition, sperm competition and sexual conflict may change reproductive variance. Signal efficacy depends on sensory ecology and receiver preferences, not universal standards of beauty.

#### Example in biological context

A conspicuous bird ornament may correlate with mating success but also predator exposure. Female and male roles vary widely across taxa; presumed sex differences must be tested rather than assumed.

#### Evidence and a workable test

Compare reproductive success using genetic parentage where feasible, quantify signal distributions and control for condition and territory quality. Separate association from experimental evidence of preference.



#### Where the inference could fail

An ornament may be correlated with genetic quality, nutrition or age; appearance alone cannot establish costly signaling. Exaggerated traits need not be favorable in all environments.



#### Apply and question

Design a two-choice assay, define an unbiased preference statistic, and specify how side bias or prior experience would alter interpretation.

Understanding sexual selection helps explain divergence of courtship signals and some reproductive barriers, with caution against uncritical extrapolation to human behavior.

Why might a signal become rare or disappear when visual conditions change? Integrate communication effectiveness and survival cost. Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

#### Quantitative case 7.3: Mating-success skew

**Assumed data (synthetic).** In a synthetic sample one phenotype sires 18 offspring while a reference phenotype sires 12 over equal observation periods.

**Calculation.** Calculate the first type's ratio of realized reproductive output. Substituting the stipulated values into `18/12` gives **1.5 reproductive ratio**. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

**Interpretation.** The observed 1.5 ratio is descriptive; environmental confounds and uncertainty prevent immediate causal interpretation.

**Model limitation.** A few highly successful parents can lower effective population size.

**Try next.** How could parentage analysis change estimates based only on observed mating behavior? Show the derivation and distinguish the numerical result from any proposed biological cause.


### 7.4 Cooperation, kin selection and social evolution

#### Core idea and causal mechanism

Helping behaviors can evolve through several mechanisms, including indirect fitness benefits among relatives, mutual benefits and repeated interactions; a visible sacrifice is not enough to determine the mechanism.

Hamilton's rule rb>c is a simplified condition under particular assumptions: r reflects genetic assortment, b benefits to recipients, and c the cost to the actor in a compatible fitness currency.

#### Example in biological context

In social insects, workers may forego some direct reproduction while aiding relatives; relatedness alone is not a complete explanation of eusociality. Cooperative breeding also occurs among birds and mammals under varied ecological circumstances.

#### Evidence and a workable test

Measure actual reproductive output and group composition, estimate relatedness with sufficient markers, and compare partner responses through time rather than inferring benefits from behavior alone.



#### Where the inference could fail

Apparent altruism may confer immediate direct benefits, and group-level language can conceal individual-level fitness pathways. Hamilton's inequality is not a universal numerical law when interactions are nonlinear.



#### Apply and question

Calculate rb-c in a classroom toy model and ask which assumptions would change with unequal partner relatedness or density-dependent effects.

Conservation of social species requires protecting interaction networks and breeding structure, not just individual body counts.

Can cooperation spread between unrelated individuals? Provide one repeated-interaction or mutual-benefit mechanism and a condition under which it would fail. Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

#### Quantitative case 7.4: Hamilton's-rule toy estimate

**Assumed data (synthetic).** Assumed relatedness r=0.25, recipient benefit b=6 offspring-equivalents and actor cost c=1.

**Calculation.** Compute rb-c. Substituting the stipulated values into `0.25*6-1` gives **0.5 fitness-equivalents**. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

**Interpretation.** The +0.5 value satisfies the simplified rule, conditional on appropriate definitions and weak-additivity assumptions.

**Model limitation.** Direct benefits, competition among relatives and nonlinear interactions can change predictions.

**Try next.** If r decreases to 0.1, does the simple inequality still hold? Show the derivation and distinguish the numerical result from any proposed biological cause.


### 7.5 Evolution of dispersal and migration strategies

#### Core idea and causal mechanism

Dispersal changes where descendants compete and can reduce kin competition or track changing environments. Its evolution balances movement benefits against mortality and energetic costs.

Habitat predictability, dispersal cues, density dependence and colonization opportunity influence heritable movement tendencies. Migration in ecological studies and gene flow in genetics are related but not interchangeable concepts.

#### Example in biological context

Winged seed dispersal and seasonal movement of animals are very different mechanisms sharing consequences for spatial population structure. A disperser that arrives but fails to reproduce has not necessarily contributed gene flow.

#### Evidence and a workable test

Compare marked individual movements with parentage-based estimates of breeding dispersal, and model distances and survival with detection uncertainty.



#### Where the inference could fail

Observed movement may reflect plastic response rather than genetic evolution. A migration corridor can be used by many individuals without restoring breeding connectivity between populations.



#### Apply and question

Propose a simple fitness accounting of natal survival versus moving to a vacant patch. Add a condition in which dispersal success falls despite more movement.

Habitat fragmentation makes dispersal evolution consequential for connectivity, rescue and invasive spread.

Why can both strong dispersal and strong site fidelity be favored in different landscapes? Connect temporal variability with the risk of unfamiliar habitat. Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

#### Quantitative case 7.5: Dispersal success probability

**Assumed data (synthetic).** A hypothetical disperser survives travel with probability 0.60 and establishes in the destination with conditional probability 0.40.

**Calculation.** Multiply conditional probabilities of survival and establishment. Substituting the stipulated values into `0.60*0.40` gives **0.24 probability**. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

**Interpretation.** The 0.24 probability is of successful establishment, not necessarily future reproduction or gene flow.

**Model limitation.** Environment and density affect establishment probabilities independently of travel.

**Try next.** How does the result change if establishment drops to 0.2 after fragmentation? Show the derivation and distinguish the numerical result from any proposed biological cause.


## Integrated investigation 7: Life tables, cooperation and reproductive timing

**Research aim.** Construct two hypothetical cohort schedules with equal lifetime expected reproduction but different ages of first breeding; add an optional social-help scenario. The investigation is designed for an introductory undergraduate seminar or computational laboratory; its specimens, dates, counts and measurements are hypothetical. Do not represent any result as a published empirical discovery.

### Materials, assumptions and ethical boundaries

Age-specific survival and fecundity tables for two populations, an assumed relatedness estimate with uncertainty and measured hypothetical effect on survival of relatives. Use a spreadsheet or local script to create tables and plots, recording pseudorandom seeds if stochastic simulations are used. Collect no personal genetic data. Do not culture pathogens, collect protected wildlife, disturb habitats or undertake medical tests. Where the real-world analog requires permits, trained staff or ethical approval, use simulation or openly available teaching data instead.

### Step-by-step procedure

1. For **life tables, cooperation and reproductive timing**, define biological units, sampling frame, time coordinate and outcome variables. Write the competing hypotheses as distinct predictions: Construct two hypothetical cohort schedules with equal lifetime expected reproduction but different ages of first breeding; add an optional social-help scenario. A generic statement that 'evolution occurs' is not a testable outcome.
2. Assemble the synthetic input table and verify totals. For counts, check whether each observation represents an individual, gene copy, fossil occurrence or independent lineage. For traits, specify scale and units. Leave missing observations explicitly missing rather than silently substituting zero.
3. Produce a first visualization with individual values visible when practical. Include replicates, not only a pooled mean. Label assumptions, treatment groups and what uncertainty is shown; error bars without a stated definition are ambiguous.
4. Recalculate the outcome after changing one defensible assumption: sampling effort, effective size, migration, branch rate or measurement error, as appropriate. The change in conclusion is a sensitivity result, not a failure of the exercise.
5. Write a short report separating observation, model, interpretation and limitation. Include a figure caption, a reproducibility note and one proposed independent test.

### Expected reasoning, not prescribed results

R0 alone does not determine calendar growth; inclusive-fitness comparisons require costs and benefits in compatible reproductive currency. In this synthetic scenario, a social helper raises the number of juvenile survivors but temporarily reduces personal fecundity. Resist presenting a single calculated value as the only outcome consistent with the mechanism. Explain what a failed prediction would require you to reconsider: an incorrect method, an unrealistic assumption or a genuinely unsupported biological hypothesis. Negative results are informative when sampling was capable of detecting the predicted difference.

### Troubleshooting the inference

A behavioral account should identify whether the fitness benefit is direct, arises through relatives, or depends on repeated reciprocation. A visible helping act is not evidence for a unique evolutionary mechanism. Pseudoreplication is a major threat when multiple measurements from one biological unit are treated as independent organisms or populations. Similarly, sampling several fossils from one well-exposed outcrop may not represent equal coverage across geological intervals. Identify the true replication unit and justify the comparator. If simulations use the same seed or parameter configuration, discuss whether the apparently repeated outcome is truly independent.

### Submission and further questions

Submit a hypothesis table, methods summary, results figure with labeled axes, numerical worked example, explanation of assumptions and a 150-word limitations statement. What information is needed to decide whether the behavior can spread under a simple kin-selection model? For extension, describe one observation that would distinguish the leading hypothesis from a realistic competing mechanism, and state how much uncertainty would remain even if that observation agreed with the prediction.
