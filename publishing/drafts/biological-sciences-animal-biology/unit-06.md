# Unit 06: Feeding, respiration, circulation and homeostasis

Physical constraints on transport and exchange interact with the evolutionary diversity of metabolic strategies.

**Unit pathway.** This unit examines animal feeding systems and nutrient acquisition, then respiratory exchange across water and air, circulatory diversity and transport limits, osmoregulation, excretion and nitrogen waste and temperature, size scaling and metabolic budgets. Study how the anatomy and ecology in each case constrain the testable mechanism rather than memorizing one explanation for all animal groups.

## Concepts, cases and methods

### 6.1 Animal feeding systems and nutrient acquisition

**Core biological account.** Feeding adaptations range from suspension capture and grazing to predation and symbiosis; gut architecture is influenced by diet composition and residence time.

**Mechanistic explanation.** Mechanical fragmentation increases available surface area, enzymes catalyze macromolecular hydrolysis, and gut-associated microbes can change the fraction of resources accessible to hosts.

**Comparison with other animal groups.** Ruminant foregut fermentation, rabbit hindgut fermentation and carnivore digestion illustrate alternative arrangements, not simply one better digestive system.

**A discriminating observation.** Construct a mass balance for food intake, egested material and assimilated energy, verifying that energy units and observation intervals agree.

**Limits of the inference.** Apparent digestibility includes microbial transformations and endogenous losses; two diets with equal calories need not support equal growth or health.

**Ecological significance.** Food-web models require distinguishing ingestion from assimilation to avoid overstating energetic transfer between trophic levels.

### 6.2 Respiratory exchange across water and air

**Core biological account.** Respiratory surfaces trade high area and short diffusion path lengths against desiccation risk, mechanical support and movement of surrounding fluid.

**Mechanistic explanation.** Countercurrent exchange can preserve gradients across fish gills; air-breathing tetrapods use diverse ventilation mechanisms, and insect tracheae deliver gases along different routes.

**Comparison with other animal groups.** Fish gills, mammal alveoli and insect tracheoles illustrate solutions adapted to different partial-pressure environments and body architectures.

**A discriminating observation.** Record oxygen uptake at graded environmental oxygen levels while separately measuring ventilation or water flow, with safe stopping criteria.

**Limits of the inference.** The concentration of oxygen in water differs from its partial pressure and availability; ignoring temperature and salinity confounds field comparisons.

**Ecological significance.** Aquatic warming and deoxygenation can jointly constrain active animals even where habitat area appears unchanged.

### 6.3 Circulatory diversity and transport limits

**Core biological account.** Diffusion alone works over short distances, but larger active bodies often require circulatory transport for respiratory gases, nutrients and signaling molecules.

**Mechanistic explanation.** Closed vessels can maintain directed high-pressure flow, while open circulatory arrangements bathe tissues in hemolymph; heart structure and fluid properties coevolve with demand.

**Comparison with other animal groups.** An annelid's closed circulation differs from many arthropod systems, while vertebrate fishes and mammals have different arrangements of cardiac and respiratory flow.

**A discriminating observation.** Use flow rate and oxygen content difference to estimate tissue oxygen delivery, then test sensitivity to hematocrit or extraction assumptions.

**Limits of the inference.** A closed circulation is not automatically more efficient in every ecological context; energy costs, pressures and respiratory carrier identity matter.

**Ecological significance.** Environmental stress can be detected by reduced aerobic scope, but heart rate alone is an imperfect proxy for oxygen transport.

### 6.4 Osmoregulation, excretion and nitrogen waste

**Core biological account.** Animal cells require regulation of water, salts and nitrogenous waste even when environmental osmolarity or food composition changes quickly.

**Mechanistic explanation.** Nephrons, metanephridia, Malpighian tubules and other excretory structures use selective transport and fluid processing in distinct ways; ammonia, urea and uric acid differ in handling costs.

**Comparison with other animal groups.** Marine teleosts lose water osmotically and drink seawater, while freshwater fishes tend to gain water and produce dilute urine; desert insects conserve water using different strategies.

**A discriminating observation.** Compare changes in body mass and excreted solute after a salinity shift and maintain a no-change control; distinguish osmotic water movement from drinking behavior.

**Limits of the inference.** Urine concentration is not a direct universal measure of water conservation when excretion rates differ; transport direction depends on the full chemical potential context.

**Ecological significance.** Changing salinity or pollution can disrupt ion homeostasis before obvious mortality, so physiological indicators may detect stress earlier than population counts.

### 6.5 Temperature, size scaling and metabolic budgets

**Core biological account.** Metabolic demand depends on body size, temperature, activity, life stage and evolutionary lineage, and simple allometric laws are approximations rather than universal identities.

**Mechanistic explanation.** Temperature affects reaction kinetics and membrane properties, while body shape changes exchange surface relative to volume; animals combine behavior and physiology to manage heat gains and losses.

**Comparison with other animal groups.** A large tuna maintains high sustained activity with specialized tissues, whereas a small terrestrial ectotherm may rely strongly on microhabitat choice for heating and cooling.

**A discriminating observation.** Fit a log–log regression of metabolic rate on mass with confidence intervals and separate within-species from across-species comparisons.

**Limits of the inference.** A fitted exponent is not an immutable biological constant; standardized fasting state, season and phylogenetic nonindependence change the estimate.

**Ecological significance.** Energy budgets clarify why habitat fragmentation, urban heat islands and disrupted food supply interact in animal survival.
