# Unit 5: Sequential Hypothesis Testing: Wald's SPRT, Stopping Boundaries, OC & ASN Functions

In classical Neyman-Pearson hypothesis testing, the sample size $n$ is treated as a pre-determined deterministic constant fixed prior to data collection. Once the experimental design specifies $n$, the statistician must observe all $n$ realizations $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ before computing the test statistic and rendering a terminal decision between the null hypothesis $H_0$ and the alternative hypothesis $H_1$. While mathematically tractable, this fixed-sample paradigm suffers from a profound operational inefficiency: when the true state of nature lies far in the interior of either the null or alternative regime, the accumulated evidence often becomes overwhelmingly decisive after only a small fraction of the planned observations have been gathered. Continuing to sample under such circumstances wastes precious experimental resources, inflates financial costs, and—in clinical trials or industrial destructive testing—exposes human subjects to inferior treatments or destroys valuable manufactured components unnecessarily.

Sequential analysis, pioneered by Abraham Wald during the Second World War under the auspices of the Columbia University Statistical Research Group (and initially classified as restricted military research due to its immense value in naval ordnance and munitions quality control), fundamentally alters this paradigm by treating the sample size $N$ as a **random stopping time** adapted to the natural filtration of the incoming observations. After each individual observation $X_m$ is recorded at stage $m = 1, 2, \dots$, the investigator evaluates the cumulative evidence and chooses among three mutually exclusive actions: (1) stop sampling immediately and accept $H_0$, (2) stop sampling immediately and reject $H_0$ in favor of $H_1$, or (3) continue sampling by drawing an additional observation $X_{m+1}$. In this unit, we develop the rigorous mathematical foundations of Wald's Sequential Probability Ratio Test (SPRT), derive the fundamental stopping inequalities and boundary approximations, prove almost-sure finite termination via Stein's Lemma, establish Wald's Fundamental Identity in sequential analysis, construct Operating Characteristic (OC) and Average Sample Number (ASN) functions across canonical parametric families, and prove the celebrated Wald-Wolfowitz optimality theorem.

## 5.1 Sequential Sampling Paradigms and Wald's Sequential Probability Ratio Test (SPRT)

To formalize sequential hypothesis testing within measure-theoretic probability, let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be an infinite sequence of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables defined on a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P}_\theta)$, where each $X_i$ possesses probability density function (or probability mass function in the discrete case) $f(x; \theta)$ with respect to a $\sigma$-finite dominating measure $\mu$. Let $\{\mathcal{F}_m\}_{m \ge 1}$ denote the natural filtration generated by the observation sequence, where $\mathcal{F}_m = \sigma(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_m)$ represents the cumulative information available up to and including stage $m$, with $\mathcal{F}_0 = \{\emptyset, \Omega\}$ representing the trivial initial $\sigma$-algebra prior to sampling.

A sequential test is formally defined as an ordered pair $(N, \delta_N)$ consisting of two distinct mathematical components:
1. **A Stopping Rule (Stopping Time) $N$**: A random variable $N : \Omega \to \{0, 1, 2, \dots\} \cup \{\infty\}$ is a valid stopping time with respect to the filtration $\{\mathcal{F}_m\}$ if, for every non-negative integer $m \ge 0$, the event $\{N = m\}$ belongs to $\mathcal{F}_m$. Equivalently, the indicator variable $\mathbb{I}(N \le m)$ is a measurable function of $(X_1, \dots, X_m)$ alone. This measurability condition guarantees that the decision to terminate sampling at step $m$ depends strictly on the historical data observed up to step $m$ and involves no clairvoyant foresight of future unrealized observations $X_{m+1}, X_{m+2}, \dots$.
2. **A Terminal Decision Rule $\delta_N$**: A sequence of measurable decision functions $\{\delta_m(X_1, \dots, X_m)\}_{m \ge 1}$, where each $\delta_m : \mathbb{R}^m \to \{0, 1\}$ maps the observed $m$-dimensional sample path into a binary decision upon termination ($0$ denoting acceptance of $H_0$, and $1$ denoting rejection of $H_0$ in favor of $H_1$).

Consider testing a simple null hypothesis $H_0 : \theta = \theta_0$ against a simple alternative hypothesis $H_1 : \theta = \theta_1$, where $\theta_0 \ne \theta_1$. Denote the null density by $f_0(x) = f(x; \theta_0)$ and the alternative density by $f_1(x) = f(x; \theta_1)$. At any fixed stage $m$, the Neyman-Pearson Fundamental Lemma dictates that the most powerful test is governed by the cumulative likelihood ratio:

$$\Lambda_m(X_1, \dots, X_m) = \frac{L_m(\theta_1)}{L_m(\theta_0)} = \prod_{i=1}^m \frac{f_1(X_i)}{f_0(X_i)}$$

By taking the natural logarithm of $\Lambda_m$, the multiplicative likelihood ratio transforms into an additive random walk. Specifically, if we define the single-observation log-likelihood ratio increment (or weight of evidence) as:

$$Z_i = \ln \left[ \frac{f_1(X_i)}{f_0(X_i)} \right], \quad i = 1, 2, \dots$$

then the cumulative log-likelihood ratio at stage $m$ is simply the partial sum of i.i.d. random variables:

$$S_m = \ln \Lambda_m = \sum_{i=1}^m Z_i = S_{m-1} + Z_i, \quad \text{with } S_0 = 0$$

Abraham Wald's **Sequential Probability Ratio Test (SPRT)** with stopping boundaries $(B, A)$, where $0 < B < 1 < A < \infty$, operates as follows. At each sampling stage $m = 1, 2, \dots$, we compute $\Lambda_m$ (or equivalently $S_m = \ln \Lambda_m$ against the logarithmic thresholds $b = \ln B < 0 < a = \ln A$) and execute one of three rules:
- **Accept $H_0$**: If $\Lambda_m \le B$ (equivalently, $S_m \le b = \ln B$), terminate the sampling process immediately at step $N = m$ and decide in favor of $H_0$ ($\delta_N = 0$).
- **Reject $H_0$ (Accept $H_1$)**: If $\Lambda_m \ge A$ (equivalently, $S_m \ge a = \ln A$), terminate the sampling process immediately at step $N = m$ and decide in favor of $H_1$ ($\delta_N = 1$).
- **Continue Sampling**: If $B < \Lambda_m < A$ (equivalently, $b < S_m < a$), neither hypothesis has accumulated sufficient evidentiary weight; draw observation $X_{m+1}$ and advance to stage $m+1$.

Mathematically, the stopping time $N$ of Wald's SPRT is the first exit time of the discrete-time random walk $\{S_m\}_{m \ge 1}$ from the open continuation interval $(b, a)$:

$$N = \inf \left\{ m \ge 1 : \Lambda_m \notin (B, A) \right\} = \inf \left\{ m \ge 1 : S_m \le b \text{ or } S_m \ge a \right\}$$

This formulation reveals a deep structural bridge between statistical inference and stochastic processes: executing an SPRT is isomorphic to solving the classic **Gambler's Ruin** or two-barrier first-passage problem for a one-dimensional random walk with drift $\mu_Z(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\theta[Z_i]$. Under the null hypothesis $H_0$, by Jensen's inequality applied to the strictly concave logarithm function (assuming $\mathbb{P}_{\theta_0}(f_1(X) \ne f_0(X)) > 0$), the expected increment is strictly negative:

$$\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[Z_i] = \mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}\left[\ln \frac{f_1(X_i)}{f_0(X_i)}\right] < \ln \mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}\left[\frac{f_1(X_i)}{f_0(X_i)}\right] = \ln \int f_1(x) \, d\mu(x) = \ln(1) = 0$$

Notice that $\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[Z_i] = -D_{\text{KL}}(f_0 \parallel f_1) < 0$, where $D_{\text{KL}}(f_0 \parallel f_1)$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence of $f_1$ from $f_0$. Conversely, under $H_1$, $\mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[Z_i] = +D_{\text{KL}}(f_1 \parallel f_0) > 0$. Thus, when $H_0$ is true, the random walk $S_m$ exhibits negative drift downward toward the lower absorption barrier $b = \ln B$; when $H_1$ is true, $S_m$ exhibits positive drift upward toward the upper absorption barrier $a = \ln A$.

## 5.2 Fundamental Stopping Inequalities and Wald's Boundary Approximations

In practical statistical design, an investigator specifies the desired Type I error probability $\alpha = \mathbb{P}_{\theta_0}(\text{Reject } H_0)$ and Type II error probability $\beta = \mathbb{P}_{\theta_1}(\text{Accept } H_0)$ in advance, and seeks the exact numerical values of the stopping thresholds $A$ and $B$ that achieve $(\alpha, \beta)$. Unlike fixed-sample tests where exact quantile inversion of a single distribution suffices, exact determination of $A$ and $B$ in a discrete-time random walk requires tracking the distribution of the excess over the boundary at termination. Nevertheless, Abraham Wald discovered a pair of universal, distribution-free inequalities linking $(A, B)$ and $(\alpha, \beta)$ that hold across all probability distributions without any parametric assumptions.

Let $\mathcal{R}_m \subset \mathbb{R}^m$ denote the subset of sample paths $(x_1, \dots, x_m)$ for which the SPRT terminates at stage $N = m$ with rejection of $H_0$ ($\Lambda_m \ge A$), and let $\mathcal{A}_m \subset \mathbb{R}^m$ denote the subset of sample paths for which the SPRT terminates at stage $N = m$ with acceptance of $H_0$ ($\Lambda_m \le B$). Because the stopping events at distinct stages $m = 1, 2, \dots$ are mutually exclusive (a path can stop for the first time at most once), the overall probability of rejecting $H_0$ when $\theta = \theta_1$ is the countable sum of disjoint stage-wise rejection probabilities. Assuming the test terminates with probability one ($\mathbb{P}_\theta(N < \infty) = 1$, which we prove rigorously in Section 5.3), the probability of rejecting $H_0$ under $H_1$ is $1 - \beta$, while the probability of rejecting $H_0$ under $H_0$ is $\alpha$.

### Theorem 5.1 (Wald's Fundamental Stopping Boundary Inequalities)
For any Sequential Probability Ratio Test with stopping boundaries $(B, A)$ that terminates with probability one under both $H_0$ and $H_1$, the true Type I error probability $\alpha$ and Type II error probability $\beta$ satisfy the exact inequalities:

$$A \le \frac{1 - \beta}{\alpha} \quad \text{and} \quad B \ge \frac{\beta}{1 - \alpha}$$

 Equivalently, solving for the error rates in terms of the boundaries yields:

$$\alpha \le \frac{1 - \beta}{A} < \frac{1}{A} \quad \text{and} \quad \beta \le (1 - \alpha) B < B$$

Furthermore, the sum of the realized error probabilities if one sets the boundaries to the nominal Wald values $A_W = (1 - \beta_0)/\alpha_0$ and $B_W = \beta_0/(1 - \alpha_0)$ satisfies $\alpha + \beta \le \alpha_0 + \beta_0$.

#### Proof:
Consider the event of rejecting $H_0$, which is the disjoint union $\bigcup_{m=1}^\infty \mathcal{R}_m$. On every sample path $(x_1, \dots, x_m) \in \mathcal{R}_m$, the stopping rule requires that the likelihood ratio at termination satisfies $\Lambda_m(x_1, \dots, x_m) \ge A$, which is equivalent to the pointwise density inequality:

$$\prod_{i=1}^m f_1(x_i) \ge A \prod_{i=1}^m f_0(x_i) \quad \text{for all } (x_1, \dots, x_m) \in \mathcal{R}_m$$

Integrating (or summing) both sides of this inequality over the rejection region $\mathcal{R}_m$ with respect to the product measure $d\mu^m$ and summing over all stages $m = 1, 2, \dots, \infty$, we obtain:

$$1 - \beta = \mathbb{P}_{\theta_1}(\text{Reject } H_0) = \sum_{m=1}^\infty \int_{\mathcal{R}_m} \left( \prod_{i=1}^m f_1(x_i) \right) d\mu^m \ge \sum_{m=1}^\infty \int_{\mathcal{R}_m} A \left( \prod_{i=1}^m f_0(x_i) \right) d\mu^m = A \sum_{m=1}^\infty \mathbb{P}_{\theta_0}(\mathcal{R}_m) = A \alpha$$

Dividing both sides of $1 - \beta \ge A \alpha$ by $\alpha > 0$ immediately yields the upper bound $A \le \frac{1 - \beta}{\alpha}$.

By completely symmetric reasoning, consider the event of accepting $H_0$, which is the disjoint union $\bigcup_{m=1}^\infty \mathcal{A}_m$. On every sample path $(x_1, \dots, x_m) \in \mathcal{A}_m$, termination at the lower boundary requires $\Lambda_m(x_1, \dots, x_m) \le B$, or equivalently:

$$\prod_{i=1}^m f_1(x_i) \le B \prod_{i=1}^m f_0(x_i) \quad \text{for all } (x_1, \dots, x_m) \in \mathcal{A}_m$$

Integrating over $\mathcal{A}_m$ and summing from $m = 1$ to $\infty$ yields:

$$\beta = \mathbb{P}_{\theta_1}(\text{Accept } H_0) = \sum_{m=1}^\infty \int_{\mathcal{A}_m} \left( \prod_{i=1}^m f_1(x_i) \right) d\mu^m \le B \sum_{m=1}^\infty \int_{\mathcal{A}_m} \left( \prod_{i=1}^m f_0(x_i) \right) d\mu^m = B \mathbb{P}_{\theta_0}(\text{Accept } H_0) = B(1 - \alpha)$$

Dividing by $1 - \alpha > 0$ establishes $B \ge \frac{\beta}{1 - \alpha}$.

Now suppose an investigator specifies target nominal error rates $(\alpha_0, \beta_0)$ and sets the boundaries using **Wald's Boundary Approximations**:

$$A \approx A_W = \frac{1 - \beta_0}{\alpha_0}, \qquad B \approx B_W = \frac{\beta_0}{1 - \alpha_0}$$

Let $\alpha_{\text{true}}$ and $\beta_{\text{true}}$ denote the actual Type I and Type II error probabilities induced by these boundaries $(B_W, A_W)$. Applying the two inequalities proven above to $(\alpha_{\text{true}}, \beta_{\text{true}})$ gives:

$$\frac{\alpha_{\text{true}}}{1 - \beta_{\text{true}}} \le \frac{1}{A_W} = \frac{\alpha_0}{1 - \beta_0} \implies \alpha_{\text{true}}(1 - \beta_0) \le \alpha_0(1 - \beta_{\text{true}})$$

$$\frac{\beta_{\text{true}}}{1 - \alpha_{\text{true}}} \le B_W = \frac{\beta_0}{1 - \alpha_0} \implies \beta_{\text{true}}(1 - \alpha_0) \le \beta_0(1 - \alpha_{\text{true}})$$

Adding these two cross-multiplied inequalities together yields:

$$\alpha_{\text{true}} - \alpha_{\text{true}}\beta_0 + \beta_{\text{true}} - \beta_{\text{true}}\alpha_0 \le \alpha_0 - \alpha_0\beta_{\text{true}} + \beta_0 - \beta_0\alpha_{\text{true}}$$

Canceling the identical cross-product terms $-\alpha_{\text{true}}\beta_0 - \beta_{\text{true}}\alpha_0$ from both sides produces the celebrated conservation bound:

$$\alpha_{\text{true}} + \beta_{\text{true}} \le \alpha_0 + \beta_0$$

This proves that at least one of the true error probabilities $\alpha_{\text{true}}$ or $\beta_{\text{true}}$ must be less than or equal to its nominal target, and neither error rate can exceed its target by more than a negligible fraction $\alpha_0 \beta_0$: specifically, $\alpha_{\text{true}} \le \frac{\alpha_0}{1 - \beta_0} \approx \alpha_0(1 + \beta_0)$ and $\beta_{\text{true}} \le \frac{\beta_0}{1 - \alpha_0} \approx \beta_0(1 + \alpha_0)$. For instance, if $\alpha_0 = 0.05$ and $\beta_0 = 0.05$, then $\alpha_{\text{true}} \le 0.05/0.95 = 0.0526$.

Why are Wald's inequalities strict inequalities in discrete time rather than exact equalities? Because the random walk $S_m$ evolves in discrete steps $Z_m$, termination at stage $N$ almost always involves **overshooting the boundary** (the excess over the boundary): at the moment of rejection, $S_N$ does not hit $a = \ln A$ exactly on the nose, but rather jumps from $S_{N-1} < a$ to $S_N = a + R_+ > a$, where $R_+ = S_N - a > 0$ is the overshoot. In the continuous-time limit where individual increments $Z_i$ have infinitesimal variance (as when $\theta_1$ is close to $\theta_0$, leading to Brownian motion sample paths with continuous trajectories), the overshoot $R_+$ vanishes identically, and Wald's approximations $A = (1-\beta)/\alpha$ and $B = \beta/(1-\alpha)$ become exact equalities. Due to this slight overshoot in discrete time, using $(B_W, A_W)$ actually produces a slightly **conservative** test whose true error probabilities $\alpha_{\text{true}}$ and $\beta_{\text{true}}$ are typically smaller than $\alpha_0$ and $\beta_0$, at the slight expense of increasing the expected sample size by one or two observations.

## 5.3 Proof of Finite Termination with Probability One via Stein's Lemma

A foundational prerequisite for any sequential procedure is that it must eventually terminate: if there were a positive probability $\mathbb{P}_\theta(N = \infty) > 0$ that the random walk $S_m$ wandered forever inside the open strip $(b, a)$ without ever crossing either threshold, the experiment could continue indefinitely. Charles Stein (1946) proved a remarkably strong theorem showing not only that $\mathbb{P}_\theta(N < \infty) = 1$ for every distribution where $Z_i$ is not identically zero, but also that the stopping time $N$ possesses an **exponentially decaying tail**, guaranteeing that all moments $\mathbb{E}_\theta[N^k]$ ($k = 1, 2, \dots$) and the moment generating function of $N$ around zero are strictly finite.

### Theorem 5.2 (Stein's Lemma on Exponential Tail Decay and Almost-Sure Termination of the SPRT)
Let $Z_1, Z_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. random variables under probability measure $\mathbb{P}_\theta$ such that $\mathbb{P}_\theta(Z_1 = 0) < 1$. Let $N = \inf\{m \ge 1 : S_m \notin (b, a)\}$ be the stopping time of an SPRT with finite logarithmic boundaries $-\infty < b < 0 < a < \infty$. Then:
1. There exist constants $C > 0$ and $\rho \in (0, 1)$ such that for all integers $n \ge 1$:
   $$\mathbb{P}_\theta(N > n) \le C \rho^n$$
2. Consequently, the SPRT terminates in a finite number of steps with probability one:
   $$\mathbb{P}_\theta(N < \infty) = 1$$
3. All positive integer moments of the stopping time are finite: $\mathbb{E}_\theta[N^k] < \infty$ for all $k \ge 1$, and there exists $t_0 > 0$ such that the moment generating function $\mathbb{E}_\theta[e^{tN}] < \infty$ for all $t < t_0$.

#### Proof:
Let $L = a - b > 0$ denote the total width of the open continuation interval $(b, a)$. Because $\mathbb{P}_\theta(Z_1 = 0) < 1$, the distribution of $Z_1$ is non-degenerate at zero; hence either $\mathbb{P}_\theta(Z_1 > 0) > 0$ or $\mathbb{P}_\theta(Z_1 < 0) > 0$ (or both). Without loss of generality, suppose $\mathbb{P}_\theta(Z_1 > 0) > 0$. Then there exists a small positive threshold $\delta > 0$ and a strictly positive probability $\epsilon > 0$ such that:

$$\mathbb{P}_\theta(Z_1 \ge \delta) = \epsilon > 0$$

Choose a fixed positive integer $r$ sufficiently large so that $r \delta > L = a - b$; specifically, let $r = \lfloor (a - b)/\delta \rfloor + 1$. Consider a block of $r$ consecutive increments $Z_{j+1}, Z_{j+2}, \dots, Z_{j+r}$ starting after any arbitrary stage $j \ge 0$. If each of these $r$ independent increments satisfies $Z_{j+i} \ge \delta$ for $i = 1, \dots, r$, then their block sum satisfies:

$$\sum_{i=1}^r Z_{j+i} \ge r \delta > a - b = L$$

Because $Z_{j+1}, \dots, Z_{j+r}$ are independent and identically distributed, the probability of this block event is strictly positive:

$$p_{\text{block}} = \mathbb{P}_\theta\left( \sum_{i=1}^r Z_{j+i} > a - b \right) \ge \mathbb{P}_\theta\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^r \{Z_{j+i} \ge \delta\} \right) = \prod_{i=1}^r \mathbb{P}_\theta(Z_{j+i} \ge \delta) = \epsilon^r > 0$$

Now observe that if the random walk is inside the continuation strip $(b, a)$ at stage $j$ (so $b < S_j < a$), and the subsequent $r$-step block sum exceeds $a - b$, then at stage $j + r$ the cumulative sum must satisfy:

$$S_{j+r} = S_j + \sum_{i=1}^r Z_{j+i} > b + (a - b) = a$$

Therefore, it is **mathematically impossible** for both $S_j \in (b, a)$ and $S_{j+r} \in (b, a)$ to occur simultaneously when $\sum_{i=1}^r Z_{j+i} > a - b$! Contrapositively, for the process to remain inside the continuation strip throughout the entire block from $j+1$ to $j+r$, the block sum cannot exceed $a - b$:

$$\mathbb{P}_\theta(N > j + r \mid N > j) \le \mathbb{P}_\theta\left( \sum_{i=1}^r Z_{j+i} \le a - b \right) = 1 - p_{\text{block}} \le 1 - \epsilon^r < 1$$

Now consider any arbitrary sample size $n \ge 1$, and divide the first $n$ observations into $k = \lfloor n / r \rfloor$ disjoint, independent blocks of length $r$ each. For the stopping time $N$ to exceed $n$, the random walk $S_m$ must remain strictly inside $(b, a)$ at the end of every single block $m = r, 2r, \dots, kr$. By the independence of disjoint blocks of i.i.d. increments:

$$\mathbb{P}_\theta(N > n) \le \mathbb{P}_\theta(N > kr) \le \prod_{m=1}^k \mathbb{P}_\theta\left( \sum_{i=1}^r Z_{(m-1)r + i} \le a - b \right) \le (1 - \epsilon^r)^k$$

Since $k = \lfloor n / r \rfloor > \frac{n}{r} - 1$, we can write $(1 - \epsilon^r)^k \le (1 - \epsilon^r)^{-1} \left[ (1 - \epsilon^r)^{1/r} \right]^n$. Defining the constants $C = (1 - \epsilon^r)^{-1} > 0$ and $\rho = (1 - \epsilon^r)^{1/r} \in (0, 1)$, we obtain the geometric tail bound:

$$\mathbb{P}_\theta(N > n) \le C \rho^n \quad \text{for all } n \ge 1$$

Taking the limit as $n \to \infty$, since $0 < \rho < 1$:

$$\mathbb{P}_\theta(N = \infty) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{P}_\theta(N > n) \le \lim_{n \to \infty} C \rho^n = 0 \implies \mathbb{P}_\theta(N < \infty) = 1$$

Finally, for any $t > 0$ such that $e^t \rho < 1$ (i.e., $t < t_0 = -\ln \rho > 0$), the moment generating function of $N$ is bounded by a convergent geometric series:

$$\mathbb{E}_\theta[e^{tN}] = \sum_{n=1}^\infty e^{tn} \mathbb{P}_\theta(N = n) \le \sum_{n=1}^\infty e^{tn} \mathbb{P}_\theta(N > n - 1) \le \frac{C}{\rho} \sum_{n=1}^\infty (e^t \rho)^n < \infty$$

Since the moment generating function $\mathbb{E}_\theta[e^{tN}]$ is finite in a neighborhood of $t = 0$, all moments $\mathbb{E}_\theta[N^k]$ are finite. This completes the proof of Stein's Lemma.

## 5.4 Wald's Fundamental Identity and the Operating Characteristic (OC) Curve

To evaluate the performance of a sequential test across the entire parameter space $\Theta$—not merely at the two design points $\theta_0$ and $\theta_1$—we require the **Operating Characteristic (OC) function**, denoted $L(\theta)$, which gives the probability of accepting the null hypothesis $H_0$ as a function of the true parameter $\theta$:

$$L(\theta) = \mathbb{P}_\theta(\text{SPRT accepts } H_0) = \mathbb{P}_\theta(S_N \le b)$$

Note that the power function of the sequential test is simply the complement of the OC function: $\text{Power}(\theta) = 1 - L(\theta) = \mathbb{P}_\theta(S_N \ge a)$. To compute $L(\theta)$ analytically, Abraham Wald proved one of the most powerful martingale identities in stochastic processes: **Wald's Fundamental Identity** in sequential analysis.

Let $M_\theta(t) = \mathbb{E}_\theta[e^{t Z_1}]$ denote the moment generating function (MGF) of a single log-likelihood ratio increment $Z_1 = \ln[f_1(X_1)/f_0(X_1)]$ when the true parameter is $\theta$. Define the discrete-time stochastic process $\{W_m(t)\}_{m \ge 0}$ by:

$$W_m(t) = \exp(t S_m) \left[ M_\theta(t) \right]^{-m}, \quad \text{with } W_0(t) = 1$$

Observe that $\{W_m(t)\}_{m \ge 0}$ is a positive **martingale** with respect to the filtration $\{\mathcal{F}_m\}$, because for every $m \ge 0$:

$$\mathbb{E}_\theta[W_{m+1}(t) \mid \mathcal{F}_m] = \mathbb{E}_\theta\left[ W_m(t) e^{t Z_{m+1}} [M_\theta(t)]^{-1} \;\middle|\; \mathcal{F}_m \right] = W_m(t) [M_\theta(t)]^{-1} \mathbb{E}_\theta[e^{t Z_{m+1}}] = W_m(t)$$

By Doob's Optional Stopping Theorem ( combined with Stein's exponential tail bound on $N$ and the uniform boundedness of $S_{m \wedge N}$ inside $(b, a)$ prior to stopping), the expected value of the martingale at the stopping time $N$ equals its initial value $W_0(t) = 1$.

### Theorem 5.3 (Wald's Fundamental Identity of Sequential Analysis)
Let $Z_1, Z_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. increments with moment generating function $M_\theta(t) = \mathbb{E}_\theta[e^{t Z_1}]$ finite in an open interval containing $t$, and let $N$ be the stopping time of an SPRT with finite boundaries $(b, a)$. Then for all $t$ in the domain of $M_\theta(t)$ such that $|M_\theta(t)| \ge 1$ (or within a neighborhood of the roots of $M_\theta(t) = 1$):

$$\mathbb{E}_\theta \left[ \exp(t S_N) \left( M_\theta(t) \right)^{-N} \right] = 1$$

How does Wald's Fundamental Identity unlock the Operating Characteristic function $L(\theta)$? Suppose that for a given parameter value $\theta$ (with $\mathbb{E}_\theta[Z_1] \ne 0$), we can find a **non-zero real root** $t = h(\theta) \ne 0$ of the characteristic equation:

$$M_\theta(h(\theta)) = \mathbb{E}_\theta \left[ e^{h(\theta) Z_1} \right] = \mathbb{E}_\theta \left[ \left( \frac{f_1(X_1)}{f_0(X_1)} \right)^{h(\theta)} \right] = 1$$

Because $M_\theta(t)$ is strictly convex ($M_\theta''(t) = \mathbb{E}_\theta[Z_1^2 e^{t Z_1}] > 0$), satisfies $M_\theta(0) = 1$, and has derivative $M_\theta'(0) = \mathbb{E}_\theta[Z_1] \ne 0$ at the origin, convexity guarantees that there exists at most one non-zero real root $h(\theta) \ne 0$ such that $M_\theta(h(\theta)) = 1$ (specifically, if $\mathbb{E}_\theta[Z_1] < 0$, then $h(\theta) > 0$; if $\mathbb{E}_\theta[Z_1] > 0$, then $h(\theta) < 0$). Notice that at the two design parameters $\theta_0$ and $\theta_1$, we can verify the root $h(\theta)$ by direct inspection:
- When $\theta = \theta_0$: $\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}\left[\left(\frac{f_1(X)}{f_0(X)}\right)^1\right] = \int \frac{f_1(x)}{f_0(x)} f_0(x) \, d\mu(x) = \int f_1(x) \, d\mu(x) = 1$, so **$h(\theta_0) = +1$**.
- When $\theta = \theta_1$: $\mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}\left[\left(\frac{f_1(X)}{f_0(X)}\right)^{-1}\right] = \int \frac{f_0(x)}{f_1(x)} f_1(x) \, d\mu(x) = \int f_0(x) \, d\mu(x) = 1$, so **$h(\theta_1) = -1$**.

Now substitute $t = h(\theta) \ne 0$ into Wald's Fundamental Identity. Since $M_\theta(h(\theta)) = 1$, the random factor $[M_\theta(h(\theta))]^{-N} = 1^{-N} = 1$ drops out completely, leaving the remarkably clean identity:

$$\mathbb{E}_\theta \left[ \exp\left( h(\theta) S_N \right) \right] = 1 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \mathbb{E}_\theta \left[ \left( \Lambda_N \right)^{h(\theta)} \right] = 1$$

Splitting this expectation across the two mutually exclusive terminal events—acceptance of $H_0$ ($S_N \le b$, which occurs with probability $L(\theta)$) and rejection of $H_0$ ($S_N \ge a$, which occurs with probability $1 - L(\theta)$)—and neglecting the boundary overshoot (so that $S_N \approx b = \ln B$ upon acceptance and $S_N \approx a = \ln A$ upon rejection), we obtain:

$$L(\theta) B^{h(\theta)} + \left[ 1 - L(\theta) \right] A^{h(\theta)} \approx 1$$

Solving this linear equation for $L(\theta)$ gives **Wald's Operating Characteristic (OC) Formula**:

$$L(\theta) \approx \frac{A^{h(\theta)} - 1}{A^{h(\theta)} - B^{h(\theta)}} = \frac{e^{a h(\theta)} - 1}{e^{a h(\theta)} - e^{b h(\theta)}}, \quad \text{for } h(\theta) \ne 0$$

What happens at the singular intermediate parameter value $\theta = \theta^*$ where the drift is zero ($\mathbb{E}_{\theta^*}[Z_1] = 0$, which corresponds to the confluent root $h(\theta^*) = 0$)? Applying L'Hôpital's rule to the OC formula as $h \to 0$:

$$L(\theta^*) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{A^h - 1}{A^h - B^h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{A^h \ln A}{A^h \ln A - B^h \ln B} = \frac{\ln A}{\ln A - \ln B} = \frac{a}{a - b} = \frac{a}{a + |b|}$$

This provides a complete parametric representation of the OC curve $(\theta(h), L(h))$ across the entire real line!

## 5.5 Average Sample Number (ASN) Function and Parametric Derivations

In sequential analysis, the sample size $N$ is a random variable whose expectation $\mathbb{E}_\theta[N]$ is called the **Average Sample Number (ASN) function**. Just as the OC curve characterizes the statistical accuracy of the SPRT across $\theta$, the ASN curve $\mathbb{E}_\theta[N]$ characterizes its sampling efficiency (expected cost or duration) as a function of the true parameter $\theta$. To derive a closed-form expression for $\mathbb{E}_\theta[N]$, we differentiate Wald's Fundamental Identity with respect to $t$ at $t = 0$ (or equivalently apply Wald's First Equation for stopped random walks).

### Theorem 5.4 (Wald's Equation and the ASN Function)
Let $Z_1, Z_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. random variables with finite mean $\mathbb{E}_\theta[Z_1]$ and finite variance $\text{Var}_\theta(Z_1)$, and let $N$ be a stopping time with $\mathbb{E}_\theta[N] < \infty$. Then:
1. **Wald's First Equation (Non-zero Drift, $\mathbb{E}_\theta[Z_1] \ne 0$)**:
   $$\mathbb{E}_\theta[S_N] = \mathbb{E}_\theta[N] \cdot \mathbb{E}_\theta[Z_1] \quad \implies \quad \mathbb{E}_\theta[N] = \frac{\mathbb{E}_\theta[S_N]}{\mathbb{E}_\theta[Z_1]}$$
2. **Wald's Second Equation (Zero Drift, $\mathbb{E}_{\theta^*}[Z_1] = 0$)**:
   $$\mathbb{E}_{\theta^*}\left[ S_N^2 \right] = \mathbb{E}_{\theta^*}[N] \cdot \mathbb{E}_{\theta^*}\left[ Z_1^2 \right] \quad \implies \quad \mathbb{E}_{\theta^*}[N] = \frac{\mathbb{E}_{\theta^*}[S_N^2]}{\mathbb{E}_{\theta^*}[Z_1^2]}$$

#### Proof via Differentiation of Wald's Fundamental Identity:
Differentiating $\mathbb{E}_\theta\left[ e^{t S_N} (M_\theta(t))^{-N} \right] = 1$ with respect to $t$ under the expectation sign (justified by Stein's exponential tail bound) gives:

$$\mathbb{E}_\theta \left[ S_N e^{t S_N} (M_\theta(t))^{-N} - N e^{t S_N} (M_\theta(t))^{-N-1} M_\theta'(t) \right] = 0$$

Evaluating at $t = 0$, where $M_\theta(0) = 1$ and $M_\theta'(0) = \mathbb{E}_\theta[Z_1]$, immediately yields $\mathbb{E}_\theta[S_N - N \mathbb{E}_\theta[Z_1]] = 0$, proving Wald's First Equation $\mathbb{E}_\theta[S_N] = \mathbb{E}_\theta[N]\mathbb{E}_\theta[Z_1]$. If $\mathbb{E}_{\theta^*}[Z_1] = 0$, differentiating a second time at $t = 0$ where $M_{\theta^*}'(0) = 0$ and $M_{\theta^*}''(0) = \mathbb{E}_{\theta^*}[Z_1^2]$ yields $\mathbb{E}_{\theta^*}[S_N^2 - N \mathbb{E}_{\theta^*}[Z_1^2]] = 0$, establishing Wald's Second Equation.

Under Wald's small-overshoot approximation, the terminal log-likelihood ratio $S_N$ is approximated as a binary random variable taking value $b = \ln B$ with probability $L(\theta)$ and value $a = \ln A$ with probability $1 - L(\theta)$. Thus:

$$\mathbb{E}_\theta[S_N] \approx L(\theta) \ln B + \left[ 1 - L(\theta) \right] \ln A = L(\theta) b + \left[ 1 - L(\theta) \right] a$$

Substituting this into Wald's First and Second Equations yields the universal **ASN Formula**:

$$\mathbb{E}_\theta[N] \approx \begin{cases} \dfrac{L(\theta) \ln B + [1 - L(\theta)] \ln A}{\mathbb{E}_\theta[Z_1]} & \text{if } \mathbb{E}_\theta[Z_1] \ne 0 \\[12pt] \dfrac{-\ln A \ln B}{\mathbb{E}_{\theta^*}[Z_1^2]} = \dfrac{|b| a}{\text{Var}_{\theta^*}(Z_1)} & \text{if } \mathbb{E}_{\theta^*}[Z_1] = 0 \end{cases}$$

In particular, at the null hypothesis $\theta = \theta_0$ where $L(\theta_0) = 1 - \alpha$, and at the alternative hypothesis $\theta = \theta_1$ where $L(\theta_1) = \beta$:

$$\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[N] \approx \frac{(1 - \alpha) \ln B + \alpha \ln A}{\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[Z_1]}, \qquad \mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[N] \approx \frac{\beta \ln B + (1 - \beta) \ln A}{\mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[Z_1]}$$

Notice the geometric shape of the ASN curve $\mathbb{E}_\theta[N]$ as a function of $\theta$: it is low at $\theta_0$ and $\theta_1$ (and approaches $1$ as $\theta \to \pm \infty$ where drift is huge), but achieves its **maximum peak** near the zero-drift indifference point $\theta = \theta^*$ where $\mathbb{E}_{\theta^*}[Z_1] = 0$. At $\theta^*$, the random walk has no drift and leaves $(b, a)$ purely by diffusive fluctuations, scaling proportionally to $|b|a / \sigma_Z^2$.

Let us now catalog the exact log-likelihood increment $Z_i$, non-zero root condition $h(\theta)$, and sequential decision boundaries for the four canonical parametric families:

1. **Normal Mean with Known Variance**: $X_i \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\theta, \sigma^2)$ for testing $H_0 : \theta = \theta_0$ vs. $H_1 : \theta = \theta_1$ ($\theta_1 > \theta_0$).
   - Log-likelihood increment: $Z_i = \frac{\theta_1 - \theta_0}{\sigma^2} X_i - \frac{\theta_1^2 - \theta_0^2}{2\sigma^2} = \frac{\theta_1 - \theta_0}{\sigma^2} \left( X_i - \frac{\theta_0 + \theta_1}{2} \right)$.
   - Drift and variance: $\mathbb{E}_\theta[Z_i] = \frac{\theta_1 - \theta_0}{\sigma^2} \left( \theta - \frac{\theta_0 + \theta_1}{2} \right)$, $\text{Var}_\theta(Z_i) = \frac{(\theta_1 - \theta_0)^2}{\sigma^2}$. Zero-drift midpoint: $\theta^* = \frac{\theta_0 + \theta_1}{2}$.
   - Explicit root $h(\theta)$: Since $Z_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_Z, \sigma_Z^2)$, its MGF is $\exp(t \mu_Z + \frac{1}{2} t^2 \sigma_Z^2) = 1$, which gives the exact closed-form root $h(\theta) = -\frac{2\mu_Z}{\sigma_Z^2} = \frac{\theta_0 + \theta_1 - 2\theta}{\theta_1 - \theta_0}$.

2. **Bernoulli Proportion (Binomial Sequential Sampling)**: $X_i \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \text{Bernoulli}(p)$ for testing $H_0 : p = p_0$ vs. $H_1 : p = p_1$ ($0 < p_0 < p_1 < 1$).
   - Log-likelihood increment: $Z_i = X_i \ln\left(\frac{p_1(1 - p_0)}{p_0(1 - p_1)}\right) + \ln\left(\frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_0}\right)$.
   - Expected increment: $\mathbb{E}_p[Z_i] = p \ln\left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right) + (1 - p) \ln\left(\frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_0}\right)$.
   - Characteristic root equation: $M_p(h) = p \left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right)^h + (1 - p) \left(\frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_0}\right)^h = 1$, which yields the explicit parametric expression for $p$ as a function of $h$:
     $$p(h) = \frac{1 - \left(\frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_0}\right)^h}{\left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right)^h - \left(\frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_0}\right)^h}$$

3. **Poisson Rate**: $X_i \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \text{Poisson}(\lambda)$ for testing $H_0 : \lambda = \lambda_0$ vs. $H_1 : \lambda = \lambda_1$ ($\lambda_1 > \lambda_0$).
   - Log-likelihood increment: $Z_i = X_i \ln\left(\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_0}\right) - (\lambda_1 - \lambda_0)$.
   - Expected increment: $\mathbb{E}_\lambda[Z_i] = \lambda \ln\left(\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_0}\right) - (\lambda_1 - \lambda_0)$, with zero-drift point $\lambda^* = \frac{\lambda_1 - \lambda_0}{\ln(\lambda_1 / \lambda_0)}$.
   - Parametric root relation: $\lambda(h) = \frac{h(\lambda_1 - \lambda_0)}{(\lambda_1 / \lambda_0)^h - 1}$.

4. **Exponential Scale/Rate**: $X_i \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \text{Exponential}(\theta)$ with density $f(x; \theta) = \theta e^{-\theta x}$ ($x > 0$) for testing $H_0 : \theta = \theta_0$ vs. $H_1 : \theta = \theta_1$ ($\theta_1 > \theta_0$).
   - Log-likelihood increment: $Z_i = \ln\left(\frac{\theta_1}{\theta_0}\right) - (\theta_1 - \theta_0) X_i$.
   - Expected increment: $\mathbb{E}_\theta[Z_i] = \ln\left(\frac{\theta_1}{\theta_0}\right) - \frac{\theta_1 - \theta_0}{\theta}$.

## 5.6 The Wald-Wolfowitz Optimality Theorem and Truncated Sequential Designs

Why did Abraham Wald select constant horizontal boundaries $(b, a)$ for the cumulative log-likelihood ratio $S_m$ rather than curved or time-varying boundaries $(b_m, a_m)$? In 1948, Abraham Wald and Jacob Wolfowitz proved one of the deepest theorems in mathematical statistics: among **all** sequential and fixed-sample tests whose error probabilities do not exceed those of the SPRT, the SPRT simultaneously minimizes the expected sample size under **both** $H_0$ and $H_1$!

### Theorem 5.5 (Wald-Wolfowitz Optimality Theorem for the SPRT)
Let $\mathcal{T}^*$ be a Sequential Probability Ratio Test with stopping time $N^*$ and terminal decision $\delta^*$ having error probabilities $\alpha(\mathcal{T}^*) = \alpha$ and $\beta(\mathcal{T}^*) = \beta$. Let $\mathcal{T} = (N, \delta)$ be any other sequential or fixed-sample test such that:

$$\alpha(\mathcal{T}) \le \alpha \quad \text{and} \quad \beta(\mathcal{T}) \le \beta$$

Then the expected sample size of $\mathcal{T}^*$ is simultaneous smaller than or equal to that of $\mathcal{T}$ under both hypotheses:

$$\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[N^*] \le \mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[N] \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[N^*] \le \mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[N]$$

#### Sketch of Proof via Bayesian Sequential Decision Theory and Kullback-Leibler Divergence:
Consider a Bayesian sequential decision problem where $\theta \in \{\theta_0, \theta_1\}$ is assigned prior probability $\pi = \mathbb{P}(\theta = \theta_1) \in (0, 1)$, let $c > 0$ be the cost per observation, and let $w_0, w_1 > 0$ be the terminal losses for Type I and Type II errors. The total Bayes risk of any test $\mathcal{T} = (N, \delta)$ is:

$$R(\pi, \mathcal{T}) = (1 - \pi)\left( w_0 \alpha(\mathcal{T}) + c \mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[N] \right) + \pi\left( w_1 \beta(\mathcal{T}) + c \mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[N] \right)$$

By backward induction and Bellman's optimality equation for Markov decision processes, since the posterior probability $\pi_m = \mathbb{P}(\theta = \theta_1 \mid X_1, \dots, X_m)$ is a monotone function of the likelihood ratio $\Lambda_m$ via Bayes' rule:

$$\frac{\pi_m}{1 - \pi_m} = \frac{\pi}{1 - \pi} \Lambda_m$$

and the future observation dynamics are time-homogeneous, the optimal Bayes continuation region in terms of $\pi_m$ is a time-invariant open interval $(\pi_L, \pi_U)$, which transforms via Bayes' rule into a time-invariant interval $B < \Lambda_m < A$, i.e., an SPRT! By varying the prior $\pi$ and loss weights $(w_0, w_1)$ to match the given boundaries $(B, A)$, if any competing test $\mathcal{T}$ had $\alpha(\mathcal{T}) \le \alpha(\mathcal{T}^*)$, $\beta(\mathcal{T}) \le \beta(\mathcal{T}^*)$, and strictly smaller $\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[N] < \mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[N^*]$ (or $\mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[N] < \mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[N^*]$), it would achieve a strictly smaller Bayes risk than the Bayes-optimal SPRT $\mathcal{T}^*$, which is a contradiction.

We can also derive a universal **Information-Theoretic Lower Bound** on $\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[N]$ and $\mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[N]$ directly from Wald's Equation and the data-processing inequality for Kullback-Leibler divergence. Note that $\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[Z_1] = -D_{\text{KL}}(f_0 \parallel f_1)$. Applying Jensen's inequality conditionally on the terminal decision $\delta_N \in \{0, 1\}$ to $\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[S_N] = \mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[\ln \Lambda_N]$ yields:

$$-\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[S_N] = \mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[N] D_{\text{KL}}(f_0 \parallel f_1) \ge (1 - \alpha) \ln\left(\frac{1 - \alpha}{\beta}\right) + \alpha \ln\left(\frac{\alpha}{1 - \beta}\right) = \text{kl}(1 - \alpha \parallel \beta)$$

Thus, for **any** test $\mathcal{T} = (N, \delta)$ with error probabilities $(\alpha, \beta)$:

$$\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[N] \ge \frac{(1 - \alpha) \ln\left(\frac{1 - \alpha}{\beta}\right) + \alpha \ln\left(\frac{\alpha}{1 - \beta}\right)}{D_{\text{KL}}(f_0 \parallel f_1)}, \qquad \mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[N] \ge \frac{(1 - \beta) \ln\left(\frac{1 - \beta}{\alpha}\right) + \beta \ln\left(\frac{\beta}{1 - \alpha}\right)}{D_{\text{KL}}(f_1 \parallel f_0)}$$

Comparing the numerator of this universal lower bound with Wald's ASN formula at $\theta_0$ and $\theta_1$ using $\ln A \approx \ln\left(\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha}\right)$ and $\ln B \approx \ln\left(\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}\right)$, we see that **Wald's SPRT attains the exact information-theoretic lower bound** up to the negligible boundary overshoot!

In clinical trials and industrial applications, one practical concern with the open-ended SPRT is that if the true parameter happens to lie near the indifference point $\theta^*$, the sample size $N$, though finite with probability one, has a positively skewed distribution and may occasionally exceed the fixed-sample size $n_{\text{fixed}}$. Furthermore, budgeting and hospital logistics require a hard upper bound $N_{\max}$ on the number of patients enrolled. This motivates **Truncated Sequential Designs** and **Group Sequential Designs**:
- **Wald's Truncated SPRT**: If the SPRT has not terminated by a pre-specified maximum stage $N_{\max}$, sampling is forcibly stopped at $N_{\max}$, and $H_0$ is accepted if $S_{N_{\max}} \le 0$ and rejected if $S_{N_{\max}} > 0$.
- **Group Sequential Clinical Trials (Pocock, O'Brien-Fleming, and Lan-DeMets $\alpha$-Spending Boundaries)**: Rather than monitoring after every single patient, data are analyzed at $K$ interim looks (e.g., $K = 4$ or $5$ stages of $m$ patients each). Pocock (1977) proposed constant standardized $Z$-thresholds across all looks, whereas O'Brien and Fleming (1979) proposed conservative early thresholds $c_k = c_{\text{OBF}} \sqrt{K/k}$ that make early stopping very stringent unless the treatment effect is dramatic, while preserving a final critical value $c_K$ nearly identical to the fixed-sample test.

## Worked Questions and Solutions

### Question 1: Sequential Probability Ratio Test for a Normal Mean—Boundaries, OC Curve, ASN, and Efficiency Gains
A semiconductor wafer fabrication plant monitors the oxide layer thickness $X$ (in nanometers) of silicon wafers, where $X_i \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\theta, \sigma^2)$ with known process standard deviation $\sigma = 2.0\text{ nm}$. A quality control engineer designs a Wald Sequential Probability Ratio Test (SPRT) to test the simple null hypothesis $H_0 : \theta = 10.0\text{ nm}$ against the simple alternative hypothesis $H_1 : \theta = 11.0\text{ nm}$, with nominal Type I error probability $\alpha = 0.01$ and Type II error probability $\beta = 0.05$.
1. Calculate Wald's stopping boundaries $A$ and $B$, and derive the explicit stage-$m$ continuation inequalities in terms of the cumulative sample sum $\sum_{i=1}^m X_i$.
2. Derive the exact formula for the non-zero root $h(\theta)$ of $\mathbb{E}_\theta[e^{h Z_1}] = 1$, and evaluate the Operating Characteristic $L(\theta)$ and Average Sample Number $\mathbb{E}_\theta[N]$ at the four parameter values $\theta \in \{10.0, 11.0, 10.5, 10.25\}$.
3. Compute the required sample size $n_{\text{fixed}}$ of the Most Powerful fixed-sample Neyman-Pearson test achieving the exact same $(\alpha = 0.01, \beta = 0.05)$, and determine the percentage sample size reduction achieved by the SPRT under $H_0$, $H_1$, and at the worst-case indifference midpoint $\theta^* = 10.5$.

### Solution, step 1: Calculation of Wald's boundaries and linear stopping lines for $\sum_{i=1}^m X_i$
Given nominal error probabilities $\alpha = 0.01$ and $\beta = 0.05$, Wald's boundary approximations are:

$$A = \frac{1 - \beta}{\alpha} = \frac{1 - 0.05}{0.01} = \frac{0.95}{0.01} = 95.0, \qquad B = \frac{\beta}{1 - \alpha} = \frac{0.05}{1 - 0.01} = \frac{0.05}{0.99} \approx 0.050505$$

Taking natural logarithms gives the logarithmic thresholds $a = \ln A$ and $b = \ln B$:

$$a = \ln(95.0) \approx 4.553877, \qquad b = \ln\left(\frac{5}{99}\right) \approx -2.985682$$

For $X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, \sigma^2)$ with $\theta_0 = 10.0$, $\theta_1 = 11.0$, and $\sigma^2 = 4.0$, the single-observation log-likelihood ratio increment $Z_i$ is:

$$Z_i = \ln\left[ \frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp\left(-\frac{(X_i - \theta_1)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp\left(-\frac{(X_i - \theta_0)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)} \right] = \frac{\theta_1 - \theta_0}{\sigma^2} X_i - \frac{\theta_1^2 - \theta_0^2}{2\sigma^2} = \frac{11 - 10}{4} X_i - \frac{121 - 100}{8} = 0.25 X_i - 2.625$$

Notice that $0.25 X_i - 2.625 = 0.25(X_i - 10.5)$. Summing over the first $m$ observations gives the cumulative log-likelihood ratio:

$$S_m = \sum_{i=1}^m Z_i = 0.25 \sum_{i=1}^m X_i - 2.625 m$$

The SPRT continuation condition $b < S_m < a$ is therefore $-2.985682 < 0.25 \sum_{i=1}^m X_i - 2.625 m < 4.553877$. Multiplying through by $4$ and adding $10.5 m$ yields the explicit linear stopping boundaries for the cumulative sum $\sum_{i=1}^m X_i$:

$$-11.9427 + 10.5 m < \sum_{i=1}^m X_i < 18.2155 + 10.5 m$$

At stage $m$, we accept $H_0$ if $\sum_{i=1}^m X_i \le 10.5 m - 11.9427$, reject $H_0$ if $\sum_{i=1}^m X_i \ge 10.5 m + 18.2155$, and continue sampling otherwise. Notice that both boundary lines have identical slope $\frac{\theta_0 + \theta_1}{2} = 10.5\text{ nm}$ and parallel vertical intercepts $-11.9427$ and $+18.2155$.

### Solution, step 2: Derivation of $h(\theta)$, Operating Characteristic $L(\theta)$, and ASN $\mathbb{E}_\theta[N]$
When the true parameter is $\theta$, $X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, 4)$, so $Z_i = 0.25(X_i - 10.5)$ is normally distributed with mean and variance:

$$\mu_Z(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\theta[Z_1] = 0.25(\theta - 10.5), \qquad \sigma_Z^2 = \text{Var}_\theta(Z_1) = (0.25)^2 (4.0) = 0.25$$

The moment generating function of $Z_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_Z(\theta), \sigma_Z^2)$ is $M_\theta(h) = \exp\left( h \mu_Z(\theta) + \frac{1}{2} h^2 \sigma_Z^2 \right)$. Setting $M_\theta(h) = 1$ for $h \ne 0$ requires the exponent to vanish:

$$h \mu_Z(\theta) + \frac{1}{2} h^2 \sigma_Z^2 = 0 \implies h(\theta) = -\frac{2 \mu_Z(\theta)}{\sigma_Z^2} = -\frac{2(0.25)(\theta - 10.5)}{0.25} = 21 - 2\theta$$

Let us now evaluate $L(\theta)$ and $\mathbb{E}_\theta[N]$ across the four specified values of $\theta$:
- **Case 1 ($\theta = \theta_0 = 10.0$)**: Here $h(10.0) = 21 - 20 = +1$, $L(10.0) = 1 - \alpha = 0.99$, and $\mathbb{E}_{10.0}[Z_1] = 0.25(10.0 - 10.5) = -0.125$. By Wald's ASN formula:
  $$\mathbb{E}_{10.0}[N] \approx \frac{0.99(-2.985682) + 0.01(4.553877)}{-0.125} = \frac{-2.955825 + 0.045539}{-0.125} = \frac{-2.910286}{-0.125} \approx 23.28\text{ wafers}$$
- **Case 2 ($\theta = \theta_1 = 11.0$)**: Here $h(11.0) = 21 - 22 = -1$, $L(11.0) = \beta = 0.05$, and $\mathbb{E}_{11.0}[Z_1] = 0.25(11.0 - 10.5) = +0.125$. By Wald's ASN formula:
  $$\mathbb{E}_{11.0}[N] \approx \frac{0.05(-2.985682) + 0.95(4.553877)}{0.125} = \frac{-0.149284 + 4.326183}{0.125} = \frac{4.176899}{0.125} \approx 33.42\text{ wafers}$$
- **Case 3 (Indifference midpoint $\theta^* = 10.5$)**: Here $h(10.5) = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}_{10.5}[Z_1] = 0$, with $\mathbb{E}_{10.5}[Z_1^2] = \sigma_Z^2 = 0.25$. Applying the L'Hôpital zero-drift formulas:
  $$L(10.5) \approx \frac{a}{a - b} = \frac{4.553877}{4.553877 - (-2.985682)} = \frac{4.553877}{7.539559} \approx 0.6040$$
  $$\mathbb{E}_{10.5}[N] \approx \frac{-a b}{\mathbb{E}_{10.5}[Z_1^2]} = \frac{-(4.553877)(-2.985682)}{0.25} = \frac{13.59643}{0.25} \approx 54.39\text{ wafers}$$
- **Case 4 (Intermediate point $\theta = 10.25$)**: Here $h(10.25) = 21 - 20.5 = +0.5$, and $\mathbb{E}_{10.25}[Z_1] = 0.25(10.25 - 10.5) = -0.0625$. Computing the OC value:
  $$L(10.25) \approx \frac{95^{0.5} - 1}{95^{0.5} - (5/99)^{0.5}} = \frac{9.746794 - 1}{9.746794 - 0.224733} = \frac{8.746794}{9.522061} \approx 0.9186$$
  Substituting $L(10.25) = 0.9186$ into the ASN equation:
  $$\mathbb{E}_{10.25}[N] \approx \frac{0.9186(-2.985682) + 0.0814(4.553877)}{-0.0625} = \frac{-2.742648 + 0.370686}{-0.0625} = \frac{-2.371962}{-0.0625} \approx 37.95\text{ wafers}$$

### Solution, step 3: Comparison with fixed-sample Neyman-Pearson sample size $n_{\text{fixed}}$
For a fixed-sample Neyman-Pearson test of $H_0 : \theta = 10.0$ against $H_1 : \theta = 11.0$ with $\sigma = 2.0$, $\alpha = 0.01$ ($z_{0.01} = 2.32635$), and $\beta = 0.05$ ($z_{0.05} = 1.64485$), the required fixed sample size is:

$$n_{\text{fixed}} = \frac{\sigma^2 (z_\alpha + z_\beta)^2}{(\theta_1 - \theta_0)^2} = \frac{4.0 (2.32635 + 1.64485)^2}{(11.0 - 10.0)^2} = 4.0 (3.97120)^2 = 4.0 \times 15.7704 \approx 63.08 \implies n_{\text{fixed}} = 64\text{ wafers}$$

Comparing the expected sample sizes of the SPRT against $n_{\text{fixed}} = 63.08$:
- Under $H_0$ ($\theta = 10.0$): $\mathbb{E}_{10.0}[N] = 23.28$ vs. $63.08$, yielding a **$63.1\%$ reduction** in average sample size!
- Under $H_1$ ($\theta = 11.0$): $\mathbb{E}_{11.0}[N] = 33.42$ vs. $63.08$, yielding a **$47.0\%$ reduction** in average sample size!
- Even at the absolute worst-case zero-drift midpoint $\theta^* = 10.5$, $\mathbb{E}_{10.5}[N] = 54.39 < 63.08$, which is still **$13.8\%$ smaller** than the fixed-sample test!

### Question 2: Binomial SPRT for Acceptance Sampling of Lot Defect Proportions
An aerospace defense contractor inspects incoming lots of titanium fasteners for microscopic fatigue cracks. Each inspected fastener is classified as defective ($X_i = 1$, with probability $p$) or non-defective ($X_i = 0$, with probability $q = 1 - p$). A lot is considered acceptable (Producer's Quality Level) if $H_0 : p = p_0 = 0.02$ and unacceptable (Consumer's Rejectable Quality Level) if $H_1 : p = p_1 = 0.10$. The sequential inspection plan is designed with Producer's Risk $\alpha = 0.05$ and Consumer's Risk $\beta = 0.10$.
1. Derive the stage-$m$ acceptance number $c_{\text{acc}}(m)$ and rejection number $c_{\text{rej}}(m)$ for the cumulative number of defectives $D_m = \sum_{i=1}^m X_i$, and determine the minimum possible number of fasteners required to accept a defect-free lot or reject a purely defective lot.
2. Find the exact indifference defect rate $p^*$ where the log-likelihood drift vanishes ($\mathbb{E}_{p^*}[Z_1] = 0$), and calculate the Operating Characteristic $L(p^*)$ and Average Sample Number $\mathbb{E}_{p^*}[N]$ at $p^*$.
3. Calculate the ASN $\mathbb{E}_p[N]$ when the lot is perfectly defect-free ($p = 0$), at the acceptable quality level ($p = 0.02$), and at the rejectable quality level ($p = 0.10$).

### Solution, step 1: Acceptance and rejection boundary lines and minimum termination stages
With $\alpha = 0.05$ and $\beta = 0.10$, Wald's boundaries and their natural logarithms are:

$$A = \frac{1 - 0.10}{0.05} = 18.0 \implies a = \ln(18.0) \approx 2.890372, \qquad B = \frac{0.10}{1 - 0.05} = \frac{2}{19} \implies b = \ln\left(\frac{2}{19}\right) \approx -2.251292$$

For $X_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)$ with $p_0 = 0.02$ ($q_0 = 0.98$) and $p_1 = 0.10$ ($q_1 = 0.90$), the single-step log-likelihood increment is:

$$Z_i = X_i \ln\left(\frac{p_1 q_0}{p_0 q_1}\right) + \ln\left(\frac{q_1}{q_0}\right) = X_i \ln\left(\frac{0.10 \times 0.98}{0.02 \times 0.90}\right) + \ln\left(\frac{0.90}{0.98}\right) = X_i \ln\left(\frac{49}{9}\right) - \ln\left(\frac{49}{45}\right)$$

Evaluating the logarithms numerically gives $\ln(49/9) \approx 1.694596$ and $\ln(49/45) \approx 0.085158$, so:

$$Z_i = 1.694596 X_i - 0.085158 \implies S_m = 1.694596 D_m - 0.085158 m$$

where $D_m = \sum_{i=1}^m X_i$ is the cumulative count of defective fasteners in the first $m$ inspections. Dividing $b < S_m < a$ by $1.694596$ gives the linear continuation region $c_{\text{acc}}(m) < D_m < c_{\text{rej}}(m)$:

$$c_{\text{acc}}(m) = \frac{-2.251292 + 0.085158 m}{1.694596} = -1.3285 + 0.05025 m, \qquad c_{\text{rej}}(m) = \frac{2.890372 + 0.085158 m}{1.694596} = 1.7056 + 0.05025 m$$

- **Minimum sample size to accept $H_0$** (when zero defectives are found, $D_m = 0$): Setting $c_{\text{acc}}(m) \ge 0$ requires $-1.3285 + 0.05025 m \ge 0 \implies m \ge 26.44$, so at least **$m = 27$ consecutive non-defective fasteners** must be inspected before a lot can be accepted!
- **Minimum sample size to reject $H_0$** (when every inspected fastener is defective, $D_m = m$): Setting $m \ge c_{\text{rej}}(m) = 1.7056 + 0.05025 m \implies 0.94975 m \ge 1.7056 \implies m \ge 1.796$, so if the **first $m = 2$ fasteners are both defective** ($D_2 = 2 > 1.806$), the lot is rejected immediately at stage $2$!

### Solution, step 2: Indifference defect proportion $p^*$, $L(p^*)$, and $\mathbb{E}_{p^*}[N]$
The expected increment under true defect rate $p$ is $\mathbb{E}_p[Z_1] = 1.694596 p - 0.085158$. Setting $\mathbb{E}_{p^*}[Z_1] = 0$ yields the indifference proportion $p^*$ (which equals the common slope of the boundary lines!):

$$p^* = \frac{\ln(q_0 / q_1)}{\ln(p_1 q_0 / p_0 q_1)} = \frac{0.085158}{1.694596} \approx 0.050253 \quad (5.03\%\text{ defective})$$

At $p = p^* = 0.050253$, since $\mathbb{E}_{p^*}[Z_1] = 0$, the second moment $\mathbb{E}_{p^*}[Z_1^2]$ equals the variance of $Z_1 = 1.694596 X_1 - 0.085158$:

$$\mathbb{E}_{p^*}[Z_1^2] = \text{Var}_{p^*}(Z_1) = (1.694596)^2 p^*(1 - p^*) = 2.871655 \times 0.050253 \times 0.949747 \approx 0.137056$$

Therefore, the Operating Characteristic and Average Sample Number at $p^* = 0.050253$ are:

$$L(p^*) \approx \frac{a}{a - b} = \frac{2.890372}{2.890372 + 2.251292} = \frac{2.890372}{5.141664} \approx 0.5621$$

$$\mathbb{E}_{p^*}[N] \approx \frac{-a b}{\mathbb{E}_{p^*}[Z_1^2]} = \frac{(2.890372)(2.251292)}{0.137056} = \frac{6.50707}{0.137056} \approx 47.48\text{ fasteners}$$

### Solution, step 3: Evaluation of ASN at $p = 0$, $p = p_0 = 0.02$, and $p = p_1 = 0.10$
- **When $p = 0$ (perfectly defect-free lot)**: Every $X_i = 0$ deterministically, so $L(0) = 1$ and $\mathbb{E}_0[Z_1] = -0.085158$. By Wald's approximation $\mathbb{E}_0[N] \approx \frac{b}{-0.085158} = \frac{-2.251292}{-0.085158} = 26.44$ (exact integer termination occurs at $N = \lceil 26.44 \rceil = 27$ fasteners).
- **When $p = p_0 = 0.02$**: Here $L(0.02) = 1 - \alpha = 0.95$ and $\mathbb{E}_{0.02}[Z_1] = 1.694596(0.02) - 0.085158 = -0.051266$:
  $$\mathbb{E}_{0.02}[N] \approx \frac{0.95(-2.251292) + 0.05(2.890372)}{-0.051266} = \frac{-2.138727 + 0.144519}{-0.051266} = \frac{-1.994208}{-0.051266} \approx 38.90\text{ fasteners}$$
- **When $p = p_1 = 0.10$**: Here $L(0.10) = \beta = 0.10$ and $\mathbb{E}_{0.10}[Z_1] = 1.694596(0.10) - 0.085158 = +0.084302$:
  $$\mathbb{E}_{0.10}[N] \approx \frac{0.10(-2.251292) + 0.90(2.890372)}{0.084302} = \frac{-0.225129 + 2.601335}{0.084302} = \frac{2.376206}{0.084302} \approx 28.19\text{ fasteners}$$

### Question 3: Exact Overshoot Analysis and Wald's Fundamental Identity for a Two-Point Discrete Increment Walk
Suppose that under a sequential test of $H_0$ vs. $H_1$, the single-observation log-likelihood ratio increment $Z_i = \ln[f_1(X_i)/f_0(X_i)]$ takes only values on the integer lattice $\{-1, +1\}$ (scaled in units of $c > 0$), or more generally let $Y_i = Z_i / c \in \{-1, +1\}$ with $\mathbb{P}_\theta(Y_i = +1) = p$ and $\mathbb{P}_\theta(Y_i = -1) = q = 1 - p$ ($p \ne 1/2$). Suppose the logarithmic stopping boundaries are exact integer multiples of the step size $c$: $b = -M c$ and $a = +K c$ for positive integers $M, K \ge 1$.
1. Show that on this discrete lattice, the boundary overshoot at termination is identically zero ($S_N = b$ or $S_N = a$ exactly), and use Wald's Fundamental Identity $\mathbb{E}_\theta[e^{h S_N}] = 1$ to derive the **exact** Operating Characteristic $L(p)$ without any approximation.
2. Assuming $Z_i = \pm c$ is a true log-likelihood ratio increment under $H_0$ ($p = p_0$) and $H_1$ ($p = p_1$), prove that $p_1 = 1 - p_0 = q_0$ and $c = \ln(p_1 / p_0)$, and verify that Wald's boundary formulas $A = (1 - \beta)/\alpha$ and $B = \beta/(1 - \alpha)$ hold with **exact equality**.
3. Derive the exact closed-form expression for the Average Sample Number $\mathbb{E}_p[N]$ for both $p \ne 1/2$ and the symmetric fair-coin case $p = 1/2$.

### Solution, step 1: Zero overshoot and exact Operating Characteristic from Wald's Identity
Let $U_m = S_m / c = \sum_{i=1}^m Y_i$ denote the integer-valued simple random walk starting at $U_0 = 0$ with absorption barriers at $-M$ and $+K$. Because each step $Y_i \in \{-1, +1\}$ changes $U_m$ by exactly $\pm 1$, the walk cannot jump over the integer boundaries $-M$ or $+K$ without landing directly on them! Hence at the stopping time $N$, the overshoot is identically zero:

$$U_N = -M \quad (\text{so } S_N = -Mc = b) \quad \text{with probability } L(p), \qquad U_N = +K \quad (\text{so } S_N = +Kc = a) \quad \text{with probability } 1 - L(p)$$

Let us find the non-zero root $h \ne 0$ of the moment generating function $M_p(h) = \mathbb{E}_p[e^{h c Y_1}] = p e^{hc} + q e^{-hc} = 1$. Setting $u = e^{hc}$, the equation $p u + q u^{-1} = 1$ becomes the quadratic $p u^2 - u + q = (u - 1)(p u - q) = 0$. Since $h \ne 0 \implies u \ne 1$, the unique non-trivial root is:

$$u = e^{hc} = \frac{q}{p} = \frac{1 - p}{p}$$

Substituting $e^{h S_N} = \left(e^{hc}\right)^{U_N} = \left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^{U_N}$ into Wald's Fundamental Identity $\mathbb{E}_p\left[e^{h S_N}\right] = 1$, and noting that $U_N$ equals $-M$ or $+K$ with zero overshoot, gives the **exact** equation:

$$L(p) \left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^{-M} + \left[1 - L(p)\right] \left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^{K} = 1 \implies L(p) = \frac{\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^K - 1}{\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^K - \left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^{-M}} \quad (p \ne 1/2)$$

### Solution, step 2: Exact verification of Wald's boundary equations $A = (1-\beta)/\alpha$ and $B = \beta/(1-\alpha)$
For $Z_i = \ln[f_1(X_i)/f_0(X_i)]$ to take only the two values $+c$ (when $Y_i = +1$) and $-c$ (when $Y_i = -1$), the likelihood ratios on the two outcomes must satisfy:

$$\frac{p_1}{p_0} = e^{+c} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{q_1}{q_0} = \frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_0} = e^{-c}$$

Solving $p_1 = p_0 e^c$ and $1 - p_1 = (1 - p_0) e^{-c}$ yields $p_0 e^c + (1 - p_0) e^{-c} = 1 \implies e^c = \frac{1 - p_0}{p_0} = \frac{q_0}{p_0}$. Therefore, $p_1 = p_0 \left(\frac{q_0}{p_0}\right) = q_0 = 1 - p_0$, and $q_1 = 1 - p_1 = p_0$, with $c = \ln(q_0 / p_0) = \ln(p_1 / p_0) > 0$ (for $p_1 > 1/2 > p_0$).
Furthermore, the original likelihood boundaries are $A = e^a = e^{Kc} = (q_0/p_0)^K$ and $B = e^b = e^{-Mc} = (q_0/p_0)^{-M}$.
- Under $H_0$ ($p = p_0$): $\frac{q_0}{p_0} = e^c$, so our exact OC formula gives $1 - \alpha = L(p_0) = \frac{e^{Kc} - 1}{e^{Kc} - e^{-Mc}} = \frac{A - 1}{A - B}$, and $\alpha = 1 - L(p_0) = \frac{1 - B}{A - B}$.
- Under $H_1$ ($p = p_1 = q_0$): $\frac{q_1}{p_1} = \frac{p_0}{q_0} = e^{-c}$, so $\beta = L(p_1) = \frac{e^{-Kc} - 1}{e^{-Kc} - e^{Mc}} = \frac{A^{-1} - 1}{A^{-1} - B^{-1}} = \frac{B(1 - A)}{B - A} = \frac{B(A - 1)}{A - B}$, and $1 - \beta = \frac{A(1 - B)}{A - B}$.

Dividing $1 - \beta$ by $\alpha$ and $\beta$ by $1 - \alpha$ immediately confirms **exact equality**:

$$\frac{1 - \beta}{\alpha} = \frac{\frac{A(1 - B)}{A - B}}{\frac{1 - B}{A - B}} = A, \qquad \frac{\beta}{1 - \alpha} = \frac{\frac{B(A - 1)}{A - B}}{\frac{A - 1}{A - B}} = B$$

### Solution, step 3: Exact closed-form Average Sample Number $\mathbb{E}_p[N]$
For $p \ne 1/2$, the single-step drift of $U_m = S_m / c$ is $\mathbb{E}_p[Y_1] = p - q = 2p - 1 \ne 0$, and the exact terminal expectation is $\mathbb{E}_p[U_N] = -M L(p) + K[1 - L(p)]$. By Wald's First Equation:

$$\mathbb{E}_p[N] = \frac{\mathbb{E}_p[U_N]}{\mathbb{E}_p[Y_1]} = \frac{K[1 - L(p)] - M L(p)}{2p - 1} = \frac{K - (K + M) \frac{(q/p)^K - 1}{(q/p)^K - (q/p)^{-M}}}{2p - 1} \quad (p \ne 1/2)$$

For the symmetric zero-drift case $p = q = 1/2$, $\mathbb{E}_{1/2}[Y_1] = 0$, $\mathbb{E}_{1/2}[Y_1^2] = 1$, and $L(1/2) = \frac{K}{K + M}$. By Wald's Second Equation $\mathbb{E}_{1/2}[N] = \frac{\mathbb{E}_{1/2}[U_N^2]}{\mathbb{E}_{1/2}[Y_1^2]}$, since $U_N^2$ equals $M^2$ with probability $\frac{K}{K+M}$ and $K^2$ with probability $\frac{M}{K+M}$:

$$\mathbb{E}_{1/2}[N] = M^2 \left(\frac{K}{K + M}\right) + K^2 \left(\frac{M}{K + M}\right) = \frac{MK(M + K)}{K + M} = M K$$

This reproduces the exact classic Gambler's Ruin expected duration $MK$ with zero approximation error!
