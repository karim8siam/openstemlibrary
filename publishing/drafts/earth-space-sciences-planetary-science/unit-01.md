# Unit 1: Comparative Planetology and Solar System Origins

**Guiding question:** How can widely different worlds originate from one protoplanetary environment?

Planetary science combines mechanics, thermodynamics, geochemistry, remote sensing and inference to explain why neighboring worlds took different evolutionary paths. The arrangement of planets offers evidence for formation processes but never supplies an unambiguous historical recording. A useful comparative study holds one physical variable constant while varying another and asks which differences are measurable.

This unit asks students to reason from data rather than memorize an inventory of worlds. The controlled case is fictional: A research group has spectra for a hypothetical young circumstellar disk, sizes for a population of solids, and imperfect age estimates for meteorite fragments. It must test whether local condensation, collisional growth and later radial migration could explain a structured planetary system without assuming a single inevitable pathway. For every diagram and numerical example, distinguish an ideal governing law from the constitutive or empirical assumptions needed to turn it into a prediction. Many planetary measurements are indirect, and their errors can be correlated because the same calibration, shape model or data-reduction algorithm was used. A transparent uncertainty statement therefore belongs in the central scientific argument, not in a footnote after the conclusion. The test of a useful explanation is whether it survives an observation designed to distinguish it from another plausible account.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, you should be able to describe the dominant physical interactions without collapsing all mechanisms into a single cause; translate an observable into a measurable quantity with units; design a synthetic comparison that varies one quantity at a time; identify at least two independent failure modes of an inference; and communicate why a simple classroom model is not an operational planetary prediction.

## Five lessons

### 1.1 Planetary science as a comparative discipline

**Central concept.** Composition, bulk density, orbit, surface age and magnetic activity offer complementary descriptions of a planetary body; none alone identifies its entire history. Comparisons should distinguish body type, energy source and observational coverage. In this part of comparative planetology and solar system origins, the important skill is to connect a plausible physical mechanism to a particular, imperfect record. The phrase *explains the evidence* should never mean merely that a sketch resembles a photograph. A mechanism must be compatible with constraints on size, time, composition and energetics. It should predict something that another observation could disprove. The expected strength of the inference depends on the quality of the instrument and the uniqueness of the prediction, not on the visual appeal of a diagram.

**What would actually be measured?** Measure normalized density, albedo, rotation and surface morphology for several synthetic worlds with different detection limits. This is a constructed teaching example; no row should be mistaken for a spacecraft measurement. Define the measurable quantities before choosing a fitting equation, and preserve calibration units, coordinate frame and acquisition conditions. For a remote world, resolution may change over the scene and the hidden part of a terrain or atmosphere may have fundamentally different characteristics. Report not only a central estimate but also which conditions remained unobserved and which parameters were held fixed for convenience.

**Failure mode and competing account.** Selection bias: well-imaged planets have richer apparent feature catalogs than distant or cloud-covered bodies. The strongest competing explanation is not an arbitrary alternative chosen to look weak. It should be physically plausible under the same observing circumstances. Ask which observable, spatial relationship or laboratory property differs between the explanations. If both models predict nearly the same signal at the available resolution, the honest result is non-identifiability. A mission proposal can still be useful in this situation by explicitly targeting that degeneracy. Do not increase certainty simply because repeated measurements use the same processing assumptions.

**Worked quantitative case — Disk temperature.** Consider the fictional parameter value **0.5 AU**. Under the stated classroom approximation, **T = 278 K / sqrt(r in AU), a toy radiative scaling.** Substituting the input gives **393.151 K**. This number is neither a retrieved physical parameter nor a validated forecast. Its role is to expose scale and sensitivity. First confirm that the units on both sides agree; next estimate how the result changes if the input moves by ten percent while all other conditions are fixed. Finally ask whether the model has an appropriate domain for extrapolation. A numerical answer without those checks can be exact arithmetic attached to an incorrect physical story.

## Integrated investigation: Comparative Planetology and Solar System Origins

**Research question.** How can widely different worlds originate from one protoplanetary environment? Use the fabricated evidence packet generated for this unit, not a downloaded mission observation, to test the issue. The packet provides five numerical inputs corresponding to five distinct lesson calculations. Prepare an analysis log before computing results: specify which data are invented, which constants are adopted from reference physics, which quantities are exact in the model, and which are uncertain in an actual measurement. Do not call synthetic demonstration values planetary facts.

**Workflow A — observations and provenance.** Build a small metadata table with columns for source, timestamp or hypothetical epoch, spatial footprint, units, calibration state, quality flag and an explicit 'synthetic' indicator. Check that every row belongs to the same stated physical or geometrical reference when the comparison requires that. If different observations cover different regions or epochs, say what assumption permits their comparison. A useful practice is to write down the interpretation you expect before opening a calculated output; doing so can reveal a tendency to favor a result after seeing it.

**Workflow B — model construction.** Start with one conservation, geometry or rate relationship from the unit. Derive the form in symbols, then set parameter values. Write an extreme-case check: an input tending to zero, a very long orbital or cooling time, or a hypothetical measurement at the edge of the instrument's range. Not every extreme has physical meaning, but the exercise can reveal sign errors or a model's restricted domain. Compare at least one dimensionless ratio where possible. Record any calibration or geological assumptions that cannot be derived from the formula itself.

**Workflow C — alternatives and sensitivity.** Construct a second explanation able to reproduce at least one headline feature of the mock dataset. Change a single uncertain parameter by plus or minus ten percent and tabulate the output. Then alter the model *structure* itself: for example, replace uniform material properties with a layered assumption, permit a nonstationary flux, or introduce a time-varying forcing condition. When the inferred history changes more from model structure than from stated measurement error, the report must say that the quantity is weakly identified. A precise fitted value is not automatically a precise scientific claim.

**Workflow D — cross-unit integration.** Connect this unit to two other themes in the book. For example, relate a dynamical interpretation to geological chronology, a spectral inference to atmospheric chemistry, or an internal energy source to an icy-surface observation. Each connection should contain one shared variable and one new source of uncertainty. This prevents a detailed but isolated calculation from being treated as a full planetary system model. If a comparison relies on terrestrial experiments, identify the gravitational, thermal or compositional regime where the analogy ceases to be straightforward.

**Workflow E — reporting and evaluation.** Submit a one-page methods statement, a table of computed values, one plot with labeled axes, an uncertainty inventory, and a claim supported by the data packet. Include an explicit alternative interpretation and a feasible next observation. The teacher should assess whether units, underlying equations, provenance and falsifiability are correct; artistic polish alone is not evidence of scientific rigor. Use language such as *consistent with*, *constrained by* and *not distinguished by these data* when warranted. Do not advertise an invented training result as mission validation.

## Unit synthesis and research discussion

The scientific difficulty of comparative planetology and solar system origins is not simply locating one more fact. It is learning what the available observations exclude. A strong synthesis distinguishes a process being physically possible from that process being historically demonstrated. It also distinguishes a local interpretation from a global planetary pattern and a present state from the history that formed it. Return to the unit's guiding question and state three claims at different strengths: one direct observation, one explanation that can be tested, and one conjecture for which available information is insufficient. For each, name the likely effect of improved instrumental precision, better geographic coverage and stronger physical modeling. These improvements address different errors and should not be treated as interchangeable. Explore whether the most valuable next mission measurement is an imaging survey, an in-situ measurement, a laboratory calibration or repeated monitoring.

**Capstone prompt.** You have resources for one additional investigation only. Present the competing hypotheses in a table and estimate which candidate observation would most clearly separate their predictions per unit cost and risk. Your justification must acknowledge a scenario in which your chosen observation gives an ambiguous result. A responsible conclusion is an explicit account of what remains unknown, not an invented certainty.
