# Unit 04 — Virus–host interactions and the immune response

> **OpenSTEM Book 14, Virology.** Undergraduate editorial draft. Every numerical example and modeled curve uses fictional input. Expert review and publication approval pending. Do not treat these pages as laboratory protocols, clinical tools or instructions for manipulating viruses.

## Learning objectives

Infection is a multi-scale interaction involving cells, tissues, immune memory and ecological opportunity. A transcript, an antibody titer and a clinical outcome are not interchangeable. This unit highlights the importance of causal diagrams, independent measurements and clinically appropriate boundaries.

The five conceptual themes are **host context; immune signaling; correlates versus outcomes; temporal dynamics; mechanistic ambiguity**. A reader completing this unit should be able to explain each of its five topics, compare at least two rival causes of a given observation, reproduce five quantitative answers, and critique a synthetic research design. Scientific maturity requires distinguishing the scale at which a fact has been measured from the scale at which an explanation is being proposed. If an observation describes nucleic-acid fragments, do not call it an infection count. If an outcome describes an immune-related marker, do not call it proven protection without validation.

## Foundations for evidence-based reasoning

Virology spans structural, cellular, ecological and clinical observations. A claim supported at one level may be unjustified at another. Check whether a measured signal is a proxy, a rate or a true outcome. Sampling time, location, group composition and denominators can change interpretation even when the plotted shape looks identical.

This unit uses models as tools for exploring assumptions. One parameter may be an arbitrary scale, while another represents a mathematical saturation point. Neither value should be mistaken for a calibrated property of a real virus. When interacting with a chart, first predict a direction, then alter one control, then explain the change in terms of the formula. Repeat with the second control and look for a limiting case. Observe how even a simple model can be illuminating for reasoning yet unsuitable for operational decisions. Reading the explicit warnings attached to each model is part of the exercise.

## Responsible research boundary

The descriptions are conceptual and pedagogical. No experiments requiring access to infectious material, clinical specimens, animals or specialized biosecurity facilities are needed or permitted by the lesson plan. Real research requires institution-specific ethical review, biosafety training and oversight. The synthetic case studies are not data collected from people, hospitals, laboratories or the environment. For work with real published material, cite the original source, verify context and licensing, and avoid reproducing identifiable records. Health, agricultural and public safety decisions should follow qualified professional guidance, not simplified student exercises.

## Topic 4.1 — Cellular sensing and antiviral interferon programs

### Mechanism and explanatory scope

Host cells respond to viral-associated molecular patterns through diverse recognition systems. Interferon signaling can coordinate antiviral states by changing expression of many proteins, but the effects depend on cell type, prior exposure, timing and signal strength. Organism-level outcomes reflect an interacting system of barriers and immune cells.

For **host context**, identify the unit of observation, the relevant timescale, and the step for which evidence is missing. A structural observation cannot alone prove a clinical or population-level effect.

### Competing explanations

A strong interferon-associated transcript signature can indicate sensing, inflammation or persistent signaling without proving that productive infection is controlled. In some contexts, late or dysregulated inflammation contributes to harm rather than protection.

### Measurement and controls

Interpret an invented time course of a sensing reporter, gene-expression marker and cell viability. Decide which measurements speak to signaling and which might speak to host function; make no conclusion about human symptoms from the toy curves.

Declare the outcome, unit, denominator, window and negative control. Technical repeats are not independent biological replicates; no fictional curve supplies a confidence interval or causal proof.

### Limitations and connections

This is not a guide to modulating antiviral immunity in vivo. Abstract signaling curves exclude complex tissue feedback and should never direct patient-specific decisions.

## Topic 4.2 — Intrinsic restriction and host-cell compatibility

### Mechanism and explanatory scope

Cells contain constitutive and induced factors that may block various stages of viral information flow. Such intrinsic restrictions can be linked to nucleic-acid sensing, protein degradation or cellular trafficking. Multiple barriers can act in series, so release from one hypothetical block need not permit full completion of the life cycle.

## Topic 4.3 — Antibodies, lymphocytes and immune memory

### Mechanism and explanatory scope

B cells, antibodies and T cells recognize different antigenic contexts and contribute in distinct ways to protective responses. Neutralization and other antibody-mediated functions are assay specific; memory can alter response speed or quality after previous exposure. Cellular immunity may matter even when a particular antibody measurement is low.

For **correlates versus outcomes**, identify the unit of observation, the relevant timescale, and the step for which evidence is missing. A structural observation cannot alone prove a clinical or population-level effect.

### Competing explanations

An antibody-binding value is not the same as a validated correlate of protection. A decline in circulating antibody may coexist with memory cells or tissue-localized immunity, and protection may differ by endpoint such as infection or severe disease.

## Topic 4.4 — Persistence, latency and chronic infection concepts

### Mechanism and explanatory scope

A transient detection event, chronic productive infection and latent genome persistence refer to different biological states. Some viral systems establish reservoirs or periods of low expression; reactivation and shedding are separate observable phenomena. These terms should be applied only where the relevant mechanism has been demonstrated.

For **temporal dynamics**, identify the unit of observation, the relevant timescale, and the step for which evidence is missing. A structural observation cannot alone prove a clinical or population-level effect.

### Competing explanations

A negative sample does not prove complete biological elimination, and a positive molecular signal does not necessarily mean active replication. Sampling site, assay threshold and the time course are essential to inference.

## Topic 4.5 — Host injury, pathology and clinical endpoint separation

### Mechanism and explanatory scope

Tissue injury during viral disease can reflect direct damage, host inflammation, vascular or metabolic consequences, secondary conditions and host baseline vulnerabilities. ## Integrated synthetic research investigation: A molecular marker with a delayed host response

### Scientific question and competing models

This investigation tests how much can be inferred from limited numerical evidence. The first explanation is that **host sensing explains a delayed reporter**. The rival explanation is that **general cellular stress explains the same reporter curve**. Each is formulated to account for a possible upward trend without supplying any actual biological or epidemiological measurements. The goal is not to make the plots resemble authentic virus experiments; it is to learn why similar-looking data can support different causal stories and how a discriminating observation improves a research plan.

The comparison must keep the outcome definition constant. Make a causal diagram with source, intermediate step, observed proxy and background variables. Place an unmeasured factor near the arrow it might influence, rather than listing confounders without indicating their pathway. A credible alternative explanation must be capable of producing a plausible observed difference. State what information would be required to refute each explanation rather than merely to generate an additional matching curve.

### Data and transparent assumptions

|--:|--:|--:|
| 0 | 14.50 | 15.80 |
| 1 | 18.16 | 17.86 |
| 2 | 22.43 | 20.03 |
| 3 | 27.16 | 22.27 |

| 5 | 37.68 | 26.92 |

These are **fabricated dimensionless teaching indices**, not real assay outputs, infection counts, cases, experimental animals or clinical data. No uncertainty bands are supplied because there are no biological replicates. The table does not support p-values, confidence intervals or causal claims. Between stage zero and stage five, series A changes from **14.5** to **37.68**; the difference is **23.18 index units**. Series B changes from **15.8** to **26.92**, a difference of **11.12 index units**. These arithmetic descriptions are valid within the constructed table only.

A simple endpoint ratio, B over A, equals **0.714** if they are defined on comparable scales. A different normalization might change that ratio dramatically without changing the underlying plotted order. Plot both series with the invented stage index on the horizontal axis. It is incorrect to label the index in hours or days because the exercise deliberately supplies no physical time units. Try plotting the percent change from baseline instead, and explain why percent changes can exaggerate differences when the baseline is small.

### Study design without pathogen experimentation

The most important planned independent measurement is: **separate viability proxy from pathway-specific signal**. The leading confound is **cell state and timing**. State how the independent measurement would behave under the two hypotheses and whether the proposed design could still produce the same answer under different mechanisms. Use only synthetic data, publicly documented nonidentifiable aggregate reports and pencil-and-paper reasoning. No live virus, infectious specimen, virus propagation, sequence engineering or physical containment procedure is authorized or required for this educational investigation.

Distinguish technical repeats, independent samples and randomized comparisons. An assay readout can be perfectly repeatable while its causal interpretation remains incorrect. Record denominator, missing observations and any change to the definition of a positive result.

### Interpretation and competing-cause exercise

Evaluate the constructed series using three levels of claims. At the first level, directly report its numerical values. At the second, describe a statistical pattern such as monotonic rise, relative difference or saturation. At the third, discuss whether a particular biological mechanism is responsible, and explicitly state that the invented data cannot decide. A fourth level, practical intervention or policy, needs real outcomes and specialist scrutiny and cannot follow from these pages.

The strongest scientific answer may be that the present design is underdetermined: more than one account fits the observations. When the two rival hypotheses make identical predictions for the chosen outcome, collecting more of the same outcome alone may not resolve the ambiguity. Change the type of observation or the comparison structure. Write a paragraph describing a result that would contradict hypothesis one but be expected under hypothesis two, while refusing to treat the imagined result as if it actually occurred.

### Six-part assignment and grading guide

1. Write a one-sentence research question with a specified outcome and observation window (2 points).
2. Draw a causal diagram with the two competing mechanisms and at least one confound (3 points).
3. Calculate absolute and proportional changes from the synthetic table with correct labels (3 points).
4. Explain how changing the sampling denominator could reverse a simplistic conclusion (3 points).
5. Specify a safer, nonexperimental, computational distinction between the hypotheses (4 points).
6. Write a short reproducibility statement with data provenance, invented status and three limitations (5 points).

Strong submissions separate calculation from explanation, use defensible language, and do not imply that a toy model proves a statement about an actual virus, patient, ecosystem or intervention. Weak submissions introduce unsupported numerical assumptions, hide their denominators or mistake plot smoothness for model validation.

## Unit synthesis: linking five concepts without forcing causality

The central lesson is that **cellular sensing and antiviral interferon programs**, **intrinsic restriction and host-cell compatibility**, **antibodies, lymphocytes and immune memory**, **persistence, latency and chronic infection concepts** and **host injury, pathology and clinical endpoint separation** answer related but not identical questions. Combine them into an evidence map. In the first column, write what the object or process is. In the second, specify a directly observable variable. In the third, state what the observation fails to establish. In the fourth, propose a separate measurement or comparison that could discriminate two competing explanations. The ability to name what evidence does *not* support is as central to science as correctly calculating a number.

### A worked critical-reading pattern

Start with a fictional headline claiming that a sample's measured index has doubled. Ask what the index measures and what unit was used. The number may describe a genome proxy, an assay output, a host marker or a reporting count. None is automatically a measure of an individual's contagiousness. Next, check whether the denominator is the same in the two periods. If collection criteria, instrument sensitivity, target population or data-processing rules have changed, the ratio may not compare like with like. Finally, decide whether alternative explanations predict different observable outcomes, and whether those outcomes were measured. You should be able to criticize the inference without asserting that the opposite explanation is necessarily true.

Build a two-stage evidence table: direct observations first, assumptions second. Note how a negative control or changed denominator could overturn a simple interpretation. Then decide which measurement would genuinely distinguish the candidate explanations.

### Open-book examination problems

**Essay A — causal chain.** Choose the topic **cellular sensing and antiviral interferon programs** and write a diagram of five plausible links from observation to explanation. For every link, distinguish an observation from a proposed cause. Show where **antibodies, lymphocytes and immune memory** might supply a competing explanation. Write a 200-word note on which inference is least supported. An appropriate answer can include 'insufficient evidence' when the source cannot distinguish alternatives.

**Essay B — dimensions and denominators.** Compare the five numeric cases in this unit. Identify which quantities are percentages, unitless proportions, indices, rates or expectations. Explain why a probability cannot exceed one, while an expected number of events may exceed one. Show an extreme-value test for one formula and state why the result need not reflect any measured system.

**Essay C — measurement design.** Using **host injury, pathology and clinical endpoint separation**, choose a hypothetical data source and a misleading interpretation that could arise from changing its sampling frame. Specify a control, a sample unit, a reporting window and one independently measured corroborating variable. Make the design noninfectious, computational and ethically appropriate. Explain what a negative result would mean and why repeated technical observations might not establish reproducibility across biological systems.

**Essay D — communicating uncertainty.** Write two abstracts for the same invented table. The first should contain only direct numerical claims. The second may offer a possible mechanism but must label it as a hypothesis and describe its strongest competitor. Identify any phrase in the second abstract that could be mistaken for causal proof and revise it. This exercise values precise communication over confident-sounding conclusions.
