# Unit 07 — Vaccination, antiviral principles and prevention literacy

> **OpenSTEM Book 14, Virology.** Undergraduate editorial draft. Every numerical example and modeled curve uses fictional input. Expert review and publication approval pending. Do not treat these pages as laboratory protocols, clinical tools or instructions for manipulating viruses.

## Learning objectives

Vaccination and therapeutic research involve distinct causal questions about immune preparation, molecular effects, safety, clinical outcomes and implementation. An observed biomarker change can be real yet medically irrelevant. Use transparent comparative arithmetic while avoiding any prescription or biological enhancement guidance.

The five conceptual themes are **immunity and endpoints; comparative risks; real-world effectiveness; evolutionary uncertainty; evidence standards**. A reader completing this unit should be able to explain each of its five topics, compare at least two rival causes of a given observation, reproduce five quantitative answers, and critique a synthetic research design. Scientific maturity requires distinguishing the scale at which a fact has been measured from the scale at which an explanation is being proposed. If an observation describes nucleic-acid fragments, do not call it an infection count. If an outcome describes an immune-related marker, do not call it proven protection without validation.

## Foundations for evidence-based reasoning

Virology spans structural, cellular, ecological and clinical observations. A claim supported at one level may be unjustified at another. Check whether a measured signal is a proxy, a rate or a true outcome. Sampling time, location, group composition and denominators can change interpretation even when the plotted shape looks identical.

This unit uses models as tools for exploring assumptions. One parameter may be an arbitrary scale, while another represents a mathematical saturation point. Neither value should be mistaken for a calibrated property of a real virus. When interacting with a chart, first predict a direction, then alter one control, then explain the change in terms of the formula. Repeat with the second control and look for a limiting case. Observe how even a simple model can be illuminating for reasoning yet unsuitable for operational decisions. Reading the explicit warnings attached to each model is part of the exercise.

## Responsible research boundary

The descriptions are conceptual and pedagogical. No experiments requiring access to infectious material, clinical specimens, animals or specialized biosecurity facilities are needed or permitted by the lesson plan. Real research requires institution-specific ethical review, biosafety training and oversight. The synthetic case studies are not data collected from people, hospitals, laboratories or the environment. For work with real published material, cite the original source, verify context and licensing, and avoid reproducing identifiable records. Health, agricultural and public safety decisions should follow qualified professional guidance, not simplified student exercises.

## Topic 7.1 — Vaccination as immune education

### Mechanism and explanatory scope

Vaccines aim to create or reinforce immune memory against selected antigens without requiring natural disease as the route to immune learning. Platforms differ in delivery and context, and outcomes can involve protection against infection, symptomatic disease, severe illness or other endpoints.

For **immunity and endpoints**, identify the unit of observation, the relevant timescale, and the step for which evidence is missing. A structural observation cannot alone prove a clinical or population-level effect.

### Competing explanations

No vaccine is automatically sterilizing, and measured effectiveness depends on the outcome, time since immunization, population and circulating pathogens. A correlational comparison can be misleading if groups differ in baseline risk.

Compare a biological change with a shifted sampling frame or instrument response. Connect the topic to **prevention, layered controls and trade-offs** and state one observation that would differentiate the explanations.

Waning measured biomarkers, waning protection and changing exposure risk must be distinguished. Memory responses can have kinetics that differ from circulating molecule levels.

## Topic 7.3 — Antiviral mechanisms and therapeutic evidence

### Mechanism and explanatory scope

Antiviral drugs may interfere with specific viral or host-dependent processes, but their use is justified by controlled evidence for particular indications. Mechanism, exposure at relevant tissues, toxicity, timing and resistance jointly influence outcomes. Pharmacodynamic concepts should not be reduced to a single cell-culture inhibition number.

For **real-world effectiveness**, identify the unit of observation, the relevant timescale, and the step for which evidence is missing. A structural observation cannot alone prove a clinical or population-level effect.

### Competing explanations

A compound that reduces a reporter signal might affect cell metabolism or assay chemistry rather than viral replication. Laboratory potency does not prove clinical benefit, and outcomes differ across patient populations.

## Topic 7.4 — Resistance as an evolutionary and clinical problem

### Mechanism and explanatory scope

Drug resistance refers to decreased susceptibility associated with particular biological contexts and validated evidence, whereas clinical nonresponse can arise for many reasons unrelated to resistance. Selection may shape population frequencies, but surveillance and clinical outcomes need separate evaluation.

For **evolutionary uncertainty**, identify the unit of observation, the relevant timescale, and the step for which evidence is missing. A structural observation cannot alone prove a clinical or population-level effect.

### Competing explanations

An unusual sequence change does not automatically confer resistance. Treatment history, adherence, diagnosis, host factors, experimental variability and mixed populations complicate interpretation.

## Topic 7.5 — Prevention, layered controls and trade-offs

### Mechanism and explanatory scope

Prevention methods address distinct links in a causal chain: exposure opportunities, transmission conditions, host susceptibility and severe outcomes. Different layers can complement one another, but effectiveness is context-specific and depends on behavior, implementation and uncertainty.

For **evidence standards**, identify the unit of observation, the relevant timescale, and the step for which evidence is missing. A structural observation cannot alone prove a clinical or population-level effect.

### Competing explanations

Adding numerical reductions from two independent protective layers is usually incorrect because sequential probabilities multiply, and layers may interact. The most effective operational response depends on the particular virus and setting.

## Integrated synthetic research investigation: A relative-risk reduction in synthetic cohorts

### Scientific question and competing models

This investigation tests how much can be inferred from limited numerical evidence. The first explanation is that **an effective biological prevention layer explains lower outcome rate**. The rival explanation is that **different baseline risk between groups explains the association**. Each is formulated to account for a possible upward trend without supplying any actual biological or epidemiological measurements. The goal is not to make the plots resemble authentic virus experiments; it is to learn why similar-looking data can support different causal stories and how a discriminating observation improves a research plan.

The comparison must keep the outcome definition constant. Make a causal diagram with source, intermediate step, observed proxy and background variables. Place an unmeasured factor near the arrow it might influence, rather than listing confounders without indicating their pathway. A credible alternative explanation must be capable of producing a plausible observed difference. State what information would be required to refute each explanation rather than merely to generate an additional matching curve.

### Data and transparent assumptions

| Stage | Series A (synthetic index) | Series B (synthetic index) |
|--:|--:|--:|
| 0 | 17.88 | 19.10 |
| 1 | 22.08 | 21.50 |
| 2 | 26.99 | 24.03 |
| 3 | 32.43 | 26.65 |
| 4 | 38.30 | 29.33 |
| 5 | 44.53 | 32.07 |

These are **fabricated dimensionless teaching indices**, not real assay outputs, infection counts, cases, experimental animals or clinical data. No uncertainty bands are supplied because there are no biological replicates. The table does not support p-values, confidence intervals or causal claims. Between stage zero and stage five, series A changes from **17.88** to **44.53**; the difference is **26.65 index units**. Series B changes from **19.1** to **32.07**, a difference of **12.97 index units**. These arithmetic descriptions are valid within the constructed table only.

A simple endpoint ratio, B over A, equals **0.720** if they are defined on comparable scales. A different normalization might change that ratio dramatically without changing the underlying plotted order. Plot both series with the invented stage index on the horizontal axis. It is incorrect to label the index in hours or days because the exercise deliberately supplies no physical time units. Try plotting the percent change from baseline instead, and explain why percent changes can exaggerate differences when the baseline is small.

### Study design without pathogen experimentation

The most important planned independent measurement is: **compare adjusted outcomes in an appropriate design**. The leading confound is **unmeasured confounding**. State how the independent measurement would behave under the two hypotheses and whether the proposed design could still produce the same answer under different mechanisms. Use only synthetic data, publicly documented nonidentifiable aggregate reports and pencil-and-paper reasoning. No live virus, infectious specimen, virus propagation, sequence engineering or physical containment procedure is authorized or required for this educational investigation.

Distinguish technical repeats, independent samples and randomized comparisons. An assay readout can be perfectly repeatable while its causal interpretation remains incorrect. Record denominator, missing observations and any change to the definition of a positive result.

### Interpretation and competing-cause exercise

Evaluate the constructed series using three levels of claims. At the first level, directly report its numerical values. At the second, describe a statistical pattern such as monotonic rise, relative difference or saturation. At the third, discuss whether a particular biological mechanism is responsible, and explicitly state that the invented data cannot decide. A fourth level, practical intervention or policy, needs real outcomes and specialist scrutiny and cannot follow from these pages.

The strongest scientific answer may be that the present design is underdetermined: more than one account fits the observations. When the two rival hypotheses make identical predictions for the chosen outcome, collecting more of the same outcome alone may not resolve the ambiguity. Change the type of observation or the comparison structure. Write a paragraph describing a result that would contradict hypothesis one but be expected under hypothesis two, while refusing to treat the imagined result as if it actually occurred.

### Six-part assignment and grading guide

1. Write a one-sentence research question with a specified outcome and observation window (2 points).
2. Draw a causal diagram with the two competing mechanisms and at least one confound (3 points).
3. Calculate absolute and proportional changes from the synthetic table with correct labels (3 points).
4. Explain how changing the sampling denominator could reverse a simplistic conclusion (3 points).
5. Specify a safer, nonexperimental, computational distinction between the hypotheses (4 points).
6. Write a short reproducibility statement with data provenance, invented status and three limitations (5 points).

Strong submissions separate calculation from explanation, use defensible language, and do not imply that a toy model proves a statement about an actual virus, patient, ecosystem or intervention. Weak submissions introduce unsupported numerical assumptions, hide their denominators or mistake plot smoothness for model validation.

## Unit synthesis: linking five concepts without forcing causality

The central lesson is that **vaccination as immune education**, **immune correlates, waning and uncertainty**, **antiviral mechanisms and therapeutic evidence**, **resistance as an evolutionary and clinical problem** and **prevention, layered controls and trade-offs** answer related but not identical questions. Combine them into an evidence map. In the first column, write what the object or process is. In the second, specify a directly observable variable. In the third, state what the observation fails to establish. In the fourth, propose a separate measurement or comparison that could discriminate two competing explanations. The ability to name what evidence does *not* support is as central to science as correctly calculating a number.

### A worked critical-reading pattern

Start with a fictional headline claiming that a sample's measured index has doubled. Ask what the index measures and what unit was used. The number may describe a genome proxy, an assay output, a host marker or a reporting count. None is automatically a measure of an individual's contagiousness. Next, check whether the denominator is the same in the two periods. If collection criteria, instrument sensitivity, target population or data-processing rules have changed, the ratio may not compare like with like. Finally, decide whether alternative explanations predict different observable outcomes, and whether those outcomes were measured. You should be able to criticize the inference without asserting that the opposite explanation is necessarily true.

Build a two-stage evidence table: direct observations first, assumptions second. Note how a negative control or changed denominator could overturn a simple interpretation. Then decide which measurement would genuinely distinguish the candidate explanations.

## Additional section: communicating risk without overstatement

Good communication distinguishes a mechanistic laboratory claim, a population association and a patient outcome. Relative percentages require denominators, confidence intervals require interpretable sampling assumptions and visualizations must not hide uncertainty or missing data.

A large relative improvement may correspond to a small absolute change, and a small case series may be highly unstable. Conclusions that change with plausible confounders should be qualified rather than advertised as discoveries.

Rewrite an imaginary press release into an evidence summary with absolute risks, sample sizes, observation windows and major unknowns. Identify any headline that implies causality from a purely observational design.

Avoid using precise-sounding numbers without validated data. Educational claims and health decisions must remain separate.
