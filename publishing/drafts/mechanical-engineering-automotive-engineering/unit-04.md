# Unit 04, Lesson 01 — Tire slip and force generation

**Learning objective.** Build a reproducible analysis of **contact-patch mechanics and traction envelope**, then evaluate whether set conservative validation limits. The illustrative field case is **instrumented hard braking on variable-grip pavement**. The primary relation is `Slip_brake = (v-R*omega_wheel)/max(v,epsilon)`; it is a starting approximation, not a complete calibration or certification method.

## 1. Physical model: meaning before arithmetic

A rolling wheel has nearly matched circumferential and vehicle speeds; during braking it turns more slowly. Slip ratio is useful only with a clear sign convention and low-speed safeguard. Tire force depends on the road interface and nonlinear effects, so higher slip does not monotonically increase usable braking force. Sensors cannot directly report the true road-friction coefficient without estimation.

A vehicle's response is generated by several coupled subsystems. To study contact-patch mechanics and traction envelope, isolate the relevant physical interaction without forgetting the boundary terms. Specify whether the driver, controller, road, tires and propulsion hardware are inputs to the calculation or the subject of the prediction. Treat the vehicle as a system with an initial state: speed, loading, gear or drive mode, road slope and environmental conditions can all alter the answer. When a component efficiency or friction coefficient enters the algebra, distinguish a measured characteristic from a nominal assumption. Otherwise the numerical output may appear precise while remaining only a scenario.

For instrumented hard braking on variable-grip pavement, draw a schematic with arrows indicating force, torque or energy transfer as appropriate. Put the chosen positive direction and sign convention on that diagram. The model is useful when its simplifications are consistent with the physical case; it is misleading when those assumptions are concealed. The critical mistake to prevent here is **assuming friction is independent of slip and normal load**. Describe which term in the governing relation this mistake corrupts and how it would appear in a field measurement.

## 2. Derivation, variables and dimensional validation

Start with `Slip_brake = (v-R*omega_wheel)/max(v,epsilon)`. Identify every physical quantity, its SI unit and whether the relation is a force balance, kinematic identity, empirical approximation or definition. Translate any speed in km/h into m/s before inserting it into a force or energy calculation. Rotational speed in revolutions per minute must be converted into rad/s for `power = torque × angular speed`; slope percentages must be converted to ratios or angles before applying trigonometric functions. Do not mix battery energy measured in kWh with instantaneous electric power measured in kW, and do not compare road-wheel output directly to fuel energy without the intervening conversion losses.

Dimensional homogeneity is a first check, but it is not enough. Consider the limit as an input tends to zero or as it reaches the largest range considered. Does the expression produce a physically sensible result? Would the equation imply negative absolute energy consumption, a wheel force greater than feasible traction, or an efficiency exceeding unity? If so, inspect the assumptions and numerical domain. Some models are not defined at standstill; others explicitly neglect slip, transient dynamics or temperature variation. The equation itself should not be forced outside the situation it describes.

If inputs are correlated, simple independent sweeps provide only a sensitivity demonstration, not a statistically defensible confidence interval.

## 3. Worked engineering calculation

A vehicle travels at 20 m/s while wheel rim equivalent speed is 16 m/s. The idealized braking slip is (20−16)/20 = 0.20, or 20%. It is not automatically the optimal slip for that tire or surface.
