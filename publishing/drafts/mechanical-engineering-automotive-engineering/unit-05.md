# Unit 05, Lesson 01 — Bicycle model and lateral acceleration

**Learning objective.** Build a reproducible analysis of **kinematic curvature and tire-force requirement**, then evaluate whether choose speed during tests. The illustrative field case is **a constant-radius corner at moderate speed**. The primary relation is `a_y = v^2/R`; it is a starting approximation, not a complete calibration or certification method.

## 1. Physical model: meaning before arithmetic

A simple bicycle model combines left and right wheels into front and rear axle representatives. The kinematic form is useful at low slip; tire cornering stiffness and lateral dynamics become important as speed rises. Required lateral acceleration increases with the square of speed. If radius stays constant, doubling speed quadruples lateral-force demand; this is why speed selection matters more than small geometric adjustments near adhesion limits.

A vehicle's response is generated by several coupled subsystems. To study kinematic curvature and tire-force requirement, isolate the relevant physical interaction without forgetting the boundary terms. Specify whether the driver, controller, road, tires and propulsion hardware are inputs to the calculation or the subject of the prediction. Treat the vehicle as a system with an initial state: speed, loading, gear or drive mode, road slope and environmental conditions can all alter the answer. When a component efficiency or friction coefficient enters the algebra, distinguish a measured characteristic from a nominal assumption. Otherwise the numerical output may appear precise while remaining only a scenario.

For a constant-radius corner at moderate speed, draw a schematic with arrows indicating force, torque or energy transfer as appropriate. Put the chosen positive direction and sign convention on that diagram. The model is useful when its simplifications are consistent with the physical case; it is misleading when those assumptions are concealed. The critical mistake to prevent here is **assuming geometric steering angle describes tire slip at high speed**. Describe which term in the governing relation this mistake corrupts and how it would appear in a field measurement.

## 2. Derivation, variables and dimensional validation

Start with `a_y = v^2/R`. Identify every physical quantity, its SI unit and whether the relation is a force balance, kinematic identity, empirical approximation or definition. Translate any speed in km/h into m/s before inserting it into a force or energy calculation. Rotational speed in revolutions per minute must be converted into rad/s for `power = torque × angular speed`; slope percentages must be converted to ratios or angles before applying trigonometric functions. Do not mix battery energy measured in kWh with instantaneous electric power measured in kW, and do not compare road-wheel output directly to fuel energy without the intervening conversion losses.

Dimensional homogeneity is a first check, but it is not enough. Consider the limit as an input tends to zero or as it reaches the largest range considered. Does the expression produce a physically sensible result? Would the equation imply negative absolute energy consumption, a wheel force greater than feasible traction, or an efficiency exceeding unity? If so, inspect the assumptions and numerical domain. Some models are not defined at standstill; others explicitly neglect slip, transient dynamics or temperature variation. The equation itself should not be forced outside the situation it describes.

For a careful calculation maintain a table with columns **symbol, definition, unit, nominal value, evidence source, plausible interval**. Recalculate the expression at at least two bounding input settings and evaluate which measurement would most reduce uncertainty. If inputs are correlated, simple independent sweeps provide only a sensitivity demonstration, not a statistically defensible confidence interval.

## 3. Worked engineering calculation

At 15 m/s on a 75 m radius, lateral acceleration = 15²/75 = 3.0 m/s² ≈ 0.306 g. At 20 m/s on the same radius the ideal value rises to 5.33 m/s².

A result can satisfy the formula exactly and still be inapplicable because one assumption is not true.

## 4. Field measurement plan and uncertainty

A defensible experiment for a constant-radius corner at moderate speed starts with a stated question, the independent variable to vary, and the output to observe. Write down how each sensor is mounted, calibrated, sampled and time-synchronized. For a force or torque study, identify where the transducer sits in the load path and whether it includes accessory losses. For a road-speed study, distinguish wheel-derived speed from independent reference speed. For energy consumption, state whether energy was measured at the fuel tank, DC battery terminals, traction inverter, motor shaft, wheels or charging plug. Each is a legitimate measurement boundary but answers a different question.

Repeat the baseline at least three times if a safe, permitted test setup exists and record run-to-run variation. Do not “correct away” unexpected results without preserving raw data. Check for instrumentation drift and changes in ambient conditions. If the inferred result changes by only 2% while a controlling input is uncertain by 5%, no strong design conclusion follows. A professional test may need many more repeats, calibrated instruments, official procedures and suitable safety supervision. Where live-vehicle testing is unsafe or inaccessible, use a documented simulation and explicitly describe it as simulated evidence.

The unit case, **a loaded vehicle negotiating a sequence of turns at progressively higher speed**, creates a potential mismatch between local component optimization and total-vehicle performance. Altering the component associated with kinematic curvature and tire-force requirement may also change auxiliary energy, mass distribution, braking stability, thermal limits or occupant comfort. Compare not only the target metric but also at least one countervailing metric, and record any failure mode that could reverse the preferred option.

## 5. Engineering trade-off, model validity and evidence quality

The decision question is: should an engineer **choose speed during tests**? To answer, define the customer service and operating interval, not merely the most favorable individual test point. Identify at least one potential gain and one cost that arises elsewhere in the architecture. Examples of wider consequences include component heating, serviceability, traction and tire wear, acoustic discomfort, power-electronics limits, emissions performance, or degraded fail-safe behavior. A design change is defensible only after the relevant constraints are identified.

Assess risk independently from estimated average performance. Vehicle brakes, steering, high-voltage batteries, crash restraint systems and propulsion controls are safety critical; a spreadsheet or browser simulation cannot demonstrate a safe implementation. Use a hazard register that identifies the initiating event, potential harm, detection mechanism and intended safe response. Where local safety rules, official vehicle regulations or manufacturer procedures apply, do not substitute this lesson's approximate relations for required evidence. For other less hazardous classroom studies, the same discipline makes reasoning clearer and reproducible.

Preserve uncertainty visibly. A coefficient derived from one tire at one condition may not transfer to another tire or temperature. An idealized constant-efficiency map may work for initial sizing while being inadequate for drive-cycle optimization. A velocity or load measurement may be biased by sensor calibration. Rather than hide limitations, rank them by potential effect on the decision. This often reveals that a simpler model with explicit assumptions is more useful than a complex simulation whose inputs cannot be justified.

## 1. Physical model: meaning before arithmetic

A good handling model reproduces representative maneuvers without overfitting one steering event. Compare predicted and measured yaw rate, lateral acceleration and wheel steering, noting delays and sensor bias. Test dry/wet surfaces, empty/full load and speed conditions separately. ## 2. Derivation, variables and dimensional validation
