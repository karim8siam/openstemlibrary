# Unit 02: Longitudinal Vehicle Dynamics and Performance

**Course Code**: AUT 302 · **Level**: Advanced Undergraduate Engineering

## Unit Overview & Physical Foundations

This comprehensive university textbook unit covers the theoretical framework, physical modeling, mathematical governing equations, and engineering applications of **Longitudinal Vehicle Dynamics and Performance**.

# Unit 02, Lesson 01 — Whole-vehicle free-body analysis

**Learning objective.** Build a reproducible analysis of **force accounting on a consistent inertial basis**, then evaluate whether identify required tire force. The illustrative field case is **an accelerating delivery van**. The primary relation is `F_wheel - F_drag - F_roll - F_grade = m*a`; it is a starting approximation, not a complete calibration or certification method.

## 1. Physical model: meaning before arithmetic

A correct longitudinal free-body diagram identifies tire tractive force, aerodynamic resistance, rolling resistance and gravity projection. Include the effective translational inertia of rotating parts when accuracy requires it. Road slope and acceleration must use consistent positive direction. Newton’s second law relates the net force to mass times acceleration, while motor and tire limits separately cap achievable wheel force.

A vehicle's response is generated by several coupled subsystems. To study force accounting on a consistent inertial basis, isolate the relevant physical interaction without forgetting the boundary terms. Specify whether the driver, controller, road, tires and propulsion hardware are inputs to the calculation or the subject of the prediction. Treat the vehicle as a system with an initial state: speed, loading, gear or drive mode, road slope and environmental conditions can all alter the answer. When a component efficiency or friction coefficient enters the algebra, distinguish a measured characteristic from a nominal assumption. Otherwise the numerical output may appear precise while remaining only a scenario.

For an accelerating delivery van, draw a schematic with arrows indicating force, torque or energy transfer as appropriate. Put the chosen positive direction and sign convention on that diagram. The model is useful when its simplifications are consistent with the physical case; it is misleading when those assumptions are concealed. The critical mistake to prevent here is **adding grade resistance with the wrong sign**. Describe which term in the governing relation this mistake corrupts and how it would appear in a field measurement.

## 2. Derivation, variables and dimensional validation

Start with `F_wheel - F_drag - F_roll - F_grade = m*a`. Identify every physical quantity, its SI unit and whether the relation is a force balance, kinematic identity, empirical approximation or definition. Translate any speed in km/h into m/s before inserting it into a force or energy calculation. Rotational speed in revolutions per minute must be converted into rad/s for `power = torque × angular speed`; slope percentages must be converted to ratios or angles before applying trigonometric functions. Do not mix battery energy measured in kWh with instantaneous electric power measured in kW, and do not compare road-wheel output directly to fuel energy without the intervening conversion losses.

Dimensional homogeneity is a first check, but it is not enough. Consider the limit as an input tends to zero or as it reaches the largest range considered. Does the expression produce a physically sensible result? Would the equation imply negative absolute energy consumption, a wheel force greater than feasible traction, or an efficiency exceeding unity? If so, inspect the assumptions and numerical domain. Some models are not defined at standstill; others explicitly neglect slip, transient dynamics or temperature variation. The equation itself should not be forced outside the situation it describes.

For a careful calculation maintain a table with columns **symbol, definition, unit, nominal value, evidence source, plausible interval**. Recalculate the expression at at least two bounding input settings and evaluate which measurement would most reduce uncertainty. If inputs are correlated, simple independent sweeps provide only a sensitivity demonstration, not a statistically defensible confidence interval.

## 3. Worked engineering calculation

For mass 1700 kg, acceleration 1.5 m/s² and combined resistance 360 N, F_wheel = 1700×1.5 + 360 = 2910 N. This is a demand, not a claim that the vehicle can actually develop that tire force.

**Cross-check:** Reconstruct the result from the stated inputs without rounding intermediate values prematurely. Describe which term would change if the test were repeated with a new vehicle mass, ambient temperature or tire configuration. At least one check should concern the scale of the result: compare the derived force with vehicle weight, the energy with a known kinetic-energy change, or the speed with a plausible road operating point. A result can satisfy the formula exactly and still be inapplicable because one assumption is not true.

## 4. Field measurement plan and uncertainty

A defensible experiment for an accelerating delivery van starts with a stated question, the independent variable to vary, and the output to observe. Write down how each sensor is mounted, calibrated, sampled and time-synchronized. For a force or torque study, identify where the transducer sits in the load path and whether it includes accessory losses. For a road-speed study, distinguish wheel-derived speed from independent reference speed. For energy consumption, state whether energy was measured at the fuel tank, DC battery terminals, traction inverter, motor shaft, wheels or charging plug. Each is a legitimate measurement boundary but answers a different question.

Repeat the baseline at least three times if a safe, permitted test setup exists and record run-to-run variation. Do not “correct away” unexpected results without preserving raw data. Check for instrumentation drift and changes in ambient conditions. If the inferred result changes by only 2% while a controlling input is uncertain by 5%, no strong design conclusion follows. A professional test may need many more repeats, calibrated instruments, official procedures and suitable safety supervision. Where live-vehicle testing is unsafe or inaccessible, use a documented simulation and explicitly describe it as simulated evidence.

The unit case, **a delivery van carrying different payloads on a route with stops, hills and variable headwind**, creates a potential mismatch between local component optimization and total-vehicle performance. Altering the component associated with force accounting on a consistent inertial basis may also change auxiliary energy, mass distribution, braking stability, thermal limits or occupant comfort. Compare not only the target metric but also at least one countervailing metric, and record any failure mode that could reverse the preferred option.

## 5. Engineering trade-off, model validity and evidence quality

The decision question is: should an engineer **identify required tire force**? To answer, define the customer service and operating interval, not merely the most favorable individual test point. Identify at least one potential gain and one cost that arises elsewhere in the architecture. Examples of wider consequences include component heating, serviceability, traction and tire wear, acoustic discomfort, power-electronics limits, emissions performance, or degraded fail-safe behavior. A design change is defensible only after the relevant constraints are identified.

Assess risk independently from estimated average performance. Vehicle brakes, steering, high-voltage batteries, crash restraint systems and propulsion controls are safety critical; a spreadsheet or browser simulation cannot demonstrate a safe implementation. Use a hazard register that identifies the initiating event, potential harm, detection mechanism and intended safe response. Where local safety rules, official vehicle regulations or manufacturer procedures apply, do not substitute this lesson's approximate relations for required evidence. For other less hazardous classroom studies, the same discipline makes reasoning clearer and reproducible.

Preserve uncertainty visibly. A coefficient derived from one tire at one condition may not transfer to another tire or temperature. An idealized constant-efficiency map may work for initial sizing while being inadequate for drive-cycle optimization. A velocity or load measurement may be biased by sensor calibration. Rather than hide limitations, rank them by potential effect on the decision. This often reveals that a simpler model with explicit assumptions is more useful than a complex simulation whose inputs cannot be justified.

## 6. Exercises, independent checks and design questions

1. **Derivation.** Reconstruct `F_wheel - F_drag - F_roll - F_grade = m*a` from its physical assumptions and check its SI dimensions. State one condition in which the expression is invalid.
2. **Numerical sensitivity.** Recompute the worked example with a ±10% change in its dominant uncertain variable, holding the others fixed. Report absolute and percentage changes; discuss why a 10% input change need not produce a 10% output change.
3. **Diagnosis.** In an accelerating delivery van, an instrument gives a value inconsistent with the model. Propose one physical cause, one calibration or unit-conversion fault, and one model-boundary error. Explain how independent evidence would distinguish them.
4. **Design choice.** Compare a conservative and an aggressive configuration for force accounting on a consistent inertial basis. State the criteria by which you would select between them rather than ranking them by one attractive scalar.
5. **Safety and communication.** Write 200 words about the most important non-modelled hazard, the professional test or standard needed, and what you would refuse to conclude from this educational calculation.

**Answer-check criteria.** A complete numerical answer states the substituted input values, units, rounded final value and input ranges. A reasoning answer should identify a falsifiable measurement, not just say “more testing is required.” A safety answer must name the affected subsystem and a hazard or control requirement.

## 7. Applied investigation and reproducible report

Design a small simulation experiment about force accounting on a consistent inertial basis using an explicitly stated parameter interval that includes the nominal values in this lesson. Vary one input while recording outputs and any violated constraints, then repeat after changing a second input to identify interaction effects. Include at least one scenario drawn from a delivery van carrying different payloads on a route with stops, hills and variable headwind. Compare the change in a local metric with the expected consequence for the whole vehicle. Plot values with real units rather than arbitrary index labels, and explain which observations would require a higher-fidelity dynamic model. Finally, write a short research note that separates **model evidence**, **synthetic examples**, **published background information**, and **actual measurements**; do not merge these evidence categories.

## 1. Physical model: meaning before arithmetic

Energy is the time integral of power, not merely a steady power multiplied by total trip duration when speed varies. Partition the cycle into acceleration, steady cruise, grade and braking. Positive wheel work comes from propulsion; negative work is partly dissipated and only partially recoverable depending on power limits and battery state. A vehicle's response is generated by several coupled subsystems.
