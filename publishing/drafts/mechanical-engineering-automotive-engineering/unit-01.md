# Unit 01, Lesson 01 — Subsystem architecture and engineering requirements

**Learning objective.** Build a reproducible analysis of **interfaces among packaging, loads, safety, service and propulsion**, then evaluate whether trade packaging flexibility against weight and structural margins. The illustrative field case is **comparing two body architectures**. The primary relation is `System mass = sum of component and payload masses`; it is a starting approximation, not a complete calibration or certification method.

## 1. Physical model: meaning before arithmetic

A change in traction battery location shifts axle loading, center of gravity and crash structure. Engineering requirements should have a measurable condition, allowable tolerance and method of verification. A requirement for “excellent acceleration” is incomplete; specify payload, speed interval, road grade, temperature and acceptance instrument. Decompose the top-level target to subsystems but retain cross-interface constraints.

A vehicle's response is generated by several coupled subsystems. To study interfaces among packaging, loads, safety, service and propulsion, isolate the relevant physical interaction without forgetting the boundary terms. Specify whether the driver, controller, road, tires and propulsion hardware are inputs to the calculation or the subject of the prediction. Treat the vehicle as a system with an initial state: speed, loading, gear or drive mode, road slope and environmental conditions can all alter the answer. When a component efficiency or friction coefficient enters the algebra, distinguish a measured characteristic from a nominal assumption. Otherwise the numerical output may appear precise while remaining only a scenario.

For comparing two body architectures, draw a schematic with arrows indicating force, torque or energy transfer as appropriate. Put the chosen positive direction and sign convention on that diagram. The model is useful when its simplifications are consistent with the physical case; it is misleading when those assumptions are concealed. The critical mistake to prevent here is **double-counting batteries or cargo**. Describe which term in the governing relation this mistake corrupts and how it would appear in a field measurement.

## 2. Derivation, variables and dimensional validation

Start with `System mass = sum of component and payload masses`. Identify every physical quantity, its SI unit and whether the relation is a force balance, kinematic identity, empirical approximation or definition. Translate any speed in km/h into m/s before inserting it into a force or energy calculation. Rotational speed in revolutions per minute must be converted into rad/s for `power = torque × angular speed`; slope percentages must be converted to ratios or angles before applying trigonometric functions. Do not mix battery energy measured in kWh with instantaneous electric power measured in kW, and do not compare road-wheel output directly to fuel energy without the intervening conversion losses.

Dimensional homogeneity is a first check, but it is not enough. Consider the limit as an input tends to zero or as it reaches the largest range considered. Does the expression produce a physically sensible result? Would the equation imply negative absolute energy consumption, a wheel force greater than feasible traction, or an efficiency exceeding unity? If so, inspect the assumptions and numerical domain. ## 3. Worked engineering calculation

An illustrative 1180 kg body and chassis, 240 kg propulsion and 130 kg fluids and accessories produce 1550 kg curb mass; with 250 kg occupants/cargo, test mass becomes 1800 kg. The distinction prevents computing acceleration using curb mass while measuring consumption with full payload.

Compare not only the target metric but also at least one countervailing metric, and record any failure mode that could reverse the preferred option.

## 5. Engineering trade-off, model validity and evidence quality

The decision question is: should an engineer **trade packaging flexibility against weight and structural margins**? To answer, define the customer service and operating interval, not merely the most favorable individual test point. Identify at least one potential gain and one cost that arises elsewhere in the architecture. Examples of wider consequences include component heating, serviceability, traction and tire wear, acoustic discomfort, power-electronics limits, emissions performance, or degraded fail-safe behavior. A design change is defensible only after the relevant constraints are identified.

Assess risk independently from estimated average performance. Vehicle brakes, steering, high-voltage batteries, crash restraint systems and propulsion controls are safety critical; a spreadsheet or browser simulation cannot demonstrate a safe implementation. Use a hazard register that identifies the initiating event, potential harm, detection mechanism and intended safe response. Where local safety rules, official vehicle regulations or manufacturer procedures apply, do not substitute this lesson's approximate relations for required evidence. For other less hazardous classroom studies, the same discipline makes reasoning clearer and reproducible.

Preserve uncertainty visibly. A coefficient derived from one tire at one condition may not transfer to another tire or temperature. An idealized constant-efficiency map may work for initial sizing while being inadequate for drive-cycle optimization. A velocity or load measurement may be biased by sensor calibration. Rather than hide limitations, rank them by potential effect on the decision. This often reveals that a simpler model with explicit assumptions is more useful than a complex simulation whose inputs cannot be justified.

## 1. Physical model: meaning before arithmetic
