§2.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Catalyst Deactivation and Kinetic Characterization

Learning outcomes and model hierarchy

This unit addresses activity evolution, experimental protocols and regeneration limits. The four lessons are Poisoning and reversible inhibition; Sintering, coke growth and pore blockage; Time-on-stream reactor performance; Regeneration and experimental design. The engineering case that ties them together is How catalyst lifecycle changes which rate is observable. A useful design decision requires at least three layers: a physical hypothesis, a mathematical prediction and a test that could disprove it. The layers must remain distinct. A correct balance is often necessary but not sufficient, because it can hold for several competing closures. A correct curve fit can likewise hide a poor mechanistic description if the experiment covers only a narrow range of conditions.

Start by defining the system of interest, its relevant time or axial coordinate, and how molar quantities are measured. For each rate or transfer term identify whether it is based on catalyst mass, geometric volume, interfacial area or pore volume. A common classroom error is to combine coefficients measured on different bases without conversion. Another is to attribute an observed outlet signal to one physical mechanism while two mechanisms respond to the same operating change. Select perturbations that move one resistance more strongly than another, and document which quantities remain controlled.

Derivation map

Write the differential species balance in a form that shows the measurable accumulation and the net production or disappearance. Next insert a constitutive closure for the local reaction or flux. Reduce the system only after checking whether heat and pressure changes can alter the closure. When a pseudo-steady simplification is imposed, explain which fast timescale justifies it. When an ideal mixing or plug-flow simplification is imposed, identify what a residence-time measurement would look like if it were valid. Finally, specify conditions at the inlet, outlet, pellet boundary or initial time, whichever coordinates are present.

Plot the resulting solution alongside a limiting expression. Limiting expressions are powerful error detectors: very fast diffusion should recover the intrinsic catalyst rate; zero catalyst activity should give no catalytic disappearance; zero pressure loss should recover a constant-pressure bed; infinite mixing stages should approach plug flow at fixed mean residence time. But a limit check does not validate behavior at intermediate conditions. Test parameter variations and report cases where the simplified model ceases to be defensible.

Engineering case study

Imagine a plant trial whose only recorded output is percentage conversion. The engineer modifies the variable that seems most relevant to the current mechanism and sees conversion improve. Before treating the change as evidence of a faster intrinsic reaction, inspect reactor hold-up, possible flow bypass, reactant dilution, bed temperature, catalyst mass and analyzer calibration. If two candidate mechanisms produce the same conversion change, design an auxiliary measurement that responds differently, such as temperature gradients, tracer distributions, surface coverage, pellet-size sensitivity or pressure loss.

For a first-stage screening calculation, hold inlet molar flow constant and construct an ideal first-order reference prediction. For a second stage, add the principal mechanism from the unit and see how the sensitivity changes. For a third stage, include at least one alternate mechanism. State exactly which observable distinguishes the models; if none does, present parameter combinations or bounds rather than asserting unique values. A validated model should reproduce conditions not used for its calibration. A visually convincing curve is not sufficient.

Analysis workflow and study questions

  1. Prepare a labeled schematic of the physical boundary and three essential measurements.
  2. Write the governing rate or flux equation with units for each coefficient.
  3. State the conditions under which the selected limiting approximation becomes exact.
  4. Construct a small sensitivity table for low, middle and high values of one operating variable.
  5. Compare an intrinsic-kinetic explanation with a transport-based explanation of the same outlet trend.
  6. Estimate an input uncertainty and propagate its effect into at least one output.
  7. Describe what independent data would be most useful before scale-up.
  8. Identify the most likely safety or operability consequence of a false model assumption.

Editorial note: This is educational material; safety decisions, equipment approval and industrial operation require validated physical-property, kinetic, pressure and heat-transfer data as well as applicable standards.

§2.2 Unit 2, Lesson 1: Poisoning and reversible inhibition

Unit 2, Lesson 1: Poisoning and reversible inhibition

Unit focus: Catalyst Deactivation and Kinetic Characterization. Theory bridge: Activity evolution, experimental protocols and regeneration limits.

Why the concept matters

The essential challenge of poisoning and reversible inhibition is to decide what the observed data can actually identify. The specific concepts are poison adsorption; reversible coverage; permanent site loss. A schematic that shows a catalyst, a pump and a temperature sensor does not tell us whether intrinsic kinetics or transport is limiting. Begin by writing the species inventory and defining the reference conditions. Activity is usually defined as the ratio of the observed rate to the fresh catalyst rate at the same reference composition and temperature. Without holding these conditions consistent, declining conversion may be misattributed to a catalyst instead of a feed disturbance. Ask which quantity remains conserved, which process provides the driving force and which resistance may control the observed response. Treat a fitted parameter as an operational inference until a second experimental condition tests it.

Governing model, variables and assumptions

Governing expression: a(t)=exp(-k_d t). Read every quantity with its units and measurement basis. In the current lesson the observable is activity-normalized rates, the control variable is time on stream, and the representative parameter interval is 0–60 h. Differentiate between an ideal local constitutive relationship and the integrated outlet result; the latter also contains hydrodynamics and operating conditions. Construct the state and boundary conditions before using any solver. A solver can only enforce the equations supplied to it, not discover missing physics.

A useful systematic derivation starts with accumulation = input − output + generation. For heterogeneous systems, include the site balance and the diffusion flux before imposing pseudo-steady assumptions. If a surface site is occupied, its fraction is unavailable to competing adsorption. If a species crosses a porous pellet, the molar flux is a gradient-driven contribution; a zero-flux center condition expresses symmetry, not an infinite diffusion coefficient. These distinctions are especially important when an equation calibrated in a small batch reactor is applied to a continuous packed bed.

Detailed engineering example

Experiment scenario. During a 12-hour test, collect rate and temperature together; compare activity estimates based on normalized intrinsic rates instead of merely averaging conversion values. The physical equipment is a long-duration catalyst test. As an initial reference check, consider an ideal first-order disappearance benchmark k=0.254 min⁻¹ and residence time τ=2.250 min at fixed density and temperature. The baseline ideal conversion is X=1−exp(−kτ)=0.4353. Increasing residence time by 25% while holding the coefficient constant gives X=0.5105, a gain of 7.52 percentage points, not a 25% increase in conversion. This reference is only for detecting numerical and interpretive mistakes; the actual topic-specific result must use the governing expression above. The two calculations separate a normalized response check from the real mechanistic model.

Validation worksheet: first calculate the dimensionless kτ=0.5715. Verify 0≤X≤1. Reproduce the exponential by hand to at least four significant digits. For a second check double the coefficient but halve residence time; the product kτ and the ideal first-order conversion must remain unchanged. If a numerical routine violates this invariance, investigate inconsistent time units, coordinate transformation, integration step and floating-point truncation. In the full poisoning and reversible inhibition problem, record the analogous dimensionless group, whether it comes from adsorption, diffusion, energy or flow.

Interpreting a controlled experiment

What can be concluded? A controlled change in time on stream should be examined against its predicted effect on activity-normalized rates. Compare at least three levels spanning 0–60 h and repeat the center point to detect drift. Plot the response with dimensional axes and also normalize by a physically appropriate reference value. Report the slope in the low-input region, identify any saturation or reversal, and ask whether variable inlet composition could mimic that shape. If a nonlinear model is used, inspect residuals across the independent variable rather than quoting only fit quality. A numerical solution is meaningful only if the feed, units, boundary conditions and kinetic basis have remained internally consistent.

Use a model-comparison table with columns for measured values, instrument uncertainty, ideal-model prediction, alternative physical hypothesis and residual. The correct mechanism cannot be selected merely because its plot looks smoother. Before using a fitted parameter in reactor design, estimate the domain over which it is supported. Ask whether the calibration data span enough variation to distinguish a surface limitation from a change in active inventory, gas density, or hydrodynamic residence time. When two models are observationally indistinguishable, document that ambiguity rather than selecting one without evidence.

Diagnostics, uncertainty and model limits

Sensitivity and failure checks. At twelve hours, an illustrative exponential deactivation model with kd=0.015 h⁻¹ has activity exp(−kd t)=0.8353. This is an independently stated diagnostic check, not a derived kinetic law for poisoning and reversible inhibition. Recompute the topic result if the relevant transport coefficient, surface area or rate constant changes by ±20%. If changing a physically positive parameter produces a negative concentration, negative conversion or conservation error, reject the result before interpreting the chart. Do not extrapolate a short-time exponential activity model through an unobserved regeneration cycle. Include measured input uncertainty rather than plotting smooth predictions as though they are observations.

A defensible engineering report should include both the nominal calculation and a counterexample. The nominal calculation uses a specified set of conditions; the counterexample changes an assumption that is often hidden. Record at least one possible instrument bias, one missing transport resistance and one reason a laboratory result may fail at commercial scale. Explain the sign of each sensitivity using the physics, then check whether the numerical result follows the expected sign. This practice catches many implementation mistakes before a model is shared with learners.

Worked investigation and review

  1. Explain why a change in time on stream could change activity-normalized rates without changing the intrinsic rate constant. State a controlled test that separates the two effects.
  2. Using a(t)=exp(-k_d t), identify which parameters are dimensioned. Show the units needed to make the output consistent with the declared rate or transport basis.
  3. For the long-duration catalyst test, draw a boundary, label all mass and energy flows, and list one measurable physical state that would falsify the simplified model.
  4. Compare the effect of variable inlet composition with a 20% intrinsic-kinetic perturbation. Under what conditions might the two mechanisms look identical in an outlet signal?
  5. Propose a calibration and an independent validation run spanning 0–60 h. Which single measurement has the highest value for identifying the dominant resistance?

Answer framework: Each explanation should begin with a statement of the controlling balance and what is held constant, then show a dimensional relation, a calculated or plotted response and a clearly labeled conclusion. A claim of mass-transfer control must be tied to transport-sensitive experiments rather than solely to a fit. A claim of thermal stability must identify the sign of dynamic net-heat response, not only a steady intersection. A claim that a reactor is well mixed must be supported by a tracer result and a justified microscale mixing model when kinetics are nonlinear.

§2.6 Process Simulation & Industrial Design Case: Catalyst Deactivation and Kinetic Characterization

Unit 2 Project — Catalyst Deactivation and Kinetic Characterization

Deliverable

Create an engineering notebook that compares at least two models for activity evolution, experimental protocols and regeneration limits. Define a physical system, specify operating inputs and use either published synthetic benchmark data or explicitly labeled generated observations. Identify which data are used to fit the model and reserve a separate condition for validation. Report the parameter values, uncertainty assumptions, plots, conservation residuals and a reasoned design recommendation.

Minimum experimental plan
