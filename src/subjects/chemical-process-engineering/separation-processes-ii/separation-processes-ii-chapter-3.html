§3.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Adsorption Equilibrium, Fixed Beds and Ion Exchange

Engineering context

A packed bed treats a feed with intermittent contaminant spikes. A batch isotherm shows high equilibrium capacity, yet the effluent exceeds the specification sooner than expected. This is compatible with slow intraparticle diffusion, channeling or competing constituents rather than evidence the isotherm is wrong. Bed depth, flow, temperature and regeneration history should be documented. A service period must be scheduled to a leakage criterion, not the nominal equilibrium loading of every gram of media.

Unit learning outcomes

  1. Develop and check adsorption equilibrium and selectivity using q_e=q_max K C/(1+K C); identify the principal assumptions and explain an experimental validation path.
  2. Develop and check adsorption kinetics and film versus pore transport using dq/dt=k_LDF(q_star-q); identify the principal assumptions and explain an experimental validation path.
  3. Develop and check fixed-bed breakthrough and regeneration using C_out/C_in=1/[1+exp(k(t_50-t))]; identify the principal assumptions and explain an experimental validation path.
  4. Develop and check ion-exchange capacity and exhaustion using Q_use=m_resin q_eq eta_use; identify the principal assumptions and explain an experimental validation path.

Concept map

1. Adsorption equilibrium and selectivity

Langmuir’s isotherm posits equivalent adsorption sites and a monolayer-like saturation capacity. It is often a useful fitting equation even when its physical assumptions are imperfect. When concentration is low, the isotherm is approximately linear; at high concentration it approaches a maximum loading. An equilibrium fit does not predict uptake rate or packed-bed breakthrough. Separate the loading units, such as mg/g sorbent, from the equilibrium concentration units, such as mg/L solution.

Central relation: q_e=q_max K C/(1+K C). Operating boundary: These coefficients represent a specified adsorbent–solute–temperature combination; do not transfer them to another chemistry without data.

2. Adsorption kinetics and film versus pore transport

A linear-driving-force approximation replaces detailed diffusion in irregular porous particles with an effective rate constant. It can describe a measured uptake transient but does not uniquely identify the actual resistance. If the external film changes with mixing intensity, the fitted effective constant may change; if pore diffusion dominates, particle radius can have a stronger effect. Always inspect the early-time and late-time portions of kinetic data before extrapolating.

Central relation: dq/dt=k_LDF(q_star-q). Operating boundary: Single-exponential uptake assumes constant equilibrium concentration; coupled batch depletion needs a liquid-phase balance.

3. Fixed-bed breakthrough and regeneration

The logistic curve is a convenient empirical representation of a breakthrough front. It should not be mistaken for a universal solution to axial dispersion and adsorption kinetics. The front steepness is affected by the feed concentration, flow, bed length, pellet size, and isotherm nonlinearity. Breakthrough criteria are generally lower than complete exhaustion. Design around an allowable outlet concentration and a credible safety factor; regeneration efficiency and cycle scheduling determine effective capacity.

Central relation: C_out/C_in=1/[1+exp(k(t_50-t))]. Operating boundary: A logistic front does not resolve multicomponent displacement, irreversible fouling or changing adsorption temperature.

4. Ion-exchange capacity and exhaustion

Ion-exchange resins are rated on charge equivalents rather than a universal mass removal per gram. For hardness removal, divalent ion moles consume twice as many equivalents as monovalent ions. Working capacity is smaller than theoretical capacity because of leakage, incomplete regeneration and nonuniform utilization. Resin swelling, osmotic shock, fouling and chemical compatibility set additional constraints. Mass balances must include counterions leaving the resin, not just the target ion being removed.

Central relation: Q_use=m_resin q_eq eta_use. Operating boundary: The model ignores mass-transfer zone leakage and competitive species; verify bed performance experimentally.

What to test experimentally

Establish the basis. A useful calculation begins with a consistent basis: mass rate, molar rate, solution volume, dry adsorbent mass or solvent mass. For equipment sizing a rate must not be confused with a cumulative inventory. Record every conversion as a separate line. Does the result represent an instantaneous rate, a time-integrated amount, a concentration, or a dimensionless ratio?

Identify equilibrium and rate assumptions. Equilibrium constrains an attainable composition or loading; kinetics determines how quickly a process approaches that constraint. Where an empirical coefficient is used, report its calibration range, units and experimental provenance. Which measured observation would show that an assumed equilibrium relation is not applicable?

Connect the model to a unit operation. A local model should be embedded within balances, operating constraints and product targets. Report useful recovery, purity and waste flows on comparable bases rather than optimizing a single local response. Which outlet becomes the limiting waste stream when product recovery increases?

Test uncertainty and reversal. If two alternatives are close, measurement error may reverse their apparent ranking. Estimate the sensitivity of performance to the largest uncertain inputs, and check that the conclusion survives a credible parameter range. Could an operating choice preferred at the reference feed become inferior when feed quality changes?
