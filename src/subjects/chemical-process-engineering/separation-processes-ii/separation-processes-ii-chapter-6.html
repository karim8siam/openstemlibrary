§6.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Mechanical Separations, Filtration and Clarification

Engineering context

A slurry enters a filter where pressure is increased to speed liquid recovery. A compressible cake becomes denser and its resistance rises, leaving the overall throughput almost unchanged. Separately, fine solids may settle too slowly for a gravity clarifier even though they have higher density than water. Particle-size measurements, settling tests and filtration curves identify whether the best intervention is flocculation, media selection, filter area, centrifugation or changing the upstream particle-production step.

Unit learning outcomes

  1. Develop and check cake filtration and membrane-prefilter design using t/V=mu alpha C V/(2 A^2 DeltaP)+mu R_m/(A DeltaP); identify the principal assumptions and explain an experimental validation path.
  2. Develop and check sedimentation, thickening and clarifier loading using v_t=(rho_p-rho_f)g d^2/(18 mu); identify the principal assumptions and explain an experimental validation path.
  3. Develop and check centrifugation and accelerated settling using RCF=omega^2 r/g; identify the principal assumptions and explain an experimental validation path.
  4. Develop and check fluidization and particle classification using DeltaP=(rho_p-rho_f)(1-epsilon_mf)L_mf g; identify the principal assumptions and explain an experimental validation path.

Concept map

1. Cake filtration and membrane-prefilter design

The constant-pressure filtration relation predicts a curved filtrate-volume trajectory because cake resistance increases as solids accumulate. Filterability cannot be estimated from solids loading alone: particle-size distribution, compressibility, filter aid, viscosity and medium resistance matter. In highly compressible cakes specific resistance may increase with pressure, so increasing pressure does not yield a proportional throughput gain. Perform a bench-scale t/V versus V analysis before extrapolating to production.

Central relation: t/V=mu alpha C V/(2 A^2 DeltaP)+mu R_m/(A DeltaP). Operating boundary: The basic model assumes incompressible cakes and constant viscosity and solid loading.

2. Sedimentation, thickening and clarifier loading

Stokes settling is valid when particle Reynolds numbers are small and particles can be approximated as isolated spheres. Flocculated particles may be irregular, porous and concentration dependent, making hindered settling and compression important. Surface overflow rate is a useful clarifier screening criterion but short-circuiting and turbulence affect capture. Use jar tests, settling columns and field observations for equipment decisions.

Central relation: v_t=(rho_p-rho_f)g d^2/(18 mu). Operating boundary: No hindered settling, turbulence or nonspherical drag correction is included.

3. Centrifugation and accelerated settling

Centrifuge specifications often quote relative centrifugal force at a particular radius, which matters because acceleration varies across the bowl. High RCF alone does not determine separation efficiency: residence time, pool depth, solids concentration and dynamic viscosity also matter. An industrial centrifuge must handle solids discharge, heat and erosion as well as particle capture. Extrapolation from bench centrifuge runs requires dimensionless reasoning.

Central relation: RCF=omega^2 r/g. Operating boundary: The RCF expression is a force comparison; it is not a particle-cut-size or retention-time design equation.

4. Fluidization and particle classification

At incipient fluidization, the upward fluid force approximately balances the effective weight of the bed. Increasing gas velocity beyond minimum fluidization generally expands the bed rather than raising pressure drop indefinitely. Real beds may channel, slug, bubble or entrain particles. Minimum fluidization velocity must be related to particle size, density and fluid properties; a weight-balance plot alone cannot calculate it.

Central relation: DeltaP=(rho_p-rho_f)(1-epsilon_mf)L_mf g. Operating boundary: The simplified bed pressure balance ignores distributor loss and wall effects.

What to test experimentally

Establish the basis. A useful calculation begins with a consistent basis: mass rate, molar rate, solution volume, dry adsorbent mass or solvent mass. For equipment sizing a rate must not be confused with a cumulative inventory. Record every conversion as a separate line. Does the result represent an instantaneous rate, a time-integrated amount, a concentration, or a dimensionless ratio?

Identify equilibrium and rate assumptions. Equilibrium constrains an attainable composition or loading; kinetics determines how quickly a process approaches that constraint. Where an empirical coefficient is used, report its calibration range, units and experimental provenance. Which measured observation would show that an assumed equilibrium relation is not applicable?

Connect the model to a unit operation. A local model should be embedded within balances, operating constraints and product targets. Report useful recovery, purity and waste flows on comparable bases rather than optimizing a single local response. Which outlet becomes the limiting waste stream when product recovery increases?

Test uncertainty and reversal. If two alternatives are close, measurement error may reverse their apparent ranking. Estimate the sensitivity of performance to the largest uncertain inputs, and check that the conclusion survives a credible parameter range. Could an operating choice preferred at the reference feed become inferior when feed quality changes?

A slurry enters a filter where pressure is increased to speed liquid recovery. A compressible cake becomes denser and its resistance rises, leaving the overall throughput almost unchanged. Deliverables and evidence

  2. A labeled process diagram naming the physical location of the driving force and the location of the main measurement.
  3. A one-page model statement with every coefficient, unit, validity range and inferred rather than measured property.
