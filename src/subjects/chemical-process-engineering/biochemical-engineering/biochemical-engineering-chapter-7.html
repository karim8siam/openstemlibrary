§7.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Scale-Up Criteria and Process Analytical Technology

Purpose. Investigate why geometric similarity fails to preserve mixing, oxygen transfer, temperature, and biological performance simultaneously.

Entry knowledge. Students should be able to balance mass and energy, work with differential equations, check dimensional consistency and interpret experimental uncertainty. Prior biological experience helps but is not assumed. The models are deliberately simplified; scientific claims require the limitations in each lesson to be stated.

  4. [Process analytics, sensors and model validation](../lessons/unit-07-lesson-04.html) — [related numerical lab](../labs/lab-21.html)

Practise and demonstrate competence

  • [Unit 07 assessment: 16 multi-part tasks](../assessments/unit-07.html) tests physical reasoning, calculation, falsification and design decision-making.
  • [Unit 07 applied project](../projects/unit-07.html) requires an evidence ledger, assumption register, data table, quantitative check and defensible conclusion.
  • [Laboratory manual](../LABORATORY-MANUAL.html) indexes the offline simulations and their modeling restrictions.

Outcomes and publication boundary

At the end of this unit, students should explain why the chosen equation is appropriate, reproduce at least one numerical prediction, describe a test that could disprove it, and articulate the information missing for a real process decision. No numerical curve in this resource is an industrial operating recommendation. All sample coefficients and operating points are fictional classroom inputs unless a source is specifically identified.

§7.2 Engineering Topic 7.2: Unit 7 · Lesson 1 — Geometric similarity and dimensionless analysis

Unit 7 · Lesson 1 — Geometric similarity and dimensionless analysis

[Unit 07 overview](../units/unit-07.html) · [Run numerical laboratory 19](../labs/lab-19.html) · [Assessment](../assessments/unit-07.html) · [Applied project](../projects/unit-07.html)

The engineering question

Scaling reactor geometry by one length ratio changes surface area as length squared but volume as length cubed. Consequently, maintaining the same reactor shape does not preserve cooling area per volume. Dimensionless groups expose which mechanisms are being matched: Reynolds number characterizes inertial-to-viscous ratios, power number characterizes impeller power under defined conditions, and gas-loading groups matter in aerated tanks. A scale-up criterion must name the physical phenomenon it intends to preserve.

The larger aim of Scale-up and process analytics is to answer this question: Investigate why geometric similarity fails to preserve mixing, oxygen transfer, temperature, and biological performance simultaneously. A result must be interpreted as a statement about a specified physical boundary and observed quantities. Distinguish the underlying biological mechanism from the mathematical convenience that makes a calculation tractable. A model can be dimensionally consistent and still be badly chosen for the organism, medium or scale. Conversely, a complicated simulator is not superior unless its extra parameters can be identified and tested with observations.

A useful model and its assumptions

Working relation: For geometrically similar vessels, V₂/V₁=(D₂/D₁)³ and A₂/A₁=(D₂/D₁)²; thus (A/V)₂/(A/V)₁=D₁/D₂.

The displayed relationship is a starting point for reasoning. Before substituting numbers, name every term, give the unit and decide whether each quantity is a measured value, a fitted coefficient, or a controlled setting. Conservation laws are independent of constitutive assumptions, while kinetic and transfer coefficients depend on conditions. Make a sketch of the relevant system boundary and show what enters, accumulates and leaves. State whether the rate is per unit liquid volume, per unit catalyst or per unit cell mass; confusing these bases can lead to order-of-magnitude errors even when individual values seem realistic.

When a relationship contains an exponential, check which variable is integrated in the exponent and whether the exponent is dimensionless. When it contains a ratio, check what happens as the denominator approaches a limiting condition. When it describes an observable signal, identify the calibration that connects that signal to the claimed physical quantity. These checks are not substitutes for experiment, but they reject many wrong models before fitting begins.

Worked investigation with synthetic numbers

Doubling vessel diameter multiplies volume by eight but surface area by four, halving the ideal area-to-volume ratio. Matching a small-scale temperature trajectory then becomes harder without additional heat-transfer equipment.

Reconstruction task. Rewrite the stated computation as a ledger: input variable and unit, conversion used, intermediate result and final quantity. Show the same calculation with one input perturbed by 10% while retaining the others, and determine whether the output response is approximately proportional, nonlinear, or sensitive to a constraint. Then reverse the question: what input accuracy would be needed to distinguish the two predictions using a real instrument? Avoid claiming more significant figures than the chosen coefficients justify.

The example is intentionally synthetic. It should teach the direction and scale of a response, not imply that a specific organism or process will exhibit these numerical coefficients. If the result suggests substantial accumulation, ask where the required mass and energy originate. If it suggests extreme sensitivity, inspect whether the sensitivity arises from a genuine mechanistic feature or from mathematically approaching a singular assumption.

Measurement design and engineering judgment

Show the scaling criterion and what is sacrificed. State geometric assumptions, viscosity regime, transport demand and motor limits. Avoid scaling a power or transfer correlation beyond the conditions where it has been verified.

Decide whether geometry, fluid regime and impeller arrangement really match before applying scaling exponents. Include limitations from baffles, spargers and multi-impeller trains.

A defensible evidence plan identifies a process objective, measured signals, their frequency, instrument or assay uncertainty, and a comparison criterion selected before viewing the data. Include control conditions and independent replicates when the biological material varies across runs. Distinguish calibration measurements from hold-out verification data. If the main parameter is estimated from one narrow operating window, do not assume it will remain identifiable when oxygen, temperature, shear, pH or medium composition changes.

In a design review, explain the alternatives rather than selecting a model because it yields the highest projected titer or lowest cost. A common problem is conflating process capacity with process utilization: an available transfer rate is not automatically used, and biological demand can be low because the culture is inhibited. When a prediction is contradicted, investigate both the experiment and the model, and keep a record of the resulting revision.

Where the simple model fails

Geometric similarity does not guarantee biochemical similarity; cell physiology responds to spatial gradients, residence time and shear.

A classroom model is especially fragile when extrapolated beyond its parameter-identification range. Carefully distinguish structural uncertainty (missing mechanism or wrong equation), parameter uncertainty (imprecisely estimated coefficient), and measurement uncertainty (sensor or assay error). Running many simulations with one incorrect equation does not resolve structural uncertainty. A useful sensitivity chart should therefore accompany a stated model boundary, not pretend to replace it.

For an independent verification, perform three checks: units and conservation; a limiting condition with a known qualitative answer; and a second calculation method or external measurement. Explain what each check can and cannot demonstrate. Automated agreement with an equation confirms the implementation, not necessarily the equation's suitability for a real reactor.

Guided laboratory and discussion

Open [Laboratory 19](../labs/lab-19.html). Record the default input values with units. Move the first slider to three equally spaced settings while leaving other controls unchanged; save the response to CSV and compare the graph with the governing relation in that laboratory. Then change a second independent parameter to test whether the original trend is stable. The laboratory describes its physical limits and reports an explicit warning where assumptions fail. The export is generated locally; no data leave the browser.

Investigative prompt: Use a dimensional audit to challenge a proposed single-criterion scale-up, explaining which additional similarity groups are sacrificed.

Interpretation prompt: Identify a response that the model cannot predict, and propose one targeted measurement needed to decide if the omission matters. Write a two-sentence decision: what is supported by the simulation and what remains unproven. A high-grade response distinguishes a mathematical identity, an observation and a contingent engineering inference.

Lesson check

  1. State the modeled system boundary, the type of measurable quantity being predicted and the appropriate units.
  2. Derive or reconstruct the stated relation from a rate law or conservation balance, and show a limiting case.
  3. Reproduce the worked calculation, then vary one input and interpret sensitivity without generalizing outside the stated model.
  4. Identify two independent measurements that could falsify the simplified hypothesis.
  5. Present a recommendation that is explicitly conditional on the evidence rather than an unsupported production specification.

§7.3 Engineering Topic 7.3: Unit 7 · Lesson 2 — Power density, tip speed and mixing time

Unit 7 · Lesson 2 — Power density, tip speed and mixing time

[Unit 07 overview](../units/unit-07.html) · [Run numerical laboratory 20](../labs/lab-20.html) · [Assessment](../assessments/unit-07.html) · [Applied project](../projects/unit-07.html)

The engineering question

An impeller speed that maintains tip speed as vessel diameter rises is different from one that maintains power per liquid volume. Under geometric similarity and constant turbulent power number, constant tip speed means N∝D⁻¹ and P/V∝D⁻¹; constant P/V instead implies N∝D⁻²ᐟ³ and tip speed increases with D¹ᐟ³. Neither condition automatically preserves mixing time, gas hold-up or cell stress. Multi-impeller vessels and gas loading introduce additional changes.

Unit 7 · Lesson 3 — Oxygen and cooling capacity in large vessels

The engineering question

At larger scale, oxygen delivery and heat rejection can become binding constraints even if average biomass growth resembles pilot data. Oxygen transfer may vary nonlinearly with agitation and gas flow; pressure changes gas solubility while mixing gradients expose cells to different conditions. Heat removal generally competes with reducing surface-area-to-volume ratio, and a robust design must consider startup, peak demand and maximum credible agitation conditions. Risk accumulates when both oxygen limitation and heat generation rise with biomass.

§7.6 Process Simulation & Industrial Design Case: Scale-Up Criteria and Process Analytical Technology

Unit 07 — Applied engineering project: Scale-up and process analytics

Status: fictional teaching case; all experimental measurements, process values and costs must be supplied or explicitly marked synthetic. This is not an authorization to conduct biological culture, run equipment, release a therapeutic or discharge wastewater.

Project brief

A geometrically similar design increases impeller diameter from 0.20 m to 1.0 m. The group is asked to preserve cell viability, oxygen delivery and thermal performance, even though the three targets are not controlled by the same scale-up criterion.

The task is not to maximize a single simulated variable. It is to demonstrate that an engineering conclusion rests on a defensible boundary, conserved quantities, experimentally testable assumptions, and explicit uncertainty. Students must distinguish a model output from a measurement and a possible risk from an observed failure. A written recommendation must name the conditions that would reverse it.

Analytical scope

Derive constant-tip-speed and constant-P/V speed rules from P∝N³D⁵ and V∝D³. Evaluate area-to-volume decrease, compare predicted oxygen margins and propose a measured kLa challenge at representative viscosity.

Construct a process map with numbered stream or information flows. Give a name, unit, origin, expected uncertainty, and timing for every requested input. Show where the model has a fitted coefficient as opposed to a measured quantity. If a coefficient is not available, express results parametrically and give an illustrative sensitivity range; do not silently invent a best-estimate industrial value.

Evaluate at least two candidate decisions with one common denominator: product mass per time, target recovery, demand/supply margin, analytical comparability, or another defensible quantity aligned with the case objective. A design that seems superior only because different quantities are being compared should be rejected as unresolved. Use a conservation audit to check whether any claimed improvement moves beyond the available mass, substrate or energy.

Numerical investigations

  1. Extract a first-principles relationship from one of the four unit lessons; identify dimensions and limiting cases.
  2. Run [Lab 19](../labs/lab-19.html) and record the full model input vector. Export its parameter sweep and explain the default trend.
  3. Run [Lab 20](../labs/lab-20.html); deliberately choose a second configuration with different assumptions. Give the independent reason to use the second model.
  4. Run [Lab 21](../labs/lab-21.html). Identify any warnings, invalid domains, or model-regime boundaries and explain their physical significance.
