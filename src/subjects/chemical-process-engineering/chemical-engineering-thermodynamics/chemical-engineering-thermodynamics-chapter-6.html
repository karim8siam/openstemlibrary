§6.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Vapor-Liquid Equilibria, Azeotropy and Phase Rule Applications

Why this unit matters

Liquid–liquid, dissolved gases, solids and electrolyte effects. The preceding prerequisite is Vapor–liquid systems; the destination is Reaction equilibrium. Engineering students should be able to move from a physical scenario to a transparent mathematical model and back to an interpretation of what would be observed in equipment. Thermodynamic design becomes unreliable when an input is precise to many decimal places while the phase model, standard states or material identities remain unspecified.

Four connected lessons

1. Liquid–liquid partitioning

When two poorly miscible solvents form distinct phases, equilibrium distribution depends on chemical potential equality and phase composition.

Useful calculation: A dilute partition model K=c_org/c_aq allows a first approximation to solute extraction.

Physical interpretation: A single extraction stage removes more solute when solvent volume increases, but decreasing marginal returns may favor several stages.

Failure to watch: Distribution factors depend on temperature, solvent choice and solute speciation; a dilute K cannot describe tie-lines at high loading.

Decision: Estimate extraction recovery before laboratory validation.

2. Gas solubility and Henry law

At dilute concentrations, dissolved-gas concentration can be approximately proportional to gas partial pressure at a given temperature.

Useful calculation: With the explicitly defined convention c=k_H p, solute concentration c increases linearly with gas partial pressure p.

Physical interpretation: A gas absorption contactor at higher partial pressure may dissolve more gas if equilibrium rather than mass-transfer rate is limiting.

Failure to watch: Different handbooks use reciprocal Henry coefficients, so writing down the chosen convention and units is mandatory.

Decision: Screen absorber conditions while noting reaction and salinity effects.

3. Solid–liquid and crystallization equilibrium

Cooling or evaporation can push a dissolved species beyond its saturation concentration and create a thermodynamic driving force to crystallize.

Useful calculation: Relative supersaturation σ=(c−c_sat)/c_sat when c_sat>0.

Physical interpretation: A crystallizer with c slightly above saturation may require seeding to control nucleation and product crystal size.

Failure to watch: Thermodynamic saturation predicts final equilibrium but not crystal growth kinetics, metastable zone width or fouling rates.

Decision: Distinguish equilibrium yield from throughput and morphology.

4. Osmosis and electrolyte solution limits

Solute activities control solvent chemical potential; dilute ideal solutions exhibit osmotic pressure roughly proportional to total dissolved particle concentration.

Useful calculation: π≈i c R T in an ideal dilute model; i approximates the number of dissolved particles per solute formula unit.

Physical interpretation: Reverse osmosis requires pressure exceeding the membrane-separated osmotic pressure difference, with further losses and concentration polarization.

Failure to watch: Real electrolytes require activity corrections; ideal van’t Hoff predictions lose reliability at high ionic strength.

Decision: Evaluate membrane driving-force directions with unit-consistent estimates.

§6.4 · Lesson 3: Solid–liquid and crystallization equilibrium

Unit 6 · Lesson 3: Solid–liquid and crystallization equilibrium

Learning objectives. Explain solid–liquid and crystallization equilibrium in physical terms; identify the relevant state or balance variables; apply a quantitative relation only within its validity range; and critically review a proposed process-engineering decision.

The physical question

Cooling or evaporation can push a dissolved species beyond its saturation concentration and create a thermodynamic driving force to crystallize.

Governing relation and interpretation

Key relation: Relative supersaturation σ=(c−c_sat)/c_sat when c_sat>0.

Engineering example and model boundary

A crystallizer with c slightly above saturation may require seeding to control nucleation and product crystal size.

Worked investigation

A solution is cooled until c_sat decreases to 0.12 mol/L while actual c is 0.15 mol/L. Relative supersaturation is (0.15−0.12)/0.12=0.25.

The thermodynamic driving force alone says little about nucleation rate; crystal sizes depend on kinetics and mixing.

Where the relation breaks down

Thermodynamic saturation predicts final equilibrium but not crystal growth kinetics, metastable zone width or fouling rates.

Engineering decision and data checklist

Distinguish equilibrium yield from throughput and morphology.

§6.6 Experimental Laboratory Investigation: Laboratory 11: Ideal-vapor dew pressure

Laboratory 11: Ideal-vapor dew pressure

Model: 1/Pdew=yA/PsatA+(1−yA)/PsatB

Controllable variables: Vapor mole fraction A (fraction) and Temperature (K).

Governing relation

1/Pdew=yA/PsatA+(1−yA)/PsatB. This relation is used for an educational screening calculation, not certified physical-property or process-safety design. Every output must be checked against dimensional consistency, equilibrium assumptions and an independent reference when applied to real chemicals.

Stated limitations

Low-pressure ideal vapor/liquid, hypothetical species data.

Step-by-step investigation

  1. Record the default inputs and the readout with its units. Sketch the qualitative expected trend before inspecting the chart.
  2. Decrease Vapor mole fraction A to its minimum while keeping Temperature fixed. Record the change and explain which term in the governing equation is responsible.
  3. Increase Vapor mole fraction A to its maximum and compare the response with the first observation. Determine whether the dependence is linear, logarithmic, exponential or nonlinear for a reason you can explain.
  4. Reset, then alter Temperature and repeat. Estimate a finite-difference sensitivity between two nearby parameter values, including its units.
  5. Export the response curve to CSV. Explain how a plotted sensitivity helps select a range for a laboratory measurement or a process-control test.
  6. Identify the first physical mechanism omitted by the model that would become important if the system were pressurized, heated, mixed or scaled up substantially.

Questions

Reasoning: Why does the dew-pressure expression use reciprocal saturation pressures?

Extension: Compare bubble and dew pressure at numerically equal xA=yA.

Uncertainty: How would uncertainty in temperature influence your interpretation of the response curve? Which single additional measurement would best distinguish model error from input uncertainty?
