§3.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Heat Effects of Industrial Operations and Chemical Reactions

Why this unit matters

Nonideal behavior, criticality, equations of state and property departures. The preceding prerequisite is Energy laws; the destination is Phase equilibrium. Engineering students should be able to move from a physical scenario to a transparent mathematical model and back to an interpretation of what would be observed in equipment. Thermodynamic design becomes unreliable when an input is precise to many decimal places while the phase model, standard states or material identities remain unspecified.

Four connected lessons

1. Compressibility factors

The ratio Z=PV/(nRT) measures departure from ideal-gas PVT behavior.

Useful calculation: Z=PV/(nRT); an ideal gas has Z=1 at every state in its model.

Physical interpretation: Natural-gas volumes at high pressure must not be obtained solely from ideal-gas volume when compressibility is substantial.

Failure to watch: Z does not by itself provide enthalpy or entropy; those demand caloric equations or a thermodynamically consistent equation of state.

Decision: Check sizing calculations under high-pressure operating windows.

2. Cubic equations of state

Excluded molecular volume and intermolecular attraction motivate cubic or related state equations for real fluids.

Useful calculation: Van der Waals: P=RT/(v−b)−a/v² for molar volume v>b.

Physical interpretation: For illustration, changing the attraction parameter changes the low-volume pressure curve; an isotherm may show an unphysical loop requiring phase-equilibrium treatment.

Failure to watch: Never interpret the unstable loop as an actual observable homogeneous path. Model parameter units and temperature-dependent attraction terms matter.

Decision: Plot and critique a qualitative real-fluid isotherm.

3. Critical states and corresponding states

At the critical point the distinction between coexisting liquid and vapor disappears; reduced variables offer qualitative comparison between substances.

Useful calculation: Reduced temperature T_r=T/T_c and reduced pressure P_r=P/P_c.

Physical interpretation: Pressure transients near a fluid critical point can accompany large density sensitivity; simplified model predictions deserve heightened scrutiny.

Failure to watch: Matching reduced coordinates does not make different fluids identical, especially polar, associating or strongly asymmetric species.

Decision: Use dimensionless comparisons to choose property models.

4. Residual functions and property data

A consistent equation of state can determine departures of enthalpy and entropy from ideal-gas reference values.

Useful calculation: h=h^ig+h^R; s=s^ig+s^R. Departure values depend on T,P and chosen EOS.

Physical interpretation: A compressor calculation may use an ideal-gas thermal contribution plus a residual correction when actual state accuracy demands it.

Failure to watch: Do not add unrelated property correlations that violate a Maxwell relation or imply an impossible negative compressibility.

Decision: Design a property-data workflow using documented references and uncertainty.

Unit 3 · Lesson 1: Compressibility factors

The physical question

The ratio Z=PV/(nRT) measures departure from ideal-gas PVT behavior.

Governing relation and interpretation

Key relation: Z=PV/(nRT); an ideal gas has Z=1 at every state in its model.

Engineering example and model boundary

Worked investigation

A 0.8 kmol gas occupies 0.30 m³ at 360 K and 11 MPa. Compare the actual measured pressure against nRT/V to calculate Z.

The resulting Z is informative about density behavior but cannot infer energy departures without an enthalpy model.

Unit 3 · Lesson 3: Critical states and corresponding states

Learning objectives. Explain critical states and corresponding states in physical terms; identify the relevant state or balance variables; apply a quantitative relation only within its validity range; and critically review a proposed process-engineering decision.

The physical question

At the critical point the distinction between coexisting liquid and vapor disappears; reduced variables offer qualitative comparison between substances.

Governing relation and interpretation

Key relation: Reduced temperature T_r=T/T_c and reduced pressure P_r=P/P_c.

Engineering example and model boundary

Pressure transients near a fluid critical point can accompany large density sensitivity; simplified model predictions deserve heightened scrutiny.

Worked investigation

Two fluids operate at equal T/Tc=1.1 and P/Pc=0.8. Contrast their approximate reduced conditions before evaluating polarity and molecular associations.

Equal reduced conditions offer a screening analogy; they do not establish equal heat capacities, enthalpies or compressibilities.

Where the relation breaks down

Matching reduced coordinates does not make different fluids identical, especially polar, associating or strongly asymmetric species.

Engineering decision and data checklist

Use dimensionless comparisons to choose property models.

§3.6 Experimental Laboratory Investigation: Laboratory 5: Availability of process heat

Laboratory 5: Availability of process heat

Model: Wmax=Q(1−T0/T)

Controllable variables: Source temperature (K) and Heat transfer rate (kW).

Governing relation

Wmax=Q(1−T0/T). This relation is used for an educational screening calculation, not certified physical-property or process-safety design. Every output must be checked against dimensional consistency, equilibrium assumptions and an independent reference when applied to real chemicals.

Stated limitations

Reversible heat extraction from reservoir at T; environment 298.15 K.

Step-by-step investigation

  1. Record the default inputs and the readout with its units. Sketch the qualitative expected trend before inspecting the chart.
  2. Decrease Source temperature to its minimum while keeping Heat transfer rate fixed. Record the change and explain which term in the governing equation is responsible.
  3. Increase Source temperature to its maximum and compare the response with the first observation. Determine whether the dependence is linear, logarithmic, exponential or nonlinear for a reason you can explain.
  4. Reset, then alter Heat transfer rate and repeat. Estimate a finite-difference sensitivity between two nearby parameter values, including its units.
  5. Export the response curve to CSV. Explain how a plotted sensitivity helps select a range for a laboratory measurement or a process-control test.
  6. Identify the first physical mechanism omitted by the model that would become important if the system were pressurized, heated, mixed or scaled up substantially.

Questions

Reasoning: Why is one kilowatt of low-grade heat less useful than high-grade heat?

Extension: Compare source temperatures delivering equal thermal energy.

Uncertainty: How would uncertainty in heat transfer rate influence your interpretation of the response curve? Which single additional measurement would best distinguish model error from input uncertainty?

Evidence to submit

Export a parameter sweep from the interactive model and attach it with your explanation of Wmax=Q(1−T0/T). Demonstrate the predicted change in Source temperature at two different settings of Heat transfer rate. Conclude by explaining whether the laboratory's approximation (Reversible heat extraction from reservoir at T; environment 298.15 K.) would remain credible in a real chemical process.
