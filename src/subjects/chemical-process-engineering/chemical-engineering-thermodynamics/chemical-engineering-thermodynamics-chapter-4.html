§4.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Second Law, Entropy Generation and Availability Analysis

Why this unit matters

Phase rules, chemical potentials, saturation boundaries and reference properties. The preceding prerequisite is Real fluids; the destination is Vapor–liquid systems. Engineering students should be able to move from a physical scenario to a transparent mathematical model and back to an interpretation of what would be observed in equipment. Thermodynamic design becomes unreliable when an input is precise to many decimal places while the phase model, standard states or material identities remain unspecified.

Four connected lessons

1. Gibbs phase rule and degrees of freedom

The phase rule counts independently adjustable intensive variables for equilibrium phases subject to specified component and reaction constraints.

Useful calculation: For a nonreactive system F=C−π+2, before fixing pressure or temperature.

Physical interpretation: For one pure component in liquid-vapor coexistence, T and P are linked; specifying either fixes the saturation state.

Failure to watch: Counting a chemical reaction without reducing independent composition dimensions will overstate degrees of freedom.

Decision: Plan experiments with enough measurements to identify a phase state.

2. Chemical potential as equilibrium criterion

Matter redistributes between phases until the chemical potential of each transferable species is equal across coexisting phases.

Useful calculation: At equilibrium μ_i^α=μ_i^β; μ_i is partial molar Gibbs energy.

Physical interpretation: Liquid and vapor can coexist at the same T and P despite sharply different densities because equality of potential, not equality of density, governs equilibrium.

Failure to watch: Equal total Gibbs energies across phases is not the appropriate condition; matching the individual species chemical potentials is essential.

Decision: Interpret multicomponent equilibrium in flash equipment.

3. Clapeyron and saturation curves

The slope of a phase boundary follows latent enthalpy and the associated volume difference.

Useful calculation: dP_sat/dT=Δh/(TΔv); at low vapor pressures a constant latent heat approximation gives ln(P2/P1)=−Δh_vap/R(1/T2−1/T1).

Physical interpretation: A vapor-pressure correlation can map how condenser temperature changes required operating pressure.

Failure to watch: The integrated approximation assumes vapor ideality, negligible liquid volume and constant latent heat; it can fail near a critical point.

Decision: Use a saturation curve within its measured or stated valid range.

4. Steam tables and property references

Steam is an important utility but its equilibrium properties require region-aware property models at high pressure and across phases.

Useful calculation: Saturated mixture h=h_f+x(h_g−h_f), where x is vapor mass quality between zero and one.

Physical interpretation: Boiler outlet quality determines turbine inlet moisture limits, while a superheated state requires a distinct property lookup.

Failure to watch: Do not apply quality to a compressed liquid or supercritical fluid; industrial IAPWS-IF97 is more complete than teaching approximations.

Decision: Select accurate property tables for heat and power equipment.

Engineering example and model boundary

A vapor-pressure correlation can map how condenser temperature changes required operating pressure.

Worked investigation

Suppose a solvent has vapor pressure 80 kPa at 340 K and constant latent enthalpy 35 kJ/mol. Approximate its pressure at 350 K and label the approximation.

Because the exponent integrates 1/T, a linear extrapolation in Celsius is not the same as Clausius–Clapeyron and may give inconsistent trends.

Where the relation breaks down

The integrated approximation assumes vapor ideality, negligible liquid volume and constant latent heat; it can fail near a critical point.

Laboratory 7: Second-virial compressibility

Model: Z≈1+B P/(RT)

Controllable variables: Pressure (MPa) and Temperature (K).

Governing relation

Z≈1+B P/(RT). This relation is used for an educational screening calculation, not certified physical-property or process-safety design. Every output must be checked against dimensional consistency, equilibrium assumptions and an independent reference when applied to real chemicals.

Stated limitations

Truncated virial approximation with B=−0.04 L/mol; illustrative validity only.

Step-by-step investigation

  1. Record the default inputs and the readout with its units. Sketch the qualitative expected trend before inspecting the chart.
  2. Decrease Pressure to its minimum while keeping Temperature fixed. Record the change and explain which term in the governing equation is responsible.
  3. Increase Pressure to its maximum and compare the response with the first observation. Determine whether the dependence is linear, logarithmic, exponential or nonlinear for a reason you can explain.
  4. Reset, then alter Temperature and repeat. Estimate a finite-difference sensitivity between two nearby parameter values, including its units.
