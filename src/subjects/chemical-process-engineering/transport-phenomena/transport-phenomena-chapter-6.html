§6.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Molecular Mass Diffusion, Fickian Shell Balances and Species Fluxes

The geometry of a transport domain shapes its governing differential operators. Planar, cylindrical and spherical coordinates use different divergence expressions because the area through which flux passes varies with position. Choosing the right coordinate system can reduce a complicated PDE to an analytical ODE; choosing the wrong geometry can produce incorrect rates even when the algebra looks consistent.

For radial conduction through a cylinder without internal generation, conservation requires the same heat rate through every cylindrical surface. Since the surface area is 2πrL, the radial temperature gradient must decrease in magnitude as radius increases. Integration produces a logarithmic temperature distribution. For a spherical shell the area grows as 4πr², yielding a reciprocal-radius distribution. Both solutions can serve as reference benchmarks for finite-element or finite-volume models. Verify not only endpoint temperatures but also that the integrated heat rate is constant with radius.

Multidimensional fields create effects that single-resistance circuits cannot capture. Around corners, contact points and embedded heaters, the temperature gradient has more than one component. Symmetry can simplify a geometry, but it must reflect actual boundary and material conditions. A zero normal derivative at a symmetry plane is different from fixing temperature there. Applying a false symmetry condition may make a solver appear stable while producing the wrong heat flux distribution.

Time-dependent equations also require initial conditions. In a heat equation, the initial temperature field may be uniform, layered or localized; all will evolve differently under the same boundary temperatures. The Fourier number gives a measure of elapsed diffusion time relative to an engineering length. During an early transient the thermal penetration may be small compared with the object thickness, making semi-infinite approximations useful. At later times the opposite wall or boundaries affect the solution.

Numerical models require discretizations that respect geometry. A cylindrical finite-volume mesh should use the face areas and cell volumes appropriate to each radius. For a steady case with no heat generation, the sum of outward face fluxes should vanish. For a transient case, the sum of outward boundary fluxes and energy storage must balance. Grid refinement checks whether the result depends on cell width; comparison with an exact cylinder or sphere solution checks code correctness. Neither verifies the actual material properties unless those have been measured independently.

§6.2 Unit 6, Lesson 1: Radial heat flow in cylinders

Unit 6, Lesson 1: Radial heat flow in cylinders

Learning objective

Compare constant-area approximations against the exact logarithmic resistance.

Conceptual model

Steady conduction through a cylinder follows (1/r)d/dr(r dT/dr)=0 when no internal heat is generated. Its solution is affine in ln(r); drawing a straight line against r therefore hides the correct radial structure.

At radius r, conductive rate q=−k(2πrL)dT/dr. A larger area compensates for a smaller gradient, preserving constant q through every cylindrical surface.

Numerical grids built with equal increments in r need geometry-aware face areas. Treating each annular cell as an equal-area slab breaks discrete conservation even when the visual temperature plot looks plausible.

Governing relationship

q = 2πkLΔT/ln(r_o/r_i)

§6.3 Unit 6, Lesson 2: Radial transport through spheres

Unit 6, Lesson 2: Radial transport through spheres

Learning objective

Explain why area increases with radius and how that shapes temperature.

Conceptual model

In a spherical shell, the conducting area increases as 4πr². Integrating steady radial Fourier law yields resistance R=(1/r_i−1/r_o)/(4πk), which cannot be replaced by a planar resistance.

The temperature function is affine in reciprocal radius. In a thick shell, most of the temperature drop can occur near the inner surface because its area is smallest.

Applications include protective shells, spherical storage and biological cells, but material anisotropy and internal heat generation can invalidate the simple equation.

§6.4 Unit 6, Lesson 3: Two-dimensional conduction

Unit 6, Lesson 3: Two-dimensional conduction

Learning objective

Specify physically consistent insulated, isothermal and convective boundaries.

Conceptual model

A two-dimensional steady field solves ∇²T=0 only when conductivity is spatially uniform and no generation exists. At corners and around embedded conductors, heat flux is multidirectional and one-dimensional thermal resistance may be inadequate.

Dirichlet boundaries impose temperature, Neumann boundaries impose normal heat flux, and Robin boundaries couple surface temperature to external convection. A wrong boundary type can be a greater error than a coarse mesh.

An insulated corner is not an isothermal corner. In a conservative discretization, each face flux must cancel between neighboring cells and the sum of boundary heat rates must balance generation.

Governing relationship

∂²T/∂x²+∂²T/∂y²=0

§6.5 Unit 6, Lesson 4: Transient PDEs and boundary conditions

Unit 6, Lesson 4: Transient PDEs and boundary conditions

Learning objective

Show how a wrong boundary condition can produce a plausible but incorrect numerical solution.

Conceptual model

Transient transport problems require both initial conditions and boundary conditions. A time derivative sets the rate of change but does not uniquely specify a solution without a starting field.

For thermal diffusion, ∂T/∂t=α∇²T. A fixed-temperature wall, insulated wall and film-cooled wall lead to different transients even with identical material properties and initial temperatures.

Engineering measurement, uncertainty and model validation

Why equations need measured inputs

An equation can be mathematically exact under its assumptions and still predict a real process poorly if its inputs were measured incorrectly. In transport calculations, viscosity, effective diffusivity, thermal conductivity and boundary conditions can all carry uncertainty. Before comparing model and experiment, list each measured quantity, its units, calibration source and expected accuracy. Keep instrument resolution separate from repeatability: repeated readings may agree closely while every reading shares the same calibration bias.

Case A: pipe radius controls flow

For a pressure-controlled laminar circular pipe, the Hagen–Poiseuille relation has Q proportional to R to the fourth power. Taking logarithmic differentials gives approximately δQ/Q = 4δR/R + δ(Δp)/(Δp) − δμ/μ − δL/L for small signed changes. If radius is uncertain by ±2% while all else is treated as exact, its first-order contribution alone is roughly ±8% in Q. This is why reporting a very precise flow from a loosely measured pipe diameter creates false confidence. In a real laboratory, measure diameter at multiple locations and inspect noncircularity; the physical pipe may not have a single well-defined radius.

Case B: heat through a layered wall

For known surface temperatures, total steady heat rate through resistances in series is q=ΔT/ΣR. If insulation conductivity is temperature dependent, evaluating k at one arbitrary temperature can systematically bias q. A physically appropriate estimate may integrate k(T) or iterate layer temperatures with measured property data. Contact resistances introduce additional drops that are absent from a perfect-contact wall model. Measure surface temperatures on both sides of each contact where possible; otherwise infer the combined thermal resistance and state that it may not be uniquely separable into component properties.

Case C: diffusivity from a release experiment

A one-dimensional Gaussian tracer pulse offers an experimental estimate of effective diffusivity because its variance grows linearly as 2Dt. Suppose measured variance increases from 0.002 m² at 10 s to 0.014 m² at 70 s. The estimated slope is (0.014−0.002)/(70−10)=0.0002 m²/s, hence D=0.0001 m²/s. This conclusion assumes a uniform infinite medium without advection, reaction or unmodeled mixing. If the mean position also shifts, convective transport must be considered. A single measurement of peak concentration cannot reliably identify both injected mass and diffusivity.

Case D: boundary-layer coefficient identification

A reported convective coefficient h is often computed as measured heat rate divided by exposed area and temperature difference. If the actual wall temperature varies across the surface, substituting a single thermocouple value may be inaccurate. In an external-flow heated-plate trial, place sensors at multiple downstream distances. Repeat at varied mean velocity and compare Nusselt trends with a correlation whose Reynolds and Prandtl limits match the equipment. Surface contamination, radiant exchange and conduction through the support can all masquerade as convection. Correcting for these parasitic paths is necessary before interpreting h as a clean fluid transfer coefficient.

Case E: numerical uncertainty versus model uncertainty

A computational solver can exhibit excellent grid convergence while its material-property model is wrong. Suppose a refined model predicts 12.0 W, a still finer mesh predicts 11.9 W, but the only experimentally measured heat flow is 9.5 W. The mesh difference is 0.1 W, yet the model–experiment discrepancy is 2.4 W. Refining the mesh further is unlikely to resolve a missing convection resistance or a conductivity error. Check residuals, boundary settings and energy conservation first, then challenge physical assumptions and measurements separately. State which uncertainty sources were quantified and which remain qualitative.

Practical uncertainty propagation

For a prediction y=f(x₁,…,xₙ) with small independent input uncertainties, one common first-order method uses u_y²≈Σ(∂f/∂x_i)²u_i². For correlated inputs there are additional covariance terms. The approximation may fail with strongly nonlinear responses, broad uncertainties, discontinuous regimes or parameters near physical limits. Monte Carlo sampling can help if plausible input distributions are available, but arbitrary normal distributions must not be treated as evidence. In all uncertainty analyses, specify whether quoted intervals are standard uncertainties, expanded uncertainties or observed ranges.

Engineering reporting checklist

A reproducible transport calculation should make it possible for a separate reader to reconstruct its main result. Record the domain geometry; draw the direction of positive flux; write all governing laws and their units; identify every boundary and initial condition; document material properties and their sources; and state any empirical correlation and validity limits. Include at least one independent limiting-case calculation, such as zero gradient causing zero pure conductive flux or a vanishing reaction rate giving nearly complete catalyst utilization. Report numerical solver settings if applicable, including grid resolution, timestep and residual definition. Finally, distinguish what was directly measured, what was fitted, what was computed and what was inferred. This separation prevents a simplified teaching simulation from being mistaken for a validated industrial design tool.
