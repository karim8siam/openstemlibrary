§8.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Macroscopic Transport Balances, Interphase Transport and Universal Analogies

Computational transport modeling is not simply the production of colorful contour plots. It is a sequence of physical modeling, mathematical formulation, discretization, numerical solution and verification. Each stage has its own possible errors. A code may solve the wrong boundary-value problem to machine precision, or solve a correct model on such a coarse mesh that its reported output is unreliable. The workflow must distinguish these cases.

Begin by nondimensionalizing the governing equations. Reynolds number expresses inertia versus viscosity; Péclet number compares advection with diffusion; Fourier number compares available time with diffusion across a characteristic length. Nondimensionalization reveals which terms may be small in the interior while still essential in thin boundary layers. Choosing length and velocity scales deliberately is crucial because the values of the dimensionless groups otherwise have no interpretable reference.

A basic explicit diffusion solver is a useful numerical laboratory. The FTCS update averages the influence of neighboring nodes with weights controlled by Fo_grid=DΔt/Δx². If Fo_grid exceeds one half in the standard one-dimensional constant-D method, the center-node weight becomes negative and the update can amplify oscillations. The simulated concentration may turn negative even though the true diffusion equation preserves nonnegative concentrations. This is a stability failure, not a new physical phenomenon.

Finite-volume schemes start from integrated conservation over each cell. At a shared face, the flux leaving one cell must equal the flux entering its neighbor with the opposite sign. This cancellation makes the global inventory budget easy to audit. Real meshes may have varying cell areas, interface resistance and material coefficients, requiring careful face interpolation. If a solver loses species despite impermeable boundaries and no reaction, first inspect the discrete flux bookkeeping.

Verification and validation require separate evidence. An analytical solution such as Gaussian diffusion checks numerical implementation under known assumptions. Grid convergence estimates discretization error by comparing coarse and refined solutions. A laboratory experiment tests whether the modeled physics applies in the real world. Report units, mesh size, timestep, conservation residual and a sensitivity study with every computational result. When more complex physics is introduced, preserve simpler benchmark cases as regression tests so future code changes cannot silently corrupt previously correct results.

§8.2 Unit 8, Lesson 1: Scaling and nondimensionalization

Unit 8, Lesson 1: Scaling and nondimensionalization

Learning objective

Select scales before identifying dominant terms in the governing PDE.

Conceptual model

Scaling removes units and highlights competing physical mechanisms. Reynolds compares inertia and viscosity, Péclet advection and diffusion, and Fourier time available for diffusion compared with diffusion across a chosen length.

Selecting characteristic U and L is a modeling decision. For a thin film, using equipment diameter instead of the film thickness can obscure the dominant gradient and lead to the wrong neglected terms.

Write boundary and initial conditions in dimensionless form too. A PDE can have a small transport coefficient but still require its retention near a wall, because boundary layers create steep gradients.

§8.3 Unit 8, Lesson 2: Explicit diffusion discretization

Unit 8, Lesson 2: Explicit diffusion discretization

Learning objective

Demonstrate why explicit one-dimensional diffusion requires Fo ≤ 1/2.

Conceptual model

The forward-time centered-space (FTCS) scheme samples ∂c/∂t from two time levels and ∂²c/∂x² from three spatial points. The coefficient Fo=DΔt/Δx² controls how strongly each time step mixes neighboring nodes.

For one-dimensional diffusion on a uniform grid with constant D, nonnegative update weights require Fo≤0.5. Above that limit the scheme can produce negative concentrations and oscillations even when the true PDE preserves positivity.

A scheme that is stable is not automatically accurate. Decrease both Δx and Δt while maintaining stability, then compare conserved quantities and profile changes.

§8.4 Unit 8, Lesson 3: Finite-volume conservation

Unit 8, Lesson 3: Finite-volume conservation

Learning objective

Explain why conservative face-flux bookkeeping matters more than a visually smooth contour.

Conceptual model

Finite-volume methods integrate conservation over cells rather than directly approximating a differential equation at points. Fluxes through a shared face enter one cell and leave the neighbor with identical magnitude, promoting exact discrete internal cancellation.

For a 1D diffusive face between neighboring cell centers, F≈−D A(c_R−c_L)/Δx. A nonuniform mesh requires a consistent face distance and material interface conductance, not the arithmetic average of unrelated resistances.

If the discretized total inventory changes without boundary flux or source, inspect face indexing, sign conventions and boundary integration before refining the mesh.

Governing relationship

(M_iⁿ⁺¹−M_iⁿ)/Δt = F_{i−1/2}−F_{i+1/2}+S_i

Fully solved numerical illustration

Problem. A one-dimensional finite-volume face joins two cells with concentrations 5 mol/m³ apart over Δx=0.10 m. Diffusivity is 10⁻⁵ m²/s and face area is 0.020 m².

Model audit. Counting two slightly different values for a shared face destroys discrete conservation even if both local gradients seem plausible.

Engineering measurement, uncertainty and model validation

Why equations need measured inputs

An equation can be mathematically exact under its assumptions and still predict a real process poorly if its inputs were measured incorrectly. In transport calculations, viscosity, effective diffusivity, thermal conductivity and boundary conditions can all carry uncertainty. Before comparing model and experiment, list each measured quantity, its units, calibration source and expected accuracy. Keep instrument resolution separate from repeatability: repeated readings may agree closely while every reading shares the same calibration bias.

Case A: pipe radius controls flow

For a pressure-controlled laminar circular pipe, the Hagen–Poiseuille relation has Q proportional to R to the fourth power. Taking logarithmic differentials gives approximately δQ/Q = 4δR/R + δ(Δp)/(Δp) − δμ/μ − δL/L for small signed changes. If radius is uncertain by ±2% while all else is treated as exact, its first-order contribution alone is roughly ±8% in Q. This is why reporting a very precise flow from a loosely measured pipe diameter creates false confidence. In a real laboratory, measure diameter at multiple locations and inspect noncircularity; the physical pipe may not have a single well-defined radius.

Case B: heat through a layered wall

For known surface temperatures, total steady heat rate through resistances in series is q=ΔT/ΣR. If insulation conductivity is temperature dependent, evaluating k at one arbitrary temperature can systematically bias q. A physically appropriate estimate may integrate k(T) or iterate layer temperatures with measured property data. Contact resistances introduce additional drops that are absent from a perfect-contact wall model. Measure surface temperatures on both sides of each contact where possible; otherwise infer the combined thermal resistance and state that it may not be uniquely separable into component properties.

Case C: diffusivity from a release experiment

A one-dimensional Gaussian tracer pulse offers an experimental estimate of effective diffusivity because its variance grows linearly as 2Dt. Suppose measured variance increases from 0.002 m² at 10 s to 0.014 m² at 70 s. The estimated slope is (0.014−0.002)/(70−10)=0.0002 m²/s, hence D=0.0001 m²/s. This conclusion assumes a uniform infinite medium without advection, reaction or unmodeled mixing. If the mean position also shifts, convective transport must be considered. A single measurement of peak concentration cannot reliably identify both injected mass and diffusivity.

Case D: boundary-layer coefficient identification

A reported convective coefficient h is often computed as measured heat rate divided by exposed area and temperature difference. If the actual wall temperature varies across the surface, substituting a single thermocouple value may be inaccurate. In an external-flow heated-plate trial, place sensors at multiple downstream distances. Repeat at varied mean velocity and compare Nusselt trends with a correlation whose Reynolds and Prandtl limits match the equipment. Surface contamination, radiant exchange and conduction through the support can all masquerade as convection. Correcting for these parasitic paths is necessary before interpreting h as a clean fluid transfer coefficient.

Case E: numerical uncertainty versus model uncertainty

A computational solver can exhibit excellent grid convergence while its material-property model is wrong. Suppose a refined model predicts 12.0 W, a still finer mesh predicts 11.9 W, but the only experimentally measured heat flow is 9.5 W. The mesh difference is 0.1 W, yet the model–experiment discrepancy is 2.4 W. Refining the mesh further is unlikely to resolve a missing convection resistance or a conductivity error. Check residuals, boundary settings and energy conservation first, then challenge physical assumptions and measurements separately. State which uncertainty sources were quantified and which remain qualitative.

Practical uncertainty propagation

For a prediction y=f(x₁,…,xₙ) with small independent input uncertainties, one common first-order method uses u_y²≈Σ(∂f/∂x_i)²u_i². For correlated inputs there are additional covariance terms. The approximation may fail with strongly nonlinear responses, broad uncertainties, discontinuous regimes or parameters near physical limits. Monte Carlo sampling can help if plausible input distributions are available, but arbitrary normal distributions must not be treated as evidence. In all uncertainty analyses, specify whether quoted intervals are standard uncertainties, expanded uncertainties or observed ranges.

Engineering reporting checklist

A reproducible transport calculation should make it possible for a separate reader to reconstruct its main result. Record the domain geometry; draw the direction of positive flux; write all governing laws and their units; identify every boundary and initial condition; document material properties and their sources; and state any empirical correlation and validity limits. Include at least one independent limiting-case calculation, such as zero gradient causing zero pure conductive flux or a vanishing reaction rate giving nearly complete catalyst utilization. Report numerical solver settings if applicable, including grid resolution, timestep and residual definition. Finally, distinguish what was directly measured, what was fitted, what was computed and what was inferred. This separation prevents a simplified teaching simulation from being mistaken for a validated industrial design tool.
