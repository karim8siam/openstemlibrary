§4.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Thermal Conduction, Heat Generation and Multidimensional Profiles

Mass transport is more than a material counterpart of Fourier heat conduction. Diffusion arises from species motion relative to a chosen reference velocity, whereas convection carries species with bulk motion. In dilute systems at nearly constant properties, Fick law often provides a workable concentration-gradient approximation. In concentrated mixtures, composition-dependent thermodynamic factors and cross-effects can make simple Fick coefficients inadequate. A careful model declares the flux frame—molar average, mass average or stationary coordinates—before performing a balance.

The elementary planar film provides an important baseline. With no reaction, uniform diffusivity and fixed boundary concentrations, the steady concentration profile is linear. Its gradient and therefore its flux increase as the film becomes thinner. But the film thickness in a simplified model may not be a directly measurable surface; often the effective mass-transfer coefficient is estimated from a correlation. An actual gas–liquid interface couples a gas-side and liquid-side profile through a phase-equilibrium relationship. Setting both interface concentrations equal without converting between the phases generally makes the calculation inconsistent.

An unsteady injected tracer gives a different physical picture. The concentration peak spreads and declines because molecules move from higher to lower concentration, but the integral amount remains fixed in an infinite nonreacting medium. The Gaussian pulse provides a strong analytical verification case. A numerical solver should conserve integrated tracer inventory within its boundary conditions, even though the peak changes rapidly. A finite laboratory vessel with absorbing or permeable walls eventually violates the infinite-medium assumptions, and the mathematical infinite-domain tails should not be read literally at molecular scales.

Reaction and diffusion interact inside porous catalysts. Reactant entering from the exterior may be consumed before penetrating deeply. A first-order slab model introduces a Thiele modulus involving thickness, rate constant and effective diffusivity. This dimensionless number diagnoses whether internal transport is relatively fast or slow. The resulting effectiveness factor can be far below unity even when intrinsic kinetic activity is strong. Increasing catalyst thickness may therefore add much less useful reaction capacity than expected from total geometric volume.

When building an experiment, distinguish three kinds of uncertainty: molecular property values, near-interface hydrodynamic transfer coefficients, and chemical kinetic parameters. If a model agrees with the outlet composition, that alone may not distinguish them because different parameter combinations can produce the same outlet. Varying stirring rate, particle size and temperature separately helps identify which physical resistance dominates. Always record molar or mass units and account for molecular weight when converting between the two.

§4.2 Unit 4, Lesson 1: Fick law and diffusion resistance

Unit 4, Lesson 1: Fick law and diffusion resistance

Learning objective

Separate diffusive flux measured relative to molar-average velocity from total molar flux.

Conceptual model

For diffusion through a stationary homogeneous planar layer, the steady gradient is linear if diffusivity remains constant and there is no reaction. The flux is proportional to concentration difference but inversely proportional to layer thickness.

Diffusive flux is defined relative to a chosen average velocity. If one component diffuses through stagnant nondiffusing gas, equimolar counterdiffusion is not the correct model; a Stefan-flow correction can be required.

Interface concentrations are determined by phase equilibria, which should not be guessed from bulk values. The displayed laboratory isolates only the diffusion resistance and does not solve coupled multicomponent transport.

Governing relationship

J_A = −D_AB ∂c_A/∂x

§4.3 Unit 4, Lesson 2: Transient dispersion of a tracer

Unit 4, Lesson 2: Transient dispersion of a tracer

Learning objective

Check that the area under a pulse remains equal to its injected inventory.

Conceptual model

A concentrated pulse spreads as a Gaussian in an infinite uniform medium when the governing PDE is ∂c/∂t=D∂²c/∂x². The standard deviation grows as √(2Dt); the maximum concentration declines as 1/√t in one dimension.

The spreading curve is a Green-function solution for an impulsive source; it is not a solution for a constant source or finite impermeable tank. Integrating concentration over all x conserves the initial tracer inventory.

The diffusion equation has a formally nonzero tail at all distances immediately. That mathematical idealization is useful at continuum scales but not a literal description of molecular finite-speed motion over microscopic times.

Governing relationship

c(x,t) = M exp[−x²/(4Dt)] / √(4πDt)

§4.4 Unit 4, Lesson 3: Convective mass transfer

Unit 4, Lesson 3: Convective mass transfer

Learning objective

Explain why mixing primarily changes the film resistance, not molecular diffusivity.

Conceptual model

The film model condenses near-interface gradients into a transfer coefficient. Its main relationship, N=k_c(c_b−c_i), needs the driving-force basis and units stated together; coefficients built on mole fractions and concentrations cannot be swapped unconverted.

Stirring tends to thin boundary layers and increase effective coefficients, but the relationship is empirical and geometry-specific. An interface equilibrium relation connects gas-side and liquid-side concentrations when two phases exchange material.

A fitted k_c may hide changes in hydrodynamics, surface contamination or interfacial area. Translate measured total transfer into per-area flux before comparing two pieces of apparatus.

Governing relationship

N_A = k_c(c_b−c_i)

§4.5 Unit 4, Lesson 4: Reaction and diffusion in a catalyst

Unit 4, Lesson 4: Reaction and diffusion in a catalyst

Learning objective

Interpret why reaction can be kinetically fast yet particle utilization is poor.

Conceptual model

Inside a porous catalytic slab, reactant molecules diffuse inward while a first-order reaction removes them. The two competing rates form the Thiele modulus, φ=L√(k/D_eff), when L is the half-thickness and the center has zero flux.

For an isothermal symmetric slab, η=tanh(φ)/φ. When φ is small, diffusion is rapid relative to consumption and η approaches one. When φ is large, the interior is starved and η becomes small.

An observed low effectiveness factor might also reflect external-film limitations, heat generation or non-first-order kinetics. Vary particle size or reaction temperature to test the model, not only the fitted rate constant.

Governing relationship

φ=L√(k/D_eff); η=tanh(φ)/φ

Engineering measurement, uncertainty and model validation

Why equations need measured inputs

An equation can be mathematically exact under its assumptions and still predict a real process poorly if its inputs were measured incorrectly. In transport calculations, viscosity, effective diffusivity, thermal conductivity and boundary conditions can all carry uncertainty. Before comparing model and experiment, list each measured quantity, its units, calibration source and expected accuracy. Keep instrument resolution separate from repeatability: repeated readings may agree closely while every reading shares the same calibration bias.

Case A: pipe radius controls flow

For a pressure-controlled laminar circular pipe, the Hagen–Poiseuille relation has Q proportional to R to the fourth power. Taking logarithmic differentials gives approximately δQ/Q = 4δR/R + δ(Δp)/(Δp) − δμ/μ − δL/L for small signed changes. If radius is uncertain by ±2% while all else is treated as exact, its first-order contribution alone is roughly ±8% in Q. This is why reporting a very precise flow from a loosely measured pipe diameter creates false confidence. In a real laboratory, measure diameter at multiple locations and inspect noncircularity; the physical pipe may not have a single well-defined radius.

Case B: heat through a layered wall

For known surface temperatures, total steady heat rate through resistances in series is q=ΔT/ΣR. If insulation conductivity is temperature dependent, evaluating k at one arbitrary temperature can systematically bias q. A physically appropriate estimate may integrate k(T) or iterate layer temperatures with measured property data. Contact resistances introduce additional drops that are absent from a perfect-contact wall model. Measure surface temperatures on both sides of each contact where possible; otherwise infer the combined thermal resistance and state that it may not be uniquely separable into component properties.

Case C: diffusivity from a release experiment

A one-dimensional Gaussian tracer pulse offers an experimental estimate of effective diffusivity because its variance grows linearly as 2Dt. Suppose measured variance increases from 0.002 m² at 10 s to 0.014 m² at 70 s. The estimated slope is (0.014−0.002)/(70−10)=0.0002 m²/s, hence D=0.0001 m²/s. This conclusion assumes a uniform infinite medium without advection, reaction or unmodeled mixing. If the mean position also shifts, convective transport must be considered. A single measurement of peak concentration cannot reliably identify both injected mass and diffusivity.

Case D: boundary-layer coefficient identification

A reported convective coefficient h is often computed as measured heat rate divided by exposed area and temperature difference. If the actual wall temperature varies across the surface, substituting a single thermocouple value may be inaccurate. In an external-flow heated-plate trial, place sensors at multiple downstream distances. Repeat at varied mean velocity and compare Nusselt trends with a correlation whose Reynolds and Prandtl limits match the equipment. Surface contamination, radiant exchange and conduction through the support can all masquerade as convection. Correcting for these parasitic paths is necessary before interpreting h as a clean fluid transfer coefficient.

Case E: numerical uncertainty versus model uncertainty

A computational solver can exhibit excellent grid convergence while its material-property model is wrong. Suppose a refined model predicts 12.0 W, a still finer mesh predicts 11.9 W, but the only experimentally measured heat flow is 9.5 W. The mesh difference is 0.1 W, yet the model–experiment discrepancy is 2.4 W. Refining the mesh further is unlikely to resolve a missing convection resistance or a conductivity error. Check residuals, boundary settings and energy conservation first, then challenge physical assumptions and measurements separately. State which uncertainty sources were quantified and which remain qualitative.

Practical uncertainty propagation

For a prediction y=f(x₁,…,xₙ) with small independent input uncertainties, one common first-order method uses u_y²≈Σ(∂f/∂x_i)²u_i². For correlated inputs there are additional covariance terms. The approximation may fail with strongly nonlinear responses, broad uncertainties, discontinuous regimes or parameters near physical limits. Monte Carlo sampling can help if plausible input distributions are available, but arbitrary normal distributions must not be treated as evidence. In all uncertainty analyses, specify whether quoted intervals are standard uncertainties, expanded uncertainties or observed ranges.

Engineering reporting checklist

A reproducible transport calculation should make it possible for a separate reader to reconstruct its main result. Record the domain geometry; draw the direction of positive flux; write all governing laws and their units; identify every boundary and initial condition; document material properties and their sources; and state any empirical correlation and validity limits. Include at least one independent limiting-case calculation, such as zero gradient causing zero pure conductive flux or a vanishing reaction rate giving nearly complete catalyst utilization. Report numerical solver settings if applicable, including grid resolution, timestep and residual definition. Finally, distinguish what was directly measured, what was fitted, what was computed and what was inferred. This separation prevents a simplified teaching simulation from being mistaken for a validated industrial design tool.
