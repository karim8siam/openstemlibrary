§8.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Equipment Scaling, Hydrodynamics and Dispersion

Scope: Area; residence time; dispersion; experimental coefficients. Four lessons connect microscopic transport or process operating principles to practical calculations. Relevant laboratory models: 18.

Central engineering ideas

Equipment scale-up should distinguish physical similarity from equal nominal equipment size. Interfacial area, energy dissipation, liquid circulation and residence-time distribution often change even when fluid composition and bulk temperature are preserved. A prototype can fit mass-transfer coefficients over a narrow regime yet fail at scale because gas bubbles coalesce or flow bypasses wetted surfaces.

Use carefully selected nondimensional criteria, not indiscriminate matching of every group: matching Reynolds and Froude numbers simultaneously may be impossible when fluid and length scale are fixed. Tracer response and transient uptake reveal different aspects of equipment behavior. Quantify uncertainty on coefficients and validate outside the calibration points; otherwise excellent curve fitting can conceal a weak model.

Four-lesson reading path

Lesson 1: Interfacial area and geometric scaling

Equal tank volume does not imply equal interfacial area or mass-transfer performance, especially when bubble sizes change. The working equation a=6 epsilon_g/d_b for spherical bubbles makes a limited, testable claim about the dependence of transfer on specified properties. A larger bioreactor with coarse bubbles may lose kLa even at comparable superficial gas speed. The validity check is especially important here: Foam, coalescence and rheology change the bubble population. Analysis, synthesis and laboratory practice

First create a one-page property table: list fluid phase, bulk composition, surface or interface state, temperature, pressure, and the reference from which viscosity or diffusivity was obtained. Next sketch the principal control volume and write a component balance even when a transfer correlation is already known. Conservation closure should be computed using independent inventory data, not simply by substituting the computed flux back into the same equation.

Then use the simulation models to build a two-dimensional experiment: perform one sweep in the primary adjustable parameter and repeat at two distinct fixed values of a secondary parameter. Compare the resulting curves and explain whether the ranking changes. When model rankings do change, identify the competing effects. Where the equation predicts no change with a parameter that matters experimentally, the discrepancy is useful evidence of a missing mechanism rather than a reason to manipulate the fit.

For technical reporting, preserve the input CSV, baseline calculation, units and full list of assumptions. Include a short paragraph that distinguishes model sensitivity from actual process uncertainty, and identify one input that should be measured more precisely before design decisions. Link the interpretation to a measurable concentration, flow, pressure or inventory instead of treating the plotted output as a stand-alone achievement.

Suggested learning evidence: a control-volume sketch, two parameter sweeps, a worked balance, one falsification experiment and a paragraph about uncertainty.

Practical measurements have time constants. §8.7 Experimental Laboratory Investigation: Laboratory 15: Concentration polarization ratio

Laboratory 15: Concentration polarization ratio

Relevant unit: Membrane transport. Governing relationship: c_m/c_b=exp(J_v/k).

Physical question

What happens to the predicted concentration polarization ratio when its principal adjustable parameter changes, and how does that sensitivity relate to the mechanism described in this unit? The baseline parameter values are synthetic teaching inputs, not measurements from a named plant. This experiment is designed to expose a mathematical trend and make its units visible before real property data are substituted.

Experimental setup and protocol

Perfect rejection, film theory and equal velocity units. List the assumptions on paper before changing inputs. Treat the first control as the independent variable; keep the remaining parameters fixed for the first sweep. Read the numerical output at the minimum, baseline and maximum setting, then export the 121-point curve. Repeat at a second value of the parameter most strongly coupled to the driving force. Use the on-screen schematic to identify the direction of transport and the conceptual location of the limiting resistance. It is not a scale drawing.

Parameters

  • Permeate velocity: default 4 μm/s, adjustable range 0.2–12 μm/s. Check the measurement source and correlation domain before substitution.
  • Boundary-layer coefficient: default 35 μm/s, adjustable range 8–90 μm/s. Check the measurement source and correlation domain before substitution.

Interpretation and model boundaries

Membrane permeance is a module-scale property that summarizes the driving-force to flux response. Osmotic pressure and hydraulic pressure oppose each other in pressure-driven desalination, while concentration polarization increases the solute concentration next to the membrane. Because membrane transport depends on chemistry, fouling and mechanical compression, a clean-water straight-line relation should be labeled a screening model.

The response curve is a local sensitivity plot in which other controls remain fixed. It does not automatically represent a physical trajectory through a reactor or a separation column. A reported ''outside model validity'' result indicates a mathematical or physical constraint such as an inverted concentration difference, invalid pinch, an incompatible moisture ordering, or a correlation beyond its specified regime. The laboratory deliberately leaves these cases visible so they can be diagnosed instead of silently clipped.

Investigation questions

  1. Calculate the baseline result independently using c_m/c_b=exp(J_v/k). Convert all parameter units first. What is the largest source of numerical uncertainty?
  2. Explain the curvature or straightness of the sweep. Does doubling the first parameter double, halve, or otherwise change the response?
  3. Which plausible operating effect is excluded by the model: property variation, pressure drop, fouling, chemical reaction, unsteady accumulation, or imperfect contact? Describe a test for that effect.
  4. If a customer specification required the output to improve by 15%, identify two possible changes and discuss an operational cost or safety implication of each.

Use restriction: Educational sensitivity model; it is not a substitute for verified correlations, material data, equipment design standards or engineering sign-off.
