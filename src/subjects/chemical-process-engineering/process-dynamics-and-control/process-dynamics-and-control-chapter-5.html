§5.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Instrumentation, Sensor Dynamics and Control Valves

Scope: calibration and measurement uncertainty; response lag; valves and saturation; communication failures. Cross-cutting case: sensor–transmitter–controller–valve chain on a plant skid.

What this unit builds

In process engineering, a dynamic model is an accounting statement about stored material, thermal energy, momentum or an instrument state. Every variable must correspond to an observable or an explicitly defined internal state. The useful question is not simply whether the differential equation can be integrated. It is whether the model has the right conservation structure, signs and operating range to support a prediction that could be checked against new data.

Lesson sequence

1. [Calibration, units, and uncertainty](../lessons/unit-05-lesson-01.html) — Calibration identifies scale and offset while uncertainty describes a credible dispersion of reported values. A precise display can still be systematically biased; instrument specification, calibration history and environmental effects matter. Governing relation: `y_m=a y_true+b+epsilon`.

2. [Sensor dynamics, filtering, and noise](../lessons/unit-05-lesson-02.html) — Measurement lags delay useful information, while filtering reduces noise at the cost of phase delay. Placement, thermowell mass and digital filter settings can matter as much as nominal sensor accuracy. Governing relation: `tau_s dy_m/dt+y_m=y_true+n`.

3. [Valve flow characteristics and saturation](../lessons/unit-05-lesson-03.html) — A position command maps to flow through a nonlinear characteristic coupled to pressure drop. Equal-percentage and linear trim describe intrinsic curves; installed behavior also depends on piping, actuator hysteresis and varying upstream pressure. Governing relation: `q=Cv(u) sqrt(Delta P/rho)`.

4. [Signal failures, modes, and final-element limits](../lessons/unit-05-lesson-04.html) — A controller communicates through signal conversion and actuators; saturation, manual overrides, communication loss and fail-safe position all affect actual plant behavior. Control logic is not a substitute for an independently designed protective system. Governing relation: `u_actual=clip(u_request,u_min,u_max)`.

Model connections and physical interpretation

Calibration, units, and uncertainty

Calibration identifies scale and offset while uncertainty describes a credible dispersion of reported values. A precise display can still be systematically biased; instrument specification, calibration history and environmental effects matter. Linear span mapping assumes no clipping and correct transmitter range; nonlinear thermocouples require appropriate conversion tables. The calculation that anchors this lesson is deliberately synthetic: A sensor transmitting 4–20 mA over 0–200 °C reports 12 mA at ideal midscale 100 °C. A +0.4 mA offset corresponds to +5 °C in this mapping. The reasoning should be checked in the next topic, because a dynamic approximation useful for one type of experiment may be inappropriate when the controlled input, disturbance or measurement location changes.

Sensor dynamics, filtering, and noise

Measurement lags delay useful information, while filtering reduces noise at the cost of phase delay. Placement, thermowell mass and digital filter settings can matter as much as nominal sensor accuracy. The one-lag probe law is an approximation; changing flow may change convection and sensor response time. The calculation that anchors this lesson is deliberately synthetic: A first-order probe with tau_s=8 s sees a 10 °C sudden true rise; after 8 s it has registered only 6.32 °C of that rise. A second filter can increase the apparent delay. The reasoning should be checked in the next topic, because a dynamic approximation useful for one type of experiment may be inappropriate when the controlled input, disturbance or measurement location changes.

Valve flow characteristics and saturation

A position command maps to flow through a nonlinear characteristic coupled to pressure drop. Equal-percentage and linear trim describe intrinsic curves; installed behavior also depends on piping, actuator hysteresis and varying upstream pressure. The simplified expression omits flashing, cavitation, compressibility and detailed valve standards. The calculation that anchors this lesson is deliberately synthetic: For equal-percentage rangeability R=50, relative capacity at 50% opening is 50^(0.5−1)=1/sqrt(50)=0.141, not 0.50. At 100% it reaches 1.0 by the ideal characteristic. The reasoning should be checked in the next topic, because a dynamic approximation useful for one type of experiment may be inappropriate when the controlled input, disturbance or measurement location changes.

Signal failures, modes, and final-element limits

A controller communicates through signal conversion and actuators; saturation, manual overrides, communication loss and fail-safe position all affect actual plant behavior. Control logic is not a substitute for an independently designed protective system. Fail-open versus fail-closed is a hazard-analysis decision, not a universal default derived from controller mathematics. The calculation that anchors this lesson is deliberately synthetic: A requested 115% valve output clips at a configured 100%; the missing 15 percentage points cannot be recovered by increasing controller gain. If integral keeps accumulating, recovery is slower. The reasoning should be checked in the next topic, because a dynamic approximation useful for one type of experiment may be inappropriate when the controlled input, disturbance or measurement location changes.

Unit synthesis: a reproducible experiment

Start from sensor–transmitter–controller–valve chain on a plant skid. Draw the boundary and place every relevant measurement on it. Give the operating point, consistent units and any assumptions about constant density, heat capacity or sensor behavior. Construct a hypothesis that could be tested with a small safe, synthetic input perturbation. Predict the output sign and a first approximation for the relevant time scale before computing anything.

Next compare a nominal model with one deliberately misspecified version. Change a gain, a lag, a delay or a sensor bias rather than modifying all uncertain features at once. Quantify the difference in output peak, settling time, residual steady error and manipulated-variable excursion. Record when the model ceases to be a good representation of the exercise. A successful classroom exercise is a transparent chain of assumptions and checks, not merely a curve.

Finally design a holdout test. In a process setting this might be a second flow rate, a different operating temperature or a load disturbance that was not included in model fitting. Compare model and measurement on the same time grid, keep a reproducible data table, and explain why any fitted coefficient cannot be assumed constant outside its observed range. Treat alarm and protective requirements as a separate design decision.

§5.2 Unit 5, Lesson 1: Calibration, units, and uncertainty

Unit 5, Lesson 1: Calibration, units, and uncertainty

Unit: Instruments, Sensors, and Final Control Elements. Physical setting: sensor–transmitter–controller–valve chain on a plant skid. Reference model: y_m=a y_true+b+epsilon.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson you should be able to (i) explain calibration, units, and uncertainty in physical terms, (ii) identify the variables and assumptions of y_m=a y_true+b+epsilon, (iii) reproduce and challenge a numerical benchmark, (iv) recognize how measurement, delay and actuator limits modify the model, and (v) design a specific experiment that can distinguish the current model from an alternative interpretation.

Physical reasoning, not just a formula

Calibration identifies scale and offset while uncertainty describes a credible dispersion of reported values. A precise display can still be systematically biased; instrument specification, calibration history and environmental effects matter.

Consider the unit-wide equipment case: sensor–transmitter–controller–valve chain on a plant skid. In this setting the same numerical trend may have more than one physical cause. A change observed at the analyzer may reflect a real process response, a changed flow residence time or the instrument dynamics. The analyst must first specify which quantity is manipulated, which is observed and which variables are treated as external disturbances. This is particularly important for calibration, units, and uncertainty, where confusing a state with a measured output can give a mathematically solvable but physically incorrect model.

The chosen governing expression is y_m=a y_true+b+epsilon. It is a compact model, not a complete plant specification. Terms on opposite sides of an equality must have compatible units and consistent time bases. When a parameter is estimated from one transient, its result belongs to that operating range unless new data demonstrate transferability. The task is to explain the meaning of the relation before substituting numbers and then to compare its predictions with a checkable boundary condition or steady state.

Working the numerical reference case

Synthetic worked example. A sensor transmitting 4–20 mA over 0–200 °C reports 12 mA at ideal midscale 100 °C. A +0.4 mA offset corresponds to +5 °C in this mapping.

First transcribe every numerical input into a short table with its physical unit. Second, compute the baseline result without a software solver. Third, identify which output should change and which should remain unchanged when the manipulated input is zero. In this calibration, units, and uncertainty exercise, the reported figures are not fitted plant data: they are an internally stated teaching benchmark. Any extension to different equipment requires new coefficients and new validation.

A useful sensitivity check is to change the selected coefficient or input by +10% while retaining all other conditions. This is not equivalent to assuming the output must also rise by 10%; exponential responses, ratios, saturation and feedback can change the mapping. Write the original expression, substitute the perturbed value and compare the sign of the result against physical intuition. Next repeat with a −10% change. An asymmetric response is evidence of nonlinearity in the selected mapping, not necessarily a numerical error.

Derivation and model restrictions

Linear span mapping assumes no clipping and correct transmitter range; nonlinear thermocouples require appropriate conversion tables. To turn a physical inventory into a differential state equation, write accumulation as incoming rate minus outgoing rate plus net production, if relevant. To turn an instrument or actuator into a dynamical element, specify its output, time constant and initial condition. To build a transfer function, take deviations about a steady operating point and verify that the assumed initial deviations are zero. Do not apply frequency-domain algebra blindly to a strongly nonlinear or switching regime.

For calibration, units, and uncertainty, the limiting checks should include a zero-change input, a long-time equilibrium where one exists and at least one infeasible input that the physical equipment cannot realize. If a parameter has dimensions of reciprocal time, use it only with a time expressed in matching units. If a parameter multiplies a temperature difference, its unit must convert the difference into a heat rate before the quantity is added to the energy balance. These simple checks detect mistakes that a graph renderer will not.

How to identify the model experimentally

Use the previously studied topic Calibration, units, and uncertainty as a source of prior knowledge and the next topic Sensor dynamics, filtering, and noise as an independent challenge. Define the reference operating point for calibration, units, and uncertainty in the process case and record a small safe input sequence, the actual actuator trajectory, the measurement times and instrument range. A step, a pulse and a slow ramp probe different properties, so do not collapse them into one claimed validation. If the fitted output differs from the recorded trace, inspect the timestamp alignment and sensor dynamics before increasing model order.

Where possible, obtain at least two input amplitudes. Normalize the measured output by the change in input and test whether the curves approximately collapse. Failure to collapse can indicate nonlinearity, saturation, temperature-dependent coefficients or changed initial conditions. A second holdout operating point is especially important when the main formula was derived by linearization. Report the residual error and uncertainty rather than relabeling an imperfect approximation as a universal physical law.

Uncertainty, troubleshooting, and design decisions

Failure mode to investigate: Mistaking a calibration offset for a process disturbance makes the controller force the true process away from its desired value. The first diagnostic question is which conservation law, measurement chain or actuator path the proposed explanation implicates. Reconstruct the actual manipulated-variable history and compare it with what the controller requested; a large difference is a plant-side constraint, not a parameter-estimation error that can be fixed by fitting a more elaborate process transfer function.

Decision from the evidence: Verify endpoints, midspan, repeatability and an independent reference while retaining raw instrument signals. State what the experiment supports and what remains unidentifiable. The decision should include whether the next useful action is to add a sensor, run a second input condition, change an assumed model structure or restrict the admissible operating range. When model outputs could guide actual equipment, independent engineering review and safety analysis are necessary before any implementation.

A challenge to the simplest explanation

Compare the present explanation with Valve flow characteristics and saturation, which appears elsewhere in this unit. Imagine obtaining a smooth measured response that both models could plausibly produce. Choose one additional measurement that would change differently under the two explanations. Define the expected sign and time scale under each. For calibration, units, and uncertainty, record not just peak error but also the total actuator travel, disturbances, measurement noise and how the reference signal was generated. This exercise forces you to separate apparent goodness of fit from the ability to predict new conditions.

Numerical implementation and independent checks

The companion [interactive model, Laboratory 09](../labs/lab-09.html) is Sensor dynamics and measurement noise. Change one slider at a time, export the series and independently reproduce at least one value from a governing equation. The browser tool is deterministic and offline. It represents a defined educational model, not an approved process simulator. Its chart scale may rescale as parameters change, so compare numerical coordinates rather than visual slope alone.

For a numerical transient, verify step-size sensitivity by recomputing with half the time increment. If a predicted instability disappears merely when the integration time step shrinks, it was likely numerical rather than physical. On the other hand, an unstable physical model may diverge more quickly when more accurately integrated. Preserve initial conditions, parameter units and exact software version in the report so another student can repeat the test.

Practice and oral-examination questions

  1. Starting from y_m=a y_true+b+epsilon, identify its state(s), manipulated input(s) and measured output(s). State how you would verify all dimensions.
  2. Recalculate the example after a 10% increase of the principal process parameter. What changes in the predicted steady value and in the transient, and why are these not necessarily the same?
  3. Propose a sensor or actuator failure that would make an otherwise correct calibration, units, and uncertainty model appear wrong. What extra logged variable would distinguish that failure?
  4. Explain how Valve flow characteristics and saturation might confound the identification of this lesson’s mechanism and propose a discriminating test.
  5. State one safety or operating limit that the simplified y_m=a y_true+b+epsilon relation does not capture. How should an engineer document that omission?

Answer guide and reflection

Question 1 is assessed by correct units and by separating the actual physical inventory from its measured signal; a memorized symbolic expression without those definitions is insufficient. For Question 2, the correct method uses the original equation twice and recomputes the equilibrium or time constant where applicable; simple proportional scaling is not accepted without showing that the equation is linear in the varied coefficient. For Question 3, the measurement or actuator diagnosis must lead to a different predicted trace. For Question 4, a proposed experiment is credible only if it can distinguish two candidate mechanisms. For Question 5, a constraint must be named and its omission explicitly stated.

Takeaway for Instruments, Sensors, and Final Control Elements. The lesson is complete only after the model can be challenged with a changed initial condition, a disturbance and a non-ideal measurement. A plotted agreement under one nominal set of synthetic conditions is the start of investigation, not its end. The next step is to test whether the assumptions retain their meaning when sensor dynamics, filtering, and noise is included in the model.

§5.5 Unit 5, Lesson 4: Signal failures, modes, and final-element limits

Unit 5, Lesson 4: Signal failures, modes, and final-element limits

Unit: Instruments, Sensors, and Final Control Elements. Physical setting: sensor–transmitter–controller–valve chain on a plant skid. Reference model: u_actual=clip(u_request,u_min,u_max).

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson you should be able to (i) explain signal failures, modes, and final-element limits in physical terms, (ii) identify the variables and assumptions of u_actual=clip(u_request,u_min,u_max), (iii) reproduce and challenge a numerical benchmark, (iv) recognize how measurement, delay and actuator limits modify the model, and (v) design a specific experiment that can distinguish the current model from an alternative interpretation.

Physical reasoning, not just a formula

A controller communicates through signal conversion and actuators; saturation, manual overrides, communication loss and fail-safe position all affect actual plant behavior. Control logic is not a substitute for an independently designed protective system.

Consider the unit-wide equipment case: sensor–transmitter–controller–valve chain on a plant skid. In this setting the same numerical trend may have more than one physical cause. A change observed at the analyzer may reflect a real process response, a changed flow residence time or the instrument dynamics. The analyst must first specify which quantity is manipulated, which is observed and which variables are treated as external disturbances. This is particularly important for signal failures, modes, and final-element limits, where confusing a state with a measured output can give a mathematically solvable but physically incorrect model.

The chosen governing expression is u_actual=clip(u_request,u_min,u_max). It is a compact model, not a complete plant specification. Terms on opposite sides of an equality must have compatible units and consistent time bases. When a parameter is estimated from one transient, its result belongs to that operating range unless new data demonstrate transferability. The task is to explain the meaning of the relation before substituting numbers and then to compare its predictions with a checkable boundary condition or steady state.

Working the numerical reference case

Synthetic worked example. A requested 115% valve output clips at a configured 100%; the missing 15 percentage points cannot be recovered by increasing controller gain. If integral keeps accumulating, recovery is slower.

First transcribe every numerical input into a short table with its physical unit. Second, compute the baseline result without a software solver. Third, identify which output should change and which should remain unchanged when the manipulated input is zero. In this signal failures, modes, and final-element limits exercise, the reported figures are not fitted plant data: they are an internally stated teaching benchmark. Any extension to different equipment requires new coefficients and new validation.

A useful sensitivity check is to change the selected coefficient or input by +10% while retaining all other conditions. This is not equivalent to assuming the output must also rise by 10%; exponential responses, ratios, saturation and feedback can change the mapping. Write the original expression, substitute the perturbed value and compare the sign of the result against physical intuition. Next repeat with a −10% change. An asymmetric response is evidence of nonlinearity in the selected mapping, not necessarily a numerical error.

Derivation and model restrictions

Fail-open versus fail-closed is a hazard-analysis decision, not a universal default derived from controller mathematics. To turn a physical inventory into a differential state equation, write accumulation as incoming rate minus outgoing rate plus net production, if relevant. To turn an instrument or actuator into a dynamical element, specify its output, time constant and initial condition. To build a transfer function, take deviations about a steady operating point and verify that the assumed initial deviations are zero. Do not apply frequency-domain algebra blindly to a strongly nonlinear or switching regime.

For signal failures, modes, and final-element limits, the limiting checks should include a zero-change input, a long-time equilibrium where one exists and at least one infeasible input that the physical equipment cannot realize. If a parameter has dimensions of reciprocal time, use it only with a time expressed in matching units. If a parameter multiplies a temperature difference, its unit must convert the difference into a heat rate before the quantity is added to the energy balance. These simple checks detect mistakes that a graph renderer will not.

How to identify the model experimentally

Use the previously studied topic Valve flow characteristics and saturation as a source of prior knowledge and the next topic Signal failures, modes, and final-element limits as an independent challenge. Define the reference operating point for signal failures, modes, and final-element limits in the process case and record a small safe input sequence, the actual actuator trajectory, the measurement times and instrument range. A step, a pulse and a slow ramp probe different properties, so do not collapse them into one claimed validation. If the fitted output differs from the recorded trace, inspect the timestamp alignment and sensor dynamics before increasing model order.

Where possible, obtain at least two input amplitudes. Normalize the measured output by the change in input and test whether the curves approximately collapse. Failure to collapse can indicate nonlinearity, saturation, temperature-dependent coefficients or changed initial conditions. A second holdout operating point is especially important when the main formula was derived by linearization. Report the residual error and uncertainty rather than relabeling an imperfect approximation as a universal physical law.

Uncertainty, troubleshooting, and design decisions

Failure mode to investigate: A software simulation that ignores output clamps predicts unrealistic recovery from severe disturbances. The first diagnostic question is which conservation law, measurement chain or actuator path the proposed explanation implicates. Reconstruct the actual manipulated-variable history and compare it with what the controller requested; a large difference is a plant-side constraint, not a parameter-estimation error that can be fixed by fitting a more elaborate process transfer function.

Decision from the evidence: Document mode, signal quality, measured actuator position and failure response in commissioning tests. State what the experiment supports and what remains unidentifiable. The decision should include whether the next useful action is to add a sensor, run a second input condition, change an assumed model structure or restrict the admissible operating range. When model outputs could guide actual equipment, independent engineering review and safety analysis are necessary before any implementation.

A challenge to the simplest explanation

Compare the present explanation with Sensor dynamics, filtering, and noise, which appears elsewhere in this unit. Imagine obtaining a smooth measured response that both models could plausibly produce. Choose one additional measurement that would change differently under the two explanations. Define the expected sign and time scale under each. For signal failures, modes, and final-element limits, record not just peak error but also the total actuator travel, disturbances, measurement noise and how the reference signal was generated. This exercise forces you to separate apparent goodness of fit from the ability to predict new conditions.
