Time-Domain and Frequency-Domain Responses

Comprehensive university textbook unit covering fundamental theory, mathematical derivations, numerical modeling, and process engineering validation of Time-Domain and Frequency-Domain Responses. Developed for undergraduate chemical engineering study.

§2.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Time-Domain and Frequency-Domain Responses

Scope: first-order and second-order transients; dead time estimation; disturbances and periodic forcing. Cross-cutting case: an instrumented heat-exchanger outlet temperature step test.

What this unit builds

A time response must be read together with the input that created it. A step response without the actuator's recorded movement can conceal valve saturation. A smooth trace without sensor location can conceal delay. When two mechanisms both explain the nominal trend, they must be tested using a disturbance or experiment that separates their effects, not selected on visual appearance alone.

Lesson sequence

1. [First-order step and impulse responses](../lessons/unit-02-lesson-01.html) — A first-order stable system reaches 63.2% of its final step change at one time constant. Its impulse response decays exponentially; a measured step trace can suggest K and tau but not automatically establish linearity. Governing relation: `y(t)=K Delta u [1-exp(-t/tau)]`.

2. [Second-order damping and overshoot](../lessons/unit-02-lesson-02.html) — A pair of dynamic states can create underdamped oscillation. Damping ratio controls overshoot; natural frequency sets the basic clock. A negative derivative signal or actuator limitation can change an apparently textbook response. Governing relation: `G(s)=omega_n²/(s²+2 zeta omega_n s+omega_n²)`.

3. [FOPDT identification and uncertainty](../lessons/unit-02-lesson-03.html) — A first-order-plus-dead-time model compresses process behavior into gain, lag and effective delay. Parameter fitting is a modeling convenience; different triples can fit a noisy short experiment, especially if the final value is unknown. Governing relation: `G(s)=K exp(-theta s)/(tau s+1)`.

4. [Disturbance and sinusoidal frequency response](../lessons/unit-02-lesson-04.html) — Transient step tests emphasize low-frequency gain; sinusoidal forcing probes attenuation and phase shift. A system that passes slowly varying disturbances may still suppress faster oscillations or suffer added delay. Governing relation: `|G(jw)|=K/sqrt(1+(w tau)^2)`.

Model connections and physical interpretation

First-order step and impulse responses

A first-order stable system reaches 63.2% of its final step change at one time constant. Its impulse response decays exponentially; a measured step trace can suggest K and tau but not automatically establish linearity. A real actuator step may be ramp-limited, and sampled measurements introduce time resolution and uncertainty. The calculation that anchors this lesson is deliberately synthetic: If K=2 K/% and input changes 3%, the final temperature gain is 6 K. At tau=40 s, the step response is 6(1−exp(−1))=3.79 K above baseline. The reasoning should be checked in the next topic, because a dynamic approximation useful for one type of experiment may be inappropriate when the controlled input, disturbance or measurement location changes.

Second-order damping and overshoot

A pair of dynamic states can create underdamped oscillation. Damping ratio controls overshoot; natural frequency sets the basic clock. A negative derivative signal or actuator limitation can change an apparently textbook response. The stated overshoot relation requires a linear, normalized, no-zero, underdamped second-order step response. The calculation that anchors this lesson is deliberately synthetic: For zeta=0.40, percent overshoot is exp(-pi zeta/sqrt(1−zeta²))*100=25.4%. With omega_n=0.20 rad/s, the ideal first peak is t_p=pi/(omega_n sqrt(1−zeta²))=17.1 s. The reasoning should be checked in the next topic, because a dynamic approximation useful for one type of experiment may be inappropriate when the controlled input, disturbance or measurement location changes.

FOPDT identification and uncertainty

A first-order-plus-dead-time model compresses process behavior into gain, lag and effective delay. Parameter fitting is a modeling convenience; different triples can fit a noisy short experiment, especially if the final value is unknown. The tangent method and direct nonlinear least squares may produce different estimates. Input saturation should be excluded. The calculation that anchors this lesson is deliberately synthetic: A 4% input step yields a 6 °C steady output change: K=1.5 °C/%. A discernible first response occurs after theta=12 s, and 63.2% of the remaining change is observed at t≈62 s, suggesting tau≈50 s. The reasoning should be checked in the next topic, because a dynamic approximation useful for one type of experiment may be inappropriate when the controlled input, disturbance or measurement location changes.

Disturbance and sinusoidal frequency response

Transient step tests emphasize low-frequency gain; sinusoidal forcing probes attenuation and phase shift. A system that passes slowly varying disturbances may still suppress faster oscillations or suffer added delay. Steady harmonic formulas omit startup transients and rely on linearity at the forced amplitude. The calculation that anchors this lesson is deliberately synthetic: For K=3, tau=20 s and angular frequency 0.10 rad/s, amplitude ratio is 3/sqrt(5)=1.342 and phase shift is −atan(2)=−63.4°. The reasoning should be checked in the next topic, because a dynamic approximation useful for one type of experiment may be inappropriate when the controlled input, disturbance or measurement location changes.

Unit synthesis: a reproducible experiment

Start from an instrumented heat-exchanger outlet temperature step test. Draw the boundary and place every relevant measurement on it. Give the operating point, consistent units and any assumptions about constant density, heat capacity or sensor behavior. Construct a hypothesis that could be tested with a small safe, synthetic input perturbation. Predict the output sign and a first approximation for the relevant time scale before computing anything.

Next compare a nominal model with one deliberately misspecified version. Change a gain, a lag, a delay or a sensor bias rather than modifying all uncertain features at once. Quantify the difference in output peak, settling time, residual steady error and manipulated-variable excursion. Record when the model ceases to be a good representation of the exercise. A successful classroom exercise is a transparent chain of assumptions and checks, not merely a curve.

Finally design a holdout test. In a process setting this might be a second flow rate, a different operating temperature or a load disturbance that was not included in model fitting. Compare model and measurement on the same time grid, keep a reproducible data table, and explain why any fitted coefficient cannot be assumed constant outside its observed range. Treat alarm and protective requirements as a separate design decision.

Unit knowledge check

  1. For First-order step and impulse responses, state the terms of y(t)=K Delta u [1-exp(-t/tau)], choose a limiting or equilibrium case, and explain when it would be invalid.
  2. For Second-order damping and overshoot, state the terms of G(s)=omega_n²/(s²+2 zeta omega_n s+omega_n²), choose a limiting or equilibrium case, and explain when it would be invalid.
  3. For FOPDT identification and uncertainty, state the terms of G(s)=K exp(-theta s)/(tau s+1), choose a limiting or equilibrium case, and explain when it would be invalid.
  4. For Disturbance and sinusoidal frequency response, state the terms of |G(jw)|=K/sqrt(1+(w tau)^2), choose a limiting or equilibrium case, and explain when it would be invalid.

§2.2 Unit 2, Lesson 1: First-order step and impulse responses

Unit 2, Lesson 1: First-order step and impulse responses

Unit: Time-Domain and Frequency-Domain Responses. Physical setting: an instrumented heat-exchanger outlet temperature step test. Reference model: y(t)=K Delta u [1-exp(-t/tau)].

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson you should be able to (i) explain first-order step and impulse responses in physical terms, (ii) identify the variables and assumptions of y(t)=K Delta u [1-exp(-t/tau)], (iii) reproduce and challenge a numerical benchmark, (iv) recognize how measurement, delay and actuator limits modify the model, and (v) design a specific experiment that can distinguish the current model from an alternative interpretation.

Physical reasoning, not just a formula

A first-order stable system reaches 63.2% of its final step change at one time constant. Its impulse response decays exponentially; a measured step trace can suggest K and tau but not automatically establish linearity.

Consider the unit-wide equipment case: an instrumented heat-exchanger outlet temperature step test. In this setting the same numerical trend may have more than one physical cause. A change observed at the analyzer may reflect a real process response, a changed flow residence time or the instrument dynamics. The analyst must first specify which quantity is manipulated, which is observed and which variables are treated as external disturbances. This is particularly important for first-order step and impulse responses, where confusing a state with a measured output can give a mathematically solvable but physically incorrect model.

The chosen governing expression is y(t)=K Delta u [1-exp(-t/tau)]. It is a compact model, not a complete plant specification. Terms on opposite sides of an equality must have compatible units and consistent time bases. When a parameter is estimated from one transient, its result belongs to that operating range unless new data demonstrate transferability. The task is to explain the meaning of the relation before substituting numbers and then to compare its predictions with a checkable boundary condition or steady state.

Working the numerical reference case

Synthetic worked example. If K=2 K/% and input changes 3%, the final temperature gain is 6 K. At tau=40 s, the step response is 6(1−exp(−1))=3.79 K above baseline.

First transcribe every numerical input into a short table with its physical unit. Second, compute the baseline result without a software solver. Third, identify which output should change and which should remain unchanged when the manipulated input is zero. In this first-order step and impulse responses exercise, the reported figures are not fitted plant data: they are an internally stated teaching benchmark. Any extension to different equipment requires new coefficients and new validation.

A useful sensitivity check is to change the selected coefficient or input by +10% while retaining all other conditions. This is not equivalent to assuming the output must also rise by 10%; exponential responses, ratios, saturation and feedback can change the mapping. Write the original expression, substitute the perturbed value and compare the sign of the result against physical intuition. Next repeat with a −10% change. An asymmetric response is evidence of nonlinearity in the selected mapping, not necessarily a numerical error.

Derivation and model restrictions

A real actuator step may be ramp-limited, and sampled measurements introduce time resolution and uncertainty. To turn a physical inventory into a differential state equation, write accumulation as incoming rate minus outgoing rate plus net production, if relevant. To turn an instrument or actuator into a dynamical element, specify its output, time constant and initial condition. To build a transfer function, take deviations about a steady operating point and verify that the assumed initial deviations are zero. Do not apply frequency-domain algebra blindly to a strongly nonlinear or switching regime.

For first-order step and impulse responses, the limiting checks should include a zero-change input, a long-time equilibrium where one exists and at least one infeasible input that the physical equipment cannot realize. If a parameter has dimensions of reciprocal time, use it only with a time expressed in matching units. If a parameter multiplies a temperature difference, its unit must convert the difference into a heat rate before the quantity is added to the energy balance. These simple checks detect mistakes that a graph renderer will not.

How to identify the model experimentally

Use the previously studied topic First-order step and impulse responses as a source of prior knowledge and the next topic Second-order damping and overshoot as an independent challenge. Define the reference operating point for first-order step and impulse responses in the process case and record a small safe input sequence, the actual actuator trajectory, the measurement times and instrument range. A step, a pulse and a slow ramp probe different properties, so do not collapse them into one claimed validation. If the fitted output differs from the recorded trace, inspect the timestamp alignment and sensor dynamics before increasing model order.

Where possible, obtain at least two input amplitudes. Normalize the measured output by the change in input and test whether the curves approximately collapse. Failure to collapse can indicate nonlinearity, saturation, temperature-dependent coefficients or changed initial conditions. A second holdout operating point is especially important when the main formula was derived by linearization. Report the residual error and uncertainty rather than relabeling an imperfect approximation as a universal physical law.

Uncertainty, troubleshooting, and design decisions

Failure mode to investigate: Forcing a non-exponential response into a one-lag model can hide parallel heat-transfer paths or a poorly mixed vessel. The first diagnostic question is which conservation law, measurement chain or actuator path the proposed explanation implicates. Reconstruct the actual manipulated-variable history and compare it with what the controller requested; a large difference is a plant-side constraint, not a parameter-estimation error that can be fixed by fitting a more elaborate process transfer function.

Decision from the evidence: Calculate gain from the final difference, estimate tau from 63.2%, and reserve later test data for validation. State what the experiment supports and what remains unidentifiable. The decision should include whether the next useful action is to add a sensor, run a second input condition, change an assumed model structure or restrict the admissible operating range. When model outputs could guide actual equipment, independent engineering review and safety analysis are necessary before any implementation.

A challenge to the simplest explanation

Compare the present explanation with FOPDT identification and uncertainty, which appears elsewhere in this unit. Imagine obtaining a smooth measured response that both models could plausibly produce. Choose one additional measurement that would change differently under the two explanations. Define the expected sign and time scale under each. For first-order step and impulse responses, record not just peak error but also the total actuator travel, disturbances, measurement noise and how the reference signal was generated. This exercise forces you to separate apparent goodness of fit from the ability to predict new conditions.

Numerical implementation and independent checks

The companion [interactive model, Laboratory 01](../labs/lab-01.html) is First-order thermal step response. Change one slider at a time, export the series and independently reproduce at least one value from a governing equation. The browser tool is deterministic and offline. It represents a defined educational model, not an approved process simulator. Its chart scale may rescale as parameters change, so compare numerical coordinates rather than visual slope alone.

For a numerical transient, verify step-size sensitivity by recomputing with half the time increment. If a predicted instability disappears merely when the integration time step shrinks, it was likely numerical rather than physical. On the other hand, an unstable physical model may diverge more quickly when more accurately integrated. Preserve initial conditions, parameter units and exact software version in the report so another student can repeat the test.

Practice and oral-examination questions

  1. Starting from y(t)=K Delta u [1-exp(-t/tau)], identify its state(s), manipulated input(s) and measured output(s). State how you would verify all dimensions.
  2. Recalculate the example after a 10% increase of the principal process parameter. What changes in the predicted steady value and in the transient, and why are these not necessarily the same?
  3. Propose a sensor or actuator failure that would make an otherwise correct first-order step and impulse responses model appear wrong. What extra logged variable would distinguish that failure?
  4. Explain how FOPDT identification and uncertainty might confound the identification of this lesson’s mechanism and propose a discriminating test.
  5. State one safety or operating limit that the simplified y(t)=K Delta u [1-exp(-t/tau)] relation does not capture. How should an engineer document that omission?

Answer guide and reflection

Question 1 is assessed by correct units and by separating the actual physical inventory from its measured signal; a memorized symbolic expression without those definitions is insufficient. For Question 2, the correct method uses the original equation twice and recomputes the equilibrium or time constant where applicable; simple proportional scaling is not accepted without showing that the equation is linear in the varied coefficient. For Question 3, the measurement or actuator diagnosis must lead to a different predicted trace. For Question 4, a proposed experiment is credible only if it can distinguish two candidate mechanisms. For Question 5, a constraint must be named and its omission explicitly stated.

Takeaway for Time-Domain and Frequency-Domain Responses. The lesson is complete only after the model can be challenged with a changed initial condition, a disturbance and a non-ideal measurement. A plotted agreement under one nominal set of synthetic conditions is the start of investigation, not its end. The next step is to test whether the assumptions retain their meaning when second-order damping and overshoot is included in the model.

§2.5 Unit 2, Lesson 4: Disturbance and sinusoidal frequency response

Unit 2, Lesson 4: Disturbance and sinusoidal frequency response

Unit: Time-Domain and Frequency-Domain Responses. Physical setting: an instrumented heat-exchanger outlet temperature step test. Reference model: |G(jw)|=K/sqrt(1+(w tau)^2).

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson you should be able to (i) explain disturbance and sinusoidal frequency response in physical terms, (ii) identify the variables and assumptions of |G(jw)|=K/sqrt(1+(w tau)^2), (iii) reproduce and challenge a numerical benchmark, (iv) recognize how measurement, delay and actuator limits modify the model, and (v) design a specific experiment that can distinguish the current model from an alternative interpretation.

Physical reasoning, not just a formula

Transient step tests emphasize low-frequency gain; sinusoidal forcing probes attenuation and phase shift. A system that passes slowly varying disturbances may still suppress faster oscillations or suffer added delay.

Consider the unit-wide equipment case: an instrumented heat-exchanger outlet temperature step test. In this setting the same numerical trend may have more than one physical cause. A change observed at the analyzer may reflect a real process response, a changed flow residence time or the instrument dynamics. The analyst must first specify which quantity is manipulated, which is observed and which variables are treated as external disturbances. This is particularly important for disturbance and sinusoidal frequency response, where confusing a state with a measured output can give a mathematically solvable but physically incorrect model.

The chosen governing expression is |G(jw)|=K/sqrt(1+(w tau)^2). It is a compact model, not a complete plant specification. Terms on opposite sides of an equality must have compatible units and consistent time bases. When a parameter is estimated from one transient, its result belongs to that operating range unless new data demonstrate transferability. The task is to explain the meaning of the relation before substituting numbers and then to compare its predictions with a checkable boundary condition or steady state.

Working the numerical reference case

Synthetic worked example. For K=3, tau=20 s and angular frequency 0.10 rad/s, amplitude ratio is 3/sqrt(5)=1.342 and phase shift is −atan(2)=−63.4°.

First transcribe every numerical input into a short table with its physical unit. Second, compute the baseline result without a software solver. Third, identify which output should change and which should remain unchanged when the manipulated input is zero. In this disturbance and sinusoidal frequency response exercise, the reported figures are not fitted plant data: they are an internally stated teaching benchmark. Any extension to different equipment requires new coefficients and new validation.

A useful sensitivity check is to change the selected coefficient or input by +10% while retaining all other conditions. This is not equivalent to assuming the output must also rise by 10%; exponential responses, ratios, saturation and feedback can change the mapping. Write the original expression, substitute the perturbed value and compare the sign of the result against physical intuition. Next repeat with a −10% change. An asymmetric response is evidence of nonlinearity in the selected mapping, not necessarily a numerical error.

Derivation and model restrictions

Steady harmonic formulas omit startup transients and rely on linearity at the forced amplitude. To turn a physical inventory into a differential state equation, write accumulation as incoming rate minus outgoing rate plus net production, if relevant. To turn an instrument or actuator into a dynamical element, specify its output, time constant and initial condition. To build a transfer function, take deviations about a steady operating point and verify that the assumed initial deviations are zero. Do not apply frequency-domain algebra blindly to a strongly nonlinear or switching regime.

For disturbance and sinusoidal frequency response, the limiting checks should include a zero-change input, a long-time equilibrium where one exists and at least one infeasible input that the physical equipment cannot realize. If a parameter has dimensions of reciprocal time, use it only with a time expressed in matching units. If a parameter multiplies a temperature difference, its unit must convert the difference into a heat rate before the quantity is added to the energy balance. These simple checks detect mistakes that a graph renderer will not.

How to identify the model experimentally

Use the previously studied topic FOPDT identification and uncertainty as a source of prior knowledge and the next topic Disturbance and sinusoidal frequency response as an independent challenge. Define the reference operating point for disturbance and sinusoidal frequency response in the process case and record a small safe input sequence, the actual actuator trajectory, the measurement times and instrument range. A step, a pulse and a slow ramp probe different properties, so do not collapse them into one claimed validation. If the fitted output differs from the recorded trace, inspect the timestamp alignment and sensor dynamics before increasing model order.

Where possible, obtain at least two input amplitudes. Normalize the measured output by the change in input and test whether the curves approximately collapse. Failure to collapse can indicate nonlinearity, saturation, temperature-dependent coefficients or changed initial conditions. A second holdout operating point is especially important when the main formula was derived by linearization. Report the residual error and uncertainty rather than relabeling an imperfect approximation as a universal physical law.

Uncertainty, troubleshooting, and design decisions

Failure mode to investigate: Fitting a curve at one frequency cannot identify both lag and dead time unambiguously. The first diagnostic question is which conservation law, measurement chain or actuator path the proposed explanation implicates. Reconstruct the actual manipulated-variable history and compare it with what the controller requested; a large difference is a plant-side constraint, not a parameter-estimation error that can be fixed by fitting a more elaborate process transfer function.

Decision from the evidence: Use at least three forcing frequencies with a stable operating point, allowing transients to decay before estimating gain. State what the experiment supports and what remains unidentifiable. The decision should include whether the next useful action is to add a sensor, run a second input condition, change an assumed model structure or restrict the admissible operating range. When model outputs could guide actual equipment, independent engineering review and safety analysis are necessary before any implementation.
