§8.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Industrial Plantwide Control and Validation

Scope: level, temperature, composition, pressure; commissioning and model limitations. Cross-cutting case: an integrated process skid with multiple instruments and disturbances.

What this unit builds

Digital control does not remove continuous physics. The code operates at discrete instants while inventories, flows and heat transfer proceed continuously. Numerical integration, filtering and saturation are part of the implemented controller and may change the predicted behavior. Modeling and testing must specify the actual sampling and execution assumptions.

Lesson sequence

1. [Tank-level control and nonlinear discharge](../lessons/unit-08-lesson-01.html) — Tank-level control must match the physical inlet and outlet roles. Large tanks may tolerate slow response, whereas small surge vessels can overflow before a delayed controller reacts; nonlinear drain flow changes local gain with level. Governing relation: `A dh/dt=q_in-C sqrt(h)`.

2. [Jacket temperature and reactor heat effects](../lessons/unit-08-lesson-02.html) — Reactor temperature regulation couples thermal storage, jacket transfer, inlet enthalpy and possible reaction heat. Adding an exothermic term makes the energy equation nonlinear and may create operating regions where a one-lag approximation fails. Governing relation: `rho V Cp dT/dt=F rho Cp(Tin-T)+UA(Tj-T)+Qrxn`.

3. [Blending composition and analyzer delay](../lessons/unit-08-lesson-03.html) — Blend composition follows species inventory rather than flow ratio alone. The mixed concentration changes when either stream flow or concentration changes; an analyzer downstream adds measurement delay and often a slow sampling cycle. Governing relation: `V dx/dt=F_A(x_A-x)+F_B(x_B-x)`.

4. [Pressure and flow control with operating constraints](../lessons/unit-08-lesson-04.html) — Pressure depends on inventory, compressibility and available inlet/outlet flow paths. Pressure loops interact with pump, compressor and valve behavior; controlling a plotted pressure value is not a substitute for relief or mechanical design. Governing relation: `C dP/dt=F_in-F_out(P,u)`.

Model connections and physical interpretation

Tank-level control and nonlinear discharge

Tank-level control must match the physical inlet and outlet roles. Large tanks may tolerate slow response, whereas small surge vessels can overflow before a delayed controller reacts; nonlinear drain flow changes local gain with level. Assume a fixed-area vessel and incompressible liquid; overflow, cavitation and pump curves require separate safeguards. The calculation that anchors this lesson is deliberately synthetic: With tank A=4 m², h=4 m, C=0.02 m^(5/2)/s, discharge is 0.04 m³/s. The mixed concentration changes when either stream flow or concentration changes; an analyzer downstream adds measurement delay and often a slow sampling cycle. Assume perfect mixing, volume additivity and constant total outlet equal to incoming flow. Pressure and flow control with operating constraints

Pressure depends on inventory, compressibility and available inlet/outlet flow paths. Pressure loops interact with pump, compressor and valve behavior; controlling a plotted pressure value is not a substitute for relief or mechanical design. Compressibility and phase behavior determine the correct physical capacitance; the example is only a local linearized sketch. The calculation that anchors this lesson is deliberately synthetic: If effective pressure capacitance is 5 mol/kPa and net accumulation is +10 mol/min, the initial pressure rate is 2 kPa/min by the simplified inventory relation. Unit synthesis: a reproducible experiment

Start from an integrated process skid with multiple instruments and disturbances. Draw the boundary and place every relevant measurement on it. Give the operating point, consistent units and any assumptions about constant density, heat capacity or sensor behavior. Construct a hypothesis that could be tested with a small safe, synthetic input perturbation. Predict the output sign and a first approximation for the relevant time scale before computing anything.

Next compare a nominal model with one deliberately misspecified version. Change a gain, a lag, a delay or a sensor bias rather than modifying all uncertain features at once. Quantify the difference in output peak, settling time, residual steady error and manipulated-variable excursion. Record when the model ceases to be a good representation of the exercise. A successful classroom exercise is a transparent chain of assumptions and checks, not merely a curve.

Finally design a holdout test. In a process setting this might be a second flow rate, a different operating temperature or a load disturbance that was not included in model fitting. Compare model and measurement on the same time grid, keep a reproducible data table, and explain why any fitted coefficient cannot be assumed constant outside its observed range. Treat alarm and protective requirements as a separate design decision.

§8.2 Unit 8, Lesson 1: Tank-level control and nonlinear discharge

Unit 8, Lesson 1: Tank-level control and nonlinear discharge

Unit: Industrial Control Case Studies and Validation. Physical setting: an integrated process skid with multiple instruments and disturbances. Reference model: A dh/dt=q_in-C sqrt(h).

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson you should be able to (i) explain tank-level control and nonlinear discharge in physical terms, (ii) identify the variables and assumptions of A dh/dt=q_in-C sqrt(h), (iii) reproduce and challenge a numerical benchmark, (iv) recognize how measurement, delay and actuator limits modify the model, and (v) design a specific experiment that can distinguish the current model from an alternative interpretation.

Physical reasoning, not just a formula

Tank-level control must match the physical inlet and outlet roles. Large tanks may tolerate slow response, whereas small surge vessels can overflow before a delayed controller reacts; nonlinear drain flow changes local gain with level.

Consider the unit-wide equipment case: an integrated process skid with multiple instruments and disturbances. In this setting the same numerical trend may have more than one physical cause. A change observed at the analyzer may reflect a real process response, a changed flow residence time or the instrument dynamics. The analyst must first specify which quantity is manipulated, which is observed and which variables are treated as external disturbances. This is particularly important for tank-level control and nonlinear discharge, where confusing a state with a measured output can give a mathematically solvable but physically incorrect model.

The chosen governing expression is A dh/dt=q_in-C sqrt(h). It is a compact model, not a complete plant specification. Terms on opposite sides of an equality must have compatible units and consistent time bases. When a parameter is estimated from one transient, its result belongs to that operating range unless new data demonstrate transferability. The task is to explain the meaning of the relation before substituting numbers and then to compare its predictions with a checkable boundary condition or steady state.

Working the numerical reference case

Synthetic worked example. With tank A=4 m², h=4 m, C=0.02 m^(5/2)/s, discharge is 0.04 m³/s. A sudden 0.01 m³/s increase in inlet causes initial level slope 0.01/4=0.0025 m/s.

First transcribe every numerical input into a short table with its physical unit. Second, compute the baseline result without a software solver. Third, identify which output should change and which should remain unchanged when the manipulated input is zero. In this tank-level control and nonlinear discharge exercise, the reported figures are not fitted plant data: they are an internally stated teaching benchmark. Any extension to different equipment requires new coefficients and new validation.

A useful sensitivity check is to change the selected coefficient or input by +10% while retaining all other conditions. This is not equivalent to assuming the output must also rise by 10%; exponential responses, ratios, saturation and feedback can change the mapping. Write the original expression, substitute the perturbed value and compare the sign of the result against physical intuition. Next repeat with a −10% change. An asymmetric response is evidence of nonlinearity in the selected mapping, not necessarily a numerical error.

Derivation and model restrictions

Assume a fixed-area vessel and incompressible liquid; overflow, cavitation and pump curves require separate safeguards. To turn a physical inventory into a differential state equation, write accumulation as incoming rate minus outgoing rate plus net production, if relevant. To turn an instrument or actuator into a dynamical element, specify its output, time constant and initial condition. To build a transfer function, take deviations about a steady operating point and verify that the assumed initial deviations are zero. Do not apply frequency-domain algebra blindly to a strongly nonlinear or switching regime.

For tank-level control and nonlinear discharge, the limiting checks should include a zero-change input, a long-time equilibrium where one exists and at least one infeasible input that the physical equipment cannot realize. If a parameter has dimensions of reciprocal time, use it only with a time expressed in matching units. If a parameter multiplies a temperature difference, its unit must convert the difference into a heat rate before the quantity is added to the energy balance. These simple checks detect mistakes that a graph renderer will not.

How to identify the model experimentally

Use the previously studied topic Tank-level control and nonlinear discharge as a source of prior knowledge and the next topic Jacket temperature and reactor heat effects as an independent challenge. Define the reference operating point for tank-level control and nonlinear discharge in the process case and record a small safe input sequence, the actual actuator trajectory, the measurement times and instrument range. A step, a pulse and a slow ramp probe different properties, so do not collapse them into one claimed validation. If the fitted output differs from the recorded trace, inspect the timestamp alignment and sensor dynamics before increasing model order.

Where possible, obtain at least two input amplitudes. Normalize the measured output by the change in input and test whether the curves approximately collapse. Failure to collapse can indicate nonlinearity, saturation, temperature-dependent coefficients or changed initial conditions. A second holdout operating point is especially important when the main formula was derived by linearization. Report the residual error and uncertainty rather than relabeling an imperfect approximation as a universal physical law.

Uncertainty, troubleshooting, and design decisions

Failure mode to investigate: Controller saturation can make large-setpoint recovery dramatically slower than a small-disturbance test indicates. The first diagnostic question is which conservation law, measurement chain or actuator path the proposed explanation implicates. Reconstruct the actual manipulated-variable history and compare it with what the controller requested; a large difference is a plant-side constraint, not a parameter-estimation error that can be fixed by fitting a more elaborate process transfer function.

Decision from the evidence: Compare full nonlinear level response with a first-order linear model derived at h=4 m. State what the experiment supports and what remains unidentifiable. The decision should include whether the next useful action is to add a sensor, run a second input condition, change an assumed model structure or restrict the admissible operating range. When model outputs could guide actual equipment, independent engineering review and safety analysis are necessary before any implementation.

A challenge to the simplest explanation

Compare the present explanation with Blending composition and analyzer delay, which appears elsewhere in this unit. Imagine obtaining a smooth measured response that both models could plausibly produce. Choose one additional measurement that would change differently under the two explanations. Define the expected sign and time scale under each. For tank-level control and nonlinear discharge, record not just peak error but also the total actuator travel, disturbances, measurement noise and how the reference signal was generated. This exercise forces you to separate apparent goodness of fit from the ability to predict new conditions.

Numerical implementation and independent checks

The companion [interactive model, Laboratory 02](../labs/lab-02.html) is Nonlinear gravity-drained tank. Change one slider at a time, export the series and independently reproduce at least one value from a governing equation. The browser tool is deterministic and offline. It represents a defined educational model, not an approved process simulator. Its chart scale may rescale as parameters change, so compare numerical coordinates rather than visual slope alone.

For a numerical transient, verify step-size sensitivity by recomputing with half the time increment. If a predicted instability disappears merely when the integration time step shrinks, it was likely numerical rather than physical. On the other hand, an unstable physical model may diverge more quickly when more accurately integrated. Preserve initial conditions, parameter units and exact software version in the report so another student can repeat the test.

Practice and oral-examination questions

  1. Starting from A dh/dt=q_in-C sqrt(h), identify its state(s), manipulated input(s) and measured output(s). State how you would verify all dimensions.
  2. Recalculate the example after a 10% increase of the principal process parameter. What changes in the predicted steady value and in the transient, and why are these not necessarily the same?
  3. Propose a sensor or actuator failure that would make an otherwise correct tank-level control and nonlinear discharge model appear wrong. What extra logged variable would distinguish that failure?
  4. Explain how Blending composition and analyzer delay might confound the identification of this lesson’s mechanism and propose a discriminating test.
  5. State one safety or operating limit that the simplified A dh/dt=q_in-C sqrt(h) relation does not capture. How should an engineer document that omission?

Answer guide and reflection

Question 1 is assessed by correct units and by separating the actual physical inventory from its measured signal; a memorized symbolic expression without those definitions is insufficient. For Question 2, the correct method uses the original equation twice and recomputes the equilibrium or time constant where applicable; simple proportional scaling is not accepted without showing that the equation is linear in the varied coefficient. For Question 3, the measurement or actuator diagnosis must lead to a different predicted trace. For Question 4, a proposed experiment is credible only if it can distinguish two candidate mechanisms. For Question 5, a constraint must be named and its omission explicitly stated.

Takeaway for Industrial Control Case Studies and Validation. The lesson is complete only after the model can be challenged with a changed initial condition, a disturbance and a non-ideal measurement. A plotted agreement under one nominal set of synthetic conditions is the start of investigation, not its end. The next step is to test whether the assumptions retain their meaning when jacket temperature and reactor heat effects is included in the model.

§8.5 Unit 8, Lesson 4: Pressure and flow control with operating constraints

Unit 8, Lesson 4: Pressure and flow control with operating constraints

Unit: Industrial Control Case Studies and Validation. Physical setting: an integrated process skid with multiple instruments and disturbances. Reference model: C dP/dt=F_in-F_out(P,u).

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson you should be able to (i) explain pressure and flow control with operating constraints in physical terms, (ii) identify the variables and assumptions of C dP/dt=F_in-F_out(P,u), (iii) reproduce and challenge a numerical benchmark, (iv) recognize how measurement, delay and actuator limits modify the model, and (v) design a specific experiment that can distinguish the current model from an alternative interpretation.

Physical reasoning, not just a formula

Pressure depends on inventory, compressibility and available inlet/outlet flow paths. Pressure loops interact with pump, compressor and valve behavior; controlling a plotted pressure value is not a substitute for relief or mechanical design.

Consider the unit-wide equipment case: an integrated process skid with multiple instruments and disturbances. In this setting the same numerical trend may have more than one physical cause. A change observed at the analyzer may reflect a real process response, a changed flow residence time or the instrument dynamics. The analyst must first specify which quantity is manipulated, which is observed and which variables are treated as external disturbances. This is particularly important for pressure and flow control with operating constraints, where confusing a state with a measured output can give a mathematically solvable but physically incorrect model.

The chosen governing expression is C dP/dt=F_in-F_out(P,u). It is a compact model, not a complete plant specification. Terms on opposite sides of an equality must have compatible units and consistent time bases. When a parameter is estimated from one transient, its result belongs to that operating range unless new data demonstrate transferability. The task is to explain the meaning of the relation before substituting numbers and then to compare its predictions with a checkable boundary condition or steady state.

Working the numerical reference case

Synthetic worked example. If effective pressure capacitance is 5 mol/kPa and net accumulation is +10 mol/min, the initial pressure rate is 2 kPa/min by the simplified inventory relation.

First transcribe every numerical input into a short table with its physical unit. Second, compute the baseline result without a software solver. Third, identify which output should change and which should remain unchanged when the manipulated input is zero. In this pressure and flow control with operating constraints exercise, the reported figures are not fitted plant data: they are an internally stated teaching benchmark. Any extension to different equipment requires new coefficients and new validation.

A useful sensitivity check is to change the selected coefficient or input by +10% while retaining all other conditions. This is not equivalent to assuming the output must also rise by 10%; exponential responses, ratios, saturation and feedback can change the mapping. Write the original expression, substitute the perturbed value and compare the sign of the result against physical intuition. Next repeat with a −10% change. An asymmetric response is evidence of nonlinearity in the selected mapping, not necessarily a numerical error.

Derivation and model restrictions

Compressibility and phase behavior determine the correct physical capacitance; the example is only a local linearized sketch. To turn a physical inventory into a differential state equation, write accumulation as incoming rate minus outgoing rate plus net production, if relevant. To turn an instrument or actuator into a dynamical element, specify its output, time constant and initial condition. To build a transfer function, take deviations about a steady operating point and verify that the assumed initial deviations are zero. Do not apply frequency-domain algebra blindly to a strongly nonlinear or switching regime.

For pressure and flow control with operating constraints, the limiting checks should include a zero-change input, a long-time equilibrium where one exists and at least one infeasible input that the physical equipment cannot realize. If a parameter has dimensions of reciprocal time, use it only with a time expressed in matching units. If a parameter multiplies a temperature difference, its unit must convert the difference into a heat rate before the quantity is added to the energy balance. These simple checks detect mistakes that a graph renderer will not.

How to identify the model experimentally

Use the previously studied topic Blending composition and analyzer delay as a source of prior knowledge and the next topic Pressure and flow control with operating constraints as an independent challenge. Define the reference operating point for pressure and flow control with operating constraints in the process case and record a small safe input sequence, the actual actuator trajectory, the measurement times and instrument range. A step, a pulse and a slow ramp probe different properties, so do not collapse them into one claimed validation. If the fitted output differs from the recorded trace, inspect the timestamp alignment and sensor dynamics before increasing model order.

Where possible, obtain at least two input amplitudes. Normalize the measured output by the change in input and test whether the curves approximately collapse. Failure to collapse can indicate nonlinearity, saturation, temperature-dependent coefficients or changed initial conditions. A second holdout operating point is especially important when the main formula was derived by linearization. Report the residual error and uncertainty rather than relabeling an imperfect approximation as a universal physical law.

Uncertainty, troubleshooting, and design decisions

Failure mode to investigate: A valve that reaches 100% opening cannot provide further pressure relief regardless of controller command. The first diagnostic question is which conservation law, measurement chain or actuator path the proposed explanation implicates. Reconstruct the actual manipulated-variable history and compare it with what the controller requested; a large difference is a plant-side constraint, not a parameter-estimation error that can be fixed by fitting a more elaborate process transfer function.

Decision from the evidence: Write a disturbance-response acceptance test that records overshoot, settle time, manipulated-variable limits and protective-system separation. State what the experiment supports and what remains unidentifiable. The decision should include whether the next useful action is to add a sensor, run a second input condition, change an assumed model structure or restrict the admissible operating range. When model outputs could guide actual equipment, independent engineering review and safety analysis are necessary before any implementation.
