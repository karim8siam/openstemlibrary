§4.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: PID Controller Sizing and Loop Tuning

Scope: proportional offset; integral accumulation; derivative filtering; robust tuning and anti-windup. Cross-cutting case: heated-tank temperature regulation with actuator limits.

What this unit builds

Digital control does not remove continuous physics. The code operates at discrete instants while inventories, flows and heat transfer proceed continuously. Numerical integration, filtering and saturation are part of the implemented controller and may change the predicted behavior. Modeling and testing must specify the actual sampling and execution assumptions.

Lesson sequence

1. [Proportional action and steady offset](../lessons/unit-04-lesson-01.html) — Proportional control corrects error immediately but typically leaves a nonzero setpoint offset for processes with finite static gain and load. Higher gain reduces offset while increasing actuator movement and possibly reducing stability margins. Governing relation: `u=Kp(r-y)`.

2. [Integral action, bias and anti-windup](../lessons/unit-04-lesson-02.html) — Integral action accumulates sustained control error and can eliminate steady-state offset for stabilizable plants. Without anti-windup, an actuator held at its stop causes integral state to grow until recovery becomes slow or oscillatory. Governing relation: `u=Kp e+Ki int(e dt)`.

3. [Derivative action, filters, and noise](../lessons/unit-04-lesson-03.html) — Derivative action can anticipate changes in measured output but strongly amplifies high-frequency measurement noise. A filtered derivative on measurement avoids the setpoint kick from differentiating a step in reference. Governing relation: `u_D=-Kd dy_f/dt`.

4. [Model-based tuning and robustness](../lessons/unit-04-lesson-04.html) — Internal-model-control-inspired PI tuning exposes an explicit closed-loop speed target lambda. Choosing very small lambda relative to process delay and model uncertainty can demand infeasible valve movement. Governing relation: `Kc = tau/[K(lambda+theta)]`.

Model connections and physical interpretation

Proportional action and steady offset

Proportional control corrects error immediately but typically leaves a nonzero setpoint offset for processes with finite static gain and load. Higher gain reduces offset while increasing actuator movement and possibly reducing stability margins. The offset result assumes unity feedback, no bias term, a stable loop and no actuator saturation. The calculation that anchors this lesson is deliberately synthetic: For plant static gain 2 and Kp=3, unit setpoint reference yields y_ss=6/7=0.857; the remaining error is 0.143 without integral action. The reasoning should be checked in the next topic, because a dynamic approximation useful for one type of experiment may be inappropriate when the controlled input, disturbance or measurement location changes.

Integral action, bias and anti-windup

Integral action accumulates sustained control error and can eliminate steady-state offset for stabilizable plants. Without anti-windup, an actuator held at its stop causes integral state to grow until recovery becomes slow or oscillatory. A PI algorithm must specify integration units, discrete sample period, manual/auto transitions and saturation logic. The calculation that anchors this lesson is deliberately synthetic: If Ki=0.20 actuator-units/(error-unit·s) and constant e=2 persists for 15 s, the integral contribution grows by 0.20215=6 actuator units before any limit is applied. The reasoning should be checked in the next topic, because a dynamic approximation useful for one type of experiment may be inappropriate when the controlled input, disturbance or measurement location changes.

Derivative action, filters, and noise

Derivative action can anticipate changes in measured output but strongly amplifies high-frequency measurement noise. A filtered derivative on measurement avoids the setpoint kick from differentiating a step in reference. Digital differentiation is a finite-difference approximation; the derivative filter adds lag and needs a documented bandwidth. The calculation that anchors this lesson is deliberately synthetic: A sensor fluctuation of 0.10 K over 0.02 s creates an unfiltered slope estimate of 5 K/s. With Kd=3 actuator-units·s/K, the instantaneous derivative contribution can reach 15 actuator units. The reasoning should be checked in the next topic, because a dynamic approximation useful for one type of experiment may be inappropriate when the controlled input, disturbance or measurement location changes.

Model-based tuning and robustness

Internal-model-control-inspired PI tuning exposes an explicit closed-loop speed target lambda. Choosing very small lambda relative to process delay and model uncertainty can demand infeasible valve movement. This is one illustrative approximate tuning relation, not a universal optimum. Process type, objective and constraints matter. The calculation that anchors this lesson is deliberately synthetic: With K=2, tau=60 s, theta=10 s, and lambda=40 s, Kc=60/[2*(40+10)]=0.60; a common FOPDT PI rule sets Ti≈tau=60 s for this simplified case. The reasoning should be checked in the next topic, because a dynamic approximation useful for one type of experiment may be inappropriate when the controlled input, disturbance or measurement location changes.

Unit synthesis: a reproducible experiment

Start from heated-tank temperature regulation with actuator limits. Draw the boundary and place every relevant measurement on it. Give the operating point, consistent units and any assumptions about constant density, heat capacity or sensor behavior. Construct a hypothesis that could be tested with a small safe, synthetic input perturbation. Predict the output sign and a first approximation for the relevant time scale before computing anything.

Next compare a nominal model with one deliberately misspecified version. Change a gain, a lag, a delay or a sensor bias rather than modifying all uncertain features at once. Quantify the difference in output peak, settling time, residual steady error and manipulated-variable excursion. Record when the model ceases to be a good representation of the exercise. A successful classroom exercise is a transparent chain of assumptions and checks, not merely a curve.

Finally design a holdout test. In a process setting this might be a second flow rate, a different operating temperature or a load disturbance that was not included in model fitting. Compare model and measurement on the same time grid, keep a reproducible data table, and explain why any fitted coefficient cannot be assumed constant outside its observed range. Treat alarm and protective requirements as a separate design decision.

§4.2 Unit 4, Lesson 1: Proportional action and steady offset

Unit 4, Lesson 1: Proportional action and steady offset

Unit: PID Control and Tuning. Physical setting: heated-tank temperature regulation with actuator limits. Reference model: u=Kp(r-y).

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson you should be able to (i) explain proportional action and steady offset in physical terms, (ii) identify the variables and assumptions of u=Kp(r-y), (iii) reproduce and challenge a numerical benchmark, (iv) recognize how measurement, delay and actuator limits modify the model, and (v) design a specific experiment that can distinguish the current model from an alternative interpretation.

Physical reasoning, not just a formula

Proportional control corrects error immediately but typically leaves a nonzero setpoint offset for processes with finite static gain and load. Higher gain reduces offset while increasing actuator movement and possibly reducing stability margins.

Consider the unit-wide equipment case: heated-tank temperature regulation with actuator limits. In this setting the same numerical trend may have more than one physical cause. A change observed at the analyzer may reflect a real process response, a changed flow residence time or the instrument dynamics. The analyst must first specify which quantity is manipulated, which is observed and which variables are treated as external disturbances. This is particularly important for proportional action and steady offset, where confusing a state with a measured output can give a mathematically solvable but physically incorrect model.

The chosen governing expression is u=Kp(r-y). It is a compact model, not a complete plant specification. Terms on opposite sides of an equality must have compatible units and consistent time bases. When a parameter is estimated from one transient, its result belongs to that operating range unless new data demonstrate transferability. The task is to explain the meaning of the relation before substituting numbers and then to compare its predictions with a checkable boundary condition or steady state.

Working the numerical reference case

Synthetic worked example. For plant static gain 2 and Kp=3, unit setpoint reference yields y_ss=6/7=0.857; the remaining error is 0.143 without integral action.

First transcribe every numerical input into a short table with its physical unit. Second, compute the baseline result without a software solver. Third, identify which output should change and which should remain unchanged when the manipulated input is zero. In this proportional action and steady offset exercise, the reported figures are not fitted plant data: they are an internally stated teaching benchmark. Any extension to different equipment requires new coefficients and new validation.

A useful sensitivity check is to change the selected coefficient or input by +10% while retaining all other conditions. This is not equivalent to assuming the output must also rise by 10%; exponential responses, ratios, saturation and feedback can change the mapping. Write the original expression, substitute the perturbed value and compare the sign of the result against physical intuition. Next repeat with a −10% change. An asymmetric response is evidence of nonlinearity in the selected mapping, not necessarily a numerical error.

Derivation and model restrictions

The offset result assumes unity feedback, no bias term, a stable loop and no actuator saturation. To turn a physical inventory into a differential state equation, write accumulation as incoming rate minus outgoing rate plus net production, if relevant. To turn an instrument or actuator into a dynamical element, specify its output, time constant and initial condition. To build a transfer function, take deviations about a steady operating point and verify that the assumed initial deviations are zero. Do not apply frequency-domain algebra blindly to a strongly nonlinear or switching regime.

For proportional action and steady offset, the limiting checks should include a zero-change input, a long-time equilibrium where one exists and at least one infeasible input that the physical equipment cannot realize. If a parameter has dimensions of reciprocal time, use it only with a time expressed in matching units. If a parameter multiplies a temperature difference, its unit must convert the difference into a heat rate before the quantity is added to the energy balance. These simple checks detect mistakes that a graph renderer will not.

How to identify the model experimentally

Use the previously studied topic Proportional action and steady offset as a source of prior knowledge and the next topic Integral action, bias and anti-windup as an independent challenge. Define the reference operating point for proportional action and steady offset in the process case and record a small safe input sequence, the actual actuator trajectory, the measurement times and instrument range. A step, a pulse and a slow ramp probe different properties, so do not collapse them into one claimed validation. If the fitted output differs from the recorded trace, inspect the timestamp alignment and sensor dynamics before increasing model order.

Where possible, obtain at least two input amplitudes. Normalize the measured output by the change in input and test whether the curves approximately collapse. Failure to collapse can indicate nonlinearity, saturation, temperature-dependent coefficients or changed initial conditions. A second holdout operating point is especially important when the main formula was derived by linearization. Report the residual error and uncertainty rather than relabeling an imperfect approximation as a universal physical law.

Uncertainty, troubleshooting, and design decisions

Failure mode to investigate: A noisy instrument can convert high proportional gain into rapid valve cycling and shortened actuator life. The first diagnostic question is which conservation law, measurement chain or actuator path the proposed explanation implicates. Reconstruct the actual manipulated-variable history and compare it with what the controller requested; a large difference is a plant-side constraint, not a parameter-estimation error that can be fixed by fitting a more elaborate process transfer function.

Decision from the evidence: Increase gain in controlled increments, plotting response time and final offset alongside manipulated-variable excursion. State what the experiment supports and what remains unidentifiable. The decision should include whether the next useful action is to add a sensor, run a second input condition, change an assumed model structure or restrict the admissible operating range. When model outputs could guide actual equipment, independent engineering review and safety analysis are necessary before any implementation.

A challenge to the simplest explanation

Compare the present explanation with Derivative action, filters, and noise, which appears elsewhere in this unit. Imagine obtaining a smooth measured response that both models could plausibly produce. Choose one additional measurement that would change differently under the two explanations. Define the expected sign and time scale under each. For proportional action and steady offset, record not just peak error but also the total actuator travel, disturbances, measurement noise and how the reference signal was generated. This exercise forces you to separate apparent goodness of fit from the ability to predict new conditions.

Numerical implementation and independent checks

The companion [interactive model, Laboratory 06](../labs/lab-06.html) is Proportional feedback and offset. Change one slider at a time, export the series and independently reproduce at least one value from a governing equation. The browser tool is deterministic and offline. It represents a defined educational model, not an approved process simulator. Its chart scale may rescale as parameters change, so compare numerical coordinates rather than visual slope alone.

For a numerical transient, verify step-size sensitivity by recomputing with half the time increment. If a predicted instability disappears merely when the integration time step shrinks, it was likely numerical rather than physical. On the other hand, an unstable physical model may diverge more quickly when more accurately integrated. Preserve initial conditions, parameter units and exact software version in the report so another student can repeat the test.

Practice and oral-examination questions

  1. Starting from u=Kp(r-y), identify its state(s), manipulated input(s) and measured output(s). State how you would verify all dimensions.
  2. Recalculate the example after a 10% increase of the principal process parameter. What changes in the predicted steady value and in the transient, and why are these not necessarily the same?
  3. Propose a sensor or actuator failure that would make an otherwise correct proportional action and steady offset model appear wrong. What extra logged variable would distinguish that failure?
  4. Explain how Derivative action, filters, and noise might confound the identification of this lesson’s mechanism and propose a discriminating test.
  5. State one safety or operating limit that the simplified u=Kp(r-y) relation does not capture. How should an engineer document that omission?

Answer guide and reflection

Question 1 is assessed by correct units and by separating the actual physical inventory from its measured signal; a memorized symbolic expression without those definitions is insufficient. For Question 2, the correct method uses the original equation twice and recomputes the equilibrium or time constant where applicable; simple proportional scaling is not accepted without showing that the equation is linear in the varied coefficient. For Question 3, the measurement or actuator diagnosis must lead to a different predicted trace. For Question 4, a proposed experiment is credible only if it can distinguish two candidate mechanisms. For Question 5, a constraint must be named and its omission explicitly stated.

Takeaway for PID Control and Tuning. The lesson is complete only after the model can be challenged with a changed initial condition, a disturbance and a non-ideal measurement. A plotted agreement under one nominal set of synthetic conditions is the start of investigation, not its end. The next step is to test whether the assumptions retain their meaning when integral action, bias and anti-windup is included in the model.

§4.5 Unit 4, Lesson 4: Model-based tuning and robustness

Unit 4, Lesson 4: Model-based tuning and robustness

Unit: PID Control and Tuning. Physical setting: heated-tank temperature regulation with actuator limits. Reference model: Kc = tau/[K(lambda+theta)].

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson you should be able to (i) explain model-based tuning and robustness in physical terms, (ii) identify the variables and assumptions of Kc = tau/[K(lambda+theta)], (iii) reproduce and challenge a numerical benchmark, (iv) recognize how measurement, delay and actuator limits modify the model, and (v) design a specific experiment that can distinguish the current model from an alternative interpretation.

Physical reasoning, not just a formula

Internal-model-control-inspired PI tuning exposes an explicit closed-loop speed target lambda. Choosing very small lambda relative to process delay and model uncertainty can demand infeasible valve movement.

Consider the unit-wide equipment case: heated-tank temperature regulation with actuator limits. In this setting the same numerical trend may have more than one physical cause. A change observed at the analyzer may reflect a real process response, a changed flow residence time or the instrument dynamics. The analyst must first specify which quantity is manipulated, which is observed and which variables are treated as external disturbances. This is particularly important for model-based tuning and robustness, where confusing a state with a measured output can give a mathematically solvable but physically incorrect model.

The chosen governing expression is Kc = tau/[K(lambda+theta)]. It is a compact model, not a complete plant specification. Terms on opposite sides of an equality must have compatible units and consistent time bases. When a parameter is estimated from one transient, its result belongs to that operating range unless new data demonstrate transferability. The task is to explain the meaning of the relation before substituting numbers and then to compare its predictions with a checkable boundary condition or steady state.

Working the numerical reference case

Synthetic worked example. With K=2, tau=60 s, theta=10 s, and lambda=40 s, Kc=60/[2*(40+10)]=0.60; a common FOPDT PI rule sets Ti≈tau=60 s for this simplified case.

First transcribe every numerical input into a short table with its physical unit. Second, compute the baseline result without a software solver. Third, identify which output should change and which should remain unchanged when the manipulated input is zero. In this model-based tuning and robustness exercise, the reported figures are not fitted plant data: they are an internally stated teaching benchmark. Any extension to different equipment requires new coefficients and new validation.

A useful sensitivity check is to change the selected coefficient or input by +10% while retaining all other conditions. This is not equivalent to assuming the output must also rise by 10%; exponential responses, ratios, saturation and feedback can change the mapping. Write the original expression, substitute the perturbed value and compare the sign of the result against physical intuition. Next repeat with a −10% change. An asymmetric response is evidence of nonlinearity in the selected mapping, not necessarily a numerical error.

Derivation and model restrictions

This is one illustrative approximate tuning relation, not a universal optimum. Process type, objective and constraints matter. To turn a physical inventory into a differential state equation, write accumulation as incoming rate minus outgoing rate plus net production, if relevant. To turn an instrument or actuator into a dynamical element, specify its output, time constant and initial condition. To build a transfer function, take deviations about a steady operating point and verify that the assumed initial deviations are zero. Do not apply frequency-domain algebra blindly to a strongly nonlinear or switching regime.

For model-based tuning and robustness, the limiting checks should include a zero-change input, a long-time equilibrium where one exists and at least one infeasible input that the physical equipment cannot realize. If a parameter has dimensions of reciprocal time, use it only with a time expressed in matching units. If a parameter multiplies a temperature difference, its unit must convert the difference into a heat rate before the quantity is added to the energy balance. These simple checks detect mistakes that a graph renderer will not.

How to identify the model experimentally

Use the previously studied topic Derivative action, filters, and noise as a source of prior knowledge and the next topic Model-based tuning and robustness as an independent challenge. Define the reference operating point for model-based tuning and robustness in the process case and record a small safe input sequence, the actual actuator trajectory, the measurement times and instrument range. A step, a pulse and a slow ramp probe different properties, so do not collapse them into one claimed validation. If the fitted output differs from the recorded trace, inspect the timestamp alignment and sensor dynamics before increasing model order.

Where possible, obtain at least two input amplitudes. Normalize the measured output by the change in input and test whether the curves approximately collapse. Failure to collapse can indicate nonlinearity, saturation, temperature-dependent coefficients or changed initial conditions. A second holdout operating point is especially important when the main formula was derived by linearization. Report the residual error and uncertainty rather than relabeling an imperfect approximation as a universal physical law.

Uncertainty, troubleshooting, and design decisions

Failure mode to investigate: An apparently faster closed-loop design can fail when delayed measurement or valve saturation invalidates the nominal process model. The first diagnostic question is which conservation law, measurement chain or actuator path the proposed explanation implicates. Reconstruct the actual manipulated-variable history and compare it with what the controller requested; a large difference is a plant-side constraint, not a parameter-estimation error that can be fixed by fitting a more elaborate process transfer function.

Decision from the evidence: Test tuning at ±20% gain, lag and delay variation and evaluate integral absolute error and valve travel. State what the experiment supports and what remains unidentifiable. The decision should include whether the next useful action is to add a sensor, run a second input condition, change an assumed model structure or restrict the admissible operating range. When model outputs could guide actual equipment, independent engineering review and safety analysis are necessary before any implementation.

A challenge to the simplest explanation

Compare the present explanation with Integral action, bias and anti-windup, which appears elsewhere in this unit. Imagine obtaining a smooth measured response that both models could plausibly produce. Choose one additional measurement that would change differently under the two explanations. Define the expected sign and time scale under each. For model-based tuning and robustness, record not just peak error but also the total actuator travel, disturbances, measurement noise and how the reference signal was generated. This exercise forces you to separate apparent goodness of fit from the ability to predict new conditions.
