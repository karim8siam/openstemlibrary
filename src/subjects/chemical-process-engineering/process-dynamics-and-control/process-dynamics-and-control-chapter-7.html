§7.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Digital Control, Sampling, PLC/DCS and Alarm Management

Scope: sampled measurements; numerical integration; quantization/filtering; alarm management. Cross-cutting case: PLC-based temperature and level control with a digital historian.

What this unit builds

Closed-loop behavior depends on the entire measurement-to-actuation path, rather than on the process alone. A regulator can be mathematically stable and still have unacceptable control quality because the actuator moves too often, the sensor is biased, the sample interval is irregular or the achievable process output is constrained. Performance reporting therefore includes both controlled-variable error and the physical effort used to obtain it.

Lesson sequence

1. [Sampling, aliasing, and execution timing](../lessons/unit-07-lesson-01.html) — A sample interval fixes the fastest frequency that can be distinguished in ideal band-limited sampling. Real plants need anti-alias filtering, timing jitter analysis, and enough samples relative to closed-loop dynamics. Governing relation: `f_s>2 f_max`.

2. [Discrete integration and controller updates](../lessons/unit-07-lesson-02.html) — Digital PI algorithms multiply error by the actual elapsed time. Forward and backward Euler and trapezoidal updates have different numerical characteristics; an implementation should state which it uses. Governing relation: `I[k]=I[k-1]+Ki Ts e[k]`.

3. [Filtering, quantization, and data quality](../lessons/unit-07-lesson-03.html) — A digital exponential filter moderates noise but creates latency, while sensor or ADC quantization causes staircase measurements. Missing or bad-quality samples must be handled by policy, not silently treated as valid zeros. Governing relation: `y_f[k]=alpha y_f[k-1]+(1-alpha)y[k]`.

4. [Alarm hysteresis, bumpless transfer, and interlocks](../lessons/unit-07-lesson-04.html) — Alarms inform operators, while protective interlocks act under defined conditions and should be engineered independently of ordinary regulation. Hysteresis and delay can prevent chattering but cannot replace hazard review. Governing relation: `alarm_on if y>=H; alarm_off if y<=L`.

Model connections and physical interpretation

Sampling, aliasing, and execution timing

A sample interval fixes the fastest frequency that can be distinguished in ideal band-limited sampling. Real plants need anti-alias filtering, timing jitter analysis, and enough samples relative to closed-loop dynamics. Nyquist is a theoretical limit for perfect bandlimited signals; control design typically needs a much faster sample rate than the bare factor of two. The calculation that anchors this lesson is deliberately synthetic: A 0.8 Hz sinusoid sampled at 1 Hz appears as a 0.2 Hz alias in uniformly sampled data; sampling has erased the original frequency information. The reasoning should be checked in the next topic, because a dynamic approximation useful for one type of experiment may be inappropriate when the controlled input, disturbance or measurement location changes.

Filtering, quantization, and data quality

A digital exponential filter moderates noise but creates latency, while sensor or ADC quantization causes staircase measurements. Missing or bad-quality samples must be handled by policy, not silently treated as valid zeros. Filter alpha is tied to sampling interval; a fixed alpha with changed Ts changes its real time constant. The calculation that anchors this lesson is deliberately synthetic: With alpha=0.8, previous filtered value 20 and new sample 30, updated output is 0.820+0.230=22. A one-count resolution on 0–100 over 10 bits is roughly 0.0977 units. The reasoning should be checked in the next topic, because a dynamic approximation useful for one type of experiment may be inappropriate when the controlled input, disturbance or measurement location changes.

Alarms inform operators, while protective interlocks act under defined conditions and should be engineered independently of ordinary regulation. Hysteresis and delay can prevent chattering but cannot replace hazard review. Thresholds are educational examples, not recommended setpoints for any process; acceptable thresholds require site-specific risk analysis. The calculation that anchors this lesson is deliberately synthetic: An illustrative high-temperature alarm raises at 90 °C and clears at 87 °C. A trace 89→91→89→88→86 °C triggers once and clears only at 86 °C. The reasoning should be checked in the next topic, because a dynamic approximation useful for one type of experiment may be inappropriate when the controlled input, disturbance or measurement location changes.

Unit synthesis: a reproducible experiment

Start from PLC-based temperature and level control with a digital historian. Draw the boundary and place every relevant measurement on it. Give the operating point, consistent units and any assumptions about constant density, heat capacity or sensor behavior. Construct a hypothesis that could be tested with a small safe, synthetic input perturbation. Predict the output sign and a first approximation for the relevant time scale before computing anything.

Next compare a nominal model with one deliberately misspecified version. Change a gain, a lag, a delay or a sensor bias rather than modifying all uncertain features at once. Quantify the difference in output peak, settling time, residual steady error and manipulated-variable excursion. Record when the model ceases to be a good representation of the exercise. A successful classroom exercise is a transparent chain of assumptions and checks, not merely a curve.

Finally design a holdout test. In a process setting this might be a second flow rate, a different operating temperature or a load disturbance that was not included in model fitting. Compare model and measurement on the same time grid, keep a reproducible data table, and explain why any fitted coefficient cannot be assumed constant outside its observed range. Treat alarm and protective requirements as a separate design decision.

§7.2 Unit 7, Lesson 1: Sampling, aliasing, and execution timing

Unit 7, Lesson 1: Sampling, aliasing, and execution timing

Unit: Digital Controllers, Data, and Alarm Logic. Physical setting: PLC-based temperature and level control with a digital historian. Reference model: f_s>2 f_max.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson you should be able to (i) explain sampling, aliasing, and execution timing in physical terms, (ii) identify the variables and assumptions of f_s>2 f_max, (iii) reproduce and challenge a numerical benchmark, (iv) recognize how measurement, delay and actuator limits modify the model, and (v) design a specific experiment that can distinguish the current model from an alternative interpretation.

Physical reasoning, not just a formula

A sample interval fixes the fastest frequency that can be distinguished in ideal band-limited sampling. Real plants need anti-alias filtering, timing jitter analysis, and enough samples relative to closed-loop dynamics.

Consider the unit-wide equipment case: PLC-based temperature and level control with a digital historian. In this setting the same numerical trend may have more than one physical cause. A change observed at the analyzer may reflect a real process response, a changed flow residence time or the instrument dynamics. The analyst must first specify which quantity is manipulated, which is observed and which variables are treated as external disturbances. This is particularly important for sampling, aliasing, and execution timing, where confusing a state with a measured output can give a mathematically solvable but physically incorrect model.

The chosen governing expression is f_s>2 f_max. It is a compact model, not a complete plant specification. Terms on opposite sides of an equality must have compatible units and consistent time bases. When a parameter is estimated from one transient, its result belongs to that operating range unless new data demonstrate transferability. The task is to explain the meaning of the relation before substituting numbers and then to compare its predictions with a checkable boundary condition or steady state.

Working the numerical reference case

Synthetic worked example. A 0.8 Hz sinusoid sampled at 1 Hz appears as a 0.2 Hz alias in uniformly sampled data; sampling has erased the original frequency information.

First transcribe every numerical input into a short table with its physical unit. Second, compute the baseline result without a software solver. Third, identify which output should change and which should remain unchanged when the manipulated input is zero. In this sampling, aliasing, and execution timing exercise, the reported figures are not fitted plant data: they are an internally stated teaching benchmark. Any extension to different equipment requires new coefficients and new validation.

A useful sensitivity check is to change the selected coefficient or input by +10% while retaining all other conditions. This is not equivalent to assuming the output must also rise by 10%; exponential responses, ratios, saturation and feedback can change the mapping. Write the original expression, substitute the perturbed value and compare the sign of the result against physical intuition. Next repeat with a −10% change. An asymmetric response is evidence of nonlinearity in the selected mapping, not necessarily a numerical error.

Derivation and model restrictions

Nyquist is a theoretical limit for perfect bandlimited signals; control design typically needs a much faster sample rate than the bare factor of two. To turn a physical inventory into a differential state equation, write accumulation as incoming rate minus outgoing rate plus net production, if relevant. To turn an instrument or actuator into a dynamical element, specify its output, time constant and initial condition. To build a transfer function, take deviations about a steady operating point and verify that the assumed initial deviations are zero. Do not apply frequency-domain algebra blindly to a strongly nonlinear or switching regime.

For sampling, aliasing, and execution timing, the limiting checks should include a zero-change input, a long-time equilibrium where one exists and at least one infeasible input that the physical equipment cannot realize. If a parameter has dimensions of reciprocal time, use it only with a time expressed in matching units. If a parameter multiplies a temperature difference, its unit must convert the difference into a heat rate before the quantity is added to the energy balance. These simple checks detect mistakes that a graph renderer will not.

How to identify the model experimentally

Use the previously studied topic Sampling, aliasing, and execution timing as a source of prior knowledge and the next topic Discrete integration and controller updates as an independent challenge. Define the reference operating point for sampling, aliasing, and execution timing in the process case and record a small safe input sequence, the actual actuator trajectory, the measurement times and instrument range. A step, a pulse and a slow ramp probe different properties, so do not collapse them into one claimed validation. If the fitted output differs from the recorded trace, inspect the timestamp alignment and sensor dynamics before increasing model order.

Where possible, obtain at least two input amplitudes. Normalize the measured output by the change in input and test whether the curves approximately collapse. Failure to collapse can indicate nonlinearity, saturation, temperature-dependent coefficients or changed initial conditions. A second holdout operating point is especially important when the main formula was derived by linearization. Report the residual error and uncertainty rather than relabeling an imperfect approximation as a universal physical law.

Uncertainty, troubleshooting, and design decisions

Failure mode to investigate: An operator trend that aliases oscillation can falsely suggest the equipment is changing slowly. The first diagnostic question is which conservation law, measurement chain or actuator path the proposed explanation implicates. Reconstruct the actual manipulated-variable history and compare it with what the controller requested; a large difference is a plant-side constraint, not a parameter-estimation error that can be fixed by fitting a more elaborate process transfer function.

Decision from the evidence: Compare the same physical signal at 1, 5 and 20 samples per second and calculate apparent frequency. State what the experiment supports and what remains unidentifiable. The decision should include whether the next useful action is to add a sensor, run a second input condition, change an assumed model structure or restrict the admissible operating range. When model outputs could guide actual equipment, independent engineering review and safety analysis are necessary before any implementation.

A challenge to the simplest explanation

Compare the present explanation with Filtering, quantization, and data quality, which appears elsewhere in this unit. Imagine obtaining a smooth measured response that both models could plausibly produce. Choose one additional measurement that would change differently under the two explanations. Define the expected sign and time scale under each. For sampling, aliasing, and execution timing, record not just peak error but also the total actuator travel, disturbances, measurement noise and how the reference signal was generated. This exercise forces you to separate apparent goodness of fit from the ability to predict new conditions.

Numerical implementation and independent checks

The companion [interactive model, Laboratory 14](../labs/lab-14.html) is Sampled measurement and zero-order hold. Change one slider at a time, export the series and independently reproduce at least one value from a governing equation. The browser tool is deterministic and offline. It represents a defined educational model, not an approved process simulator. Its chart scale may rescale as parameters change, so compare numerical coordinates rather than visual slope alone.

For a numerical transient, verify step-size sensitivity by recomputing with half the time increment. If a predicted instability disappears merely when the integration time step shrinks, it was likely numerical rather than physical. On the other hand, an unstable physical model may diverge more quickly when more accurately integrated. Preserve initial conditions, parameter units and exact software version in the report so another student can repeat the test.

Practice and oral-examination questions

  1. Starting from f_s>2 f_max, identify its state(s), manipulated input(s) and measured output(s). State how you would verify all dimensions.
  2. Recalculate the example after a 10% increase of the principal process parameter. What changes in the predicted steady value and in the transient, and why are these not necessarily the same?
  3. Propose a sensor or actuator failure that would make an otherwise correct sampling, aliasing, and execution timing model appear wrong. What extra logged variable would distinguish that failure?
  4. Explain how Filtering, quantization, and data quality might confound the identification of this lesson’s mechanism and propose a discriminating test.
  5. State one safety or operating limit that the simplified f_s>2 f_max relation does not capture. How should an engineer document that omission?

Answer guide and reflection

Question 1 is assessed by correct units and by separating the actual physical inventory from its measured signal; a memorized symbolic expression without those definitions is insufficient. For Question 2, the correct method uses the original equation twice and recomputes the equilibrium or time constant where applicable; simple proportional scaling is not accepted without showing that the equation is linear in the varied coefficient. For Question 3, the measurement or actuator diagnosis must lead to a different predicted trace. For Question 4, a proposed experiment is credible only if it can distinguish two candidate mechanisms. For Question 5, a constraint must be named and its omission explicitly stated.

Takeaway for Digital Controllers, Data, and Alarm Logic. The lesson is complete only after the model can be challenged with a changed initial condition, a disturbance and a non-ideal measurement. A plotted agreement under one nominal set of synthetic conditions is the start of investigation, not its end. The next step is to test whether the assumptions retain their meaning when discrete integration and controller updates is included in the model.

§7.5 Unit 7, Lesson 4: Alarm hysteresis, bumpless transfer, and interlocks

Unit 7, Lesson 4: Alarm hysteresis, bumpless transfer, and interlocks

Unit: Digital Controllers, Data, and Alarm Logic. Physical setting: PLC-based temperature and level control with a digital historian. Reference model: alarm_on if y>=H; alarm_off if y<=L.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson you should be able to (i) explain alarm hysteresis, bumpless transfer, and interlocks in physical terms, (ii) identify the variables and assumptions of alarm_on if y>=H; alarm_off if y<=L, (iii) reproduce and challenge a numerical benchmark, (iv) recognize how measurement, delay and actuator limits modify the model, and (v) design a specific experiment that can distinguish the current model from an alternative interpretation.

Physical reasoning, not just a formula

Alarms inform operators, while protective interlocks act under defined conditions and should be engineered independently of ordinary regulation. Hysteresis and delay can prevent chattering but cannot replace hazard review.

Consider the unit-wide equipment case: PLC-based temperature and level control with a digital historian. In this setting the same numerical trend may have more than one physical cause. A change observed at the analyzer may reflect a real process response, a changed flow residence time or the instrument dynamics. The analyst must first specify which quantity is manipulated, which is observed and which variables are treated as external disturbances. This is particularly important for alarm hysteresis, bumpless transfer, and interlocks, where confusing a state with a measured output can give a mathematically solvable but physically incorrect model.

The chosen governing expression is alarm_on if y>=H; alarm_off if y<=L. It is a compact model, not a complete plant specification. Terms on opposite sides of an equality must have compatible units and consistent time bases. When a parameter is estimated from one transient, its result belongs to that operating range unless new data demonstrate transferability. The task is to explain the meaning of the relation before substituting numbers and then to compare its predictions with a checkable boundary condition or steady state.

Working the numerical reference case

Synthetic worked example. An illustrative high-temperature alarm raises at 90 °C and clears at 87 °C. A trace 89→91→89→88→86 °C triggers once and clears only at 86 °C.

First transcribe every numerical input into a short table with its physical unit. Second, compute the baseline result without a software solver. Third, identify which output should change and which should remain unchanged when the manipulated input is zero. In this alarm hysteresis, bumpless transfer, and interlocks exercise, the reported figures are not fitted plant data: they are an internally stated teaching benchmark. Any extension to different equipment requires new coefficients and new validation.

A useful sensitivity check is to change the selected coefficient or input by +10% while retaining all other conditions. This is not equivalent to assuming the output must also rise by 10%; exponential responses, ratios, saturation and feedback can change the mapping. Write the original expression, substitute the perturbed value and compare the sign of the result against physical intuition. Next repeat with a −10% change. An asymmetric response is evidence of nonlinearity in the selected mapping, not necessarily a numerical error.

Derivation and model restrictions

Thresholds are educational examples, not recommended setpoints for any process; acceptable thresholds require site-specific risk analysis. To turn a physical inventory into a differential state equation, write accumulation as incoming rate minus outgoing rate plus net production, if relevant. To turn an instrument or actuator into a dynamical element, specify its output, time constant and initial condition. To build a transfer function, take deviations about a steady operating point and verify that the assumed initial deviations are zero. Do not apply frequency-domain algebra blindly to a strongly nonlinear or switching regime.

For alarm hysteresis, bumpless transfer, and interlocks, the limiting checks should include a zero-change input, a long-time equilibrium where one exists and at least one infeasible input that the physical equipment cannot realize. If a parameter has dimensions of reciprocal time, use it only with a time expressed in matching units. If a parameter multiplies a temperature difference, its unit must convert the difference into a heat rate before the quantity is added to the energy balance. These simple checks detect mistakes that a graph renderer will not.

How to identify the model experimentally

Use the previously studied topic Filtering, quantization, and data quality as a source of prior knowledge and the next topic Alarm hysteresis, bumpless transfer, and interlocks as an independent challenge. Define the reference operating point for alarm hysteresis, bumpless transfer, and interlocks in the process case and record a small safe input sequence, the actual actuator trajectory, the measurement times and instrument range. A step, a pulse and a slow ramp probe different properties, so do not collapse them into one claimed validation. If the fitted output differs from the recorded trace, inspect the timestamp alignment and sensor dynamics before increasing model order.

Where possible, obtain at least two input amplitudes. Normalize the measured output by the change in input and test whether the curves approximately collapse. Failure to collapse can indicate nonlinearity, saturation, temperature-dependent coefficients or changed initial conditions. A second holdout operating point is especially important when the main formula was derived by linearization. Report the residual error and uncertainty rather than relabeling an imperfect approximation as a universal physical law.

Uncertainty, troubleshooting, and design decisions

Failure mode to investigate: Without hysteresis, noisy measurements around 90 °C can generate dozens of nuisance alarms in a few minutes. The first diagnostic question is which conservation law, measurement chain or actuator path the proposed explanation implicates. Reconstruct the actual manipulated-variable history and compare it with what the controller requested; a large difference is a plant-side constraint, not a parameter-estimation error that can be fixed by fitting a more elaborate process transfer function.

Decision from the evidence: Describe manual-to-auto transfer with matched integral state and test sensor disconnection, stuck values and restored communications. State what the experiment supports and what remains unidentifiable. The decision should include whether the next useful action is to add a sensor, run a second input condition, change an assumed model structure or restrict the admissible operating range. When model outputs could guide actual equipment, independent engineering review and safety analysis are necessary before any implementation.
