§5.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Radiation and High-Temperature Heating

The engineering problem

Thermal radiation transfers energy through electromagnetic waves. Its fourth-power temperature dependence makes it unusually important at furnace temperatures, while emissivity, geometry and participating gases limit simple blackbody calculations. Radiation is not inherently absent at low temperature; it is simply competing with other transfer paths.

Scope and learning outcomes

Work with absolute temperatures and emissivity, model gray-body exchanges, and examine thermal radiation in furnaces and ovens. Students should be able to select a governing equation, construct a measurable boundary condition, calculate a plausible output with its units, and explain a failure case before applying a correlation.

Concept map

5.1 Blackbody emission and emissivity

A real surface emits less than a blackbody when its hemispherical emissivity is below unity; a surface facing surroundings exchanges net radiation. A hot processing tray cools to an enclosed room. Compare a polished and oxidized surface using Kelvin, and explain why Celsius to the fourth power is invalid. Representative relationship: Qdot=epsilonsigmaA*(Ts^4−Tsur^4).

5.2 Two-surface gray radiation

A gray parallel-plate enclosure can be represented by radiative resistances that include both surface emissivities. Two large furnace panels exchange heat across a narrow gap. Investigate when the large-parallel-plate approximation becomes poor because of edge view factors. Representative relationship: Qdot=sigmaA(T1^4−T2^4)/(1/e1+1/e2−1).

5.3 Radiation shielding and furnaces

An intermediate low-emissivity shield partitions radiative resistance, but its benefit depends on thermal contact and geometry. A metal radiation shield is installed inside a furnace enclosure. State the ideal adiabatic-shield assumption and why gas convection can erode the calculated reduction. Representative relationship: Qdot≈sigmaA(T1^4−T2^4)/Rrad.

5.4 Radiative and convective interaction

Surface temperature is determined by simultaneous convection, radiation and conduction; fitting a single apparent h may conceal changes with temperature. A heated pipe is exposed to moving air in a production hall. Derive the sign of both contributions and examine ambient temperature and air-speed uncertainty. Representative relationship: Qdot=A[h(Ts−Tinf)+epsilonsigma(Ts^4−Tsur^4)].

A disciplined workflow for this unit

Start with a diagram of a process boundary, including fluid inlets, outlets, phase boundaries, hot and cold external surfaces and any energy storage. Record which values are measurements, which are assumed, and which are calculated. Write the conservation statement before inserting a transfer correlation. After obtaining a numerical result, test the limiting case: the heat flow should vanish with zero temperature difference in a passive linear resistance model, while doubling the wall area should increase conduction duty if all other conditions remain fixed. For nonlinear radiation a twofold temperature increase cannot be treated as a twofold radiation increase.

Report results with their uncertainty drivers. For a heated process tube, a thermocouple may measure external wall temperature but not internal wall temperature, and a flow meter may give volumetric rather than mass rate. An apparent discrepancy between measured and predicted duty may be the result of imperfect knowledge of heat capacities, uncontrolled bypass or fouling. If a correlation is used beyond the printed range, label the result an extrapolation instead of assigning spurious precision.

Unit investigation method

Use a blank process sheet with five sections: known properties, governing physics, calculation, validation, and operating decision. At minimum compare two physically plausible operating states and identify what extra measurement would most efficiently distinguish them. Do not rely on the number of digits printed by a simulation to argue that a real process is controlled to that precision.

§5.2 Unit 5, Lesson 1: Blackbody emission and emissivity

Unit 5, Lesson 1: Blackbody emission and emissivity

Unit: Radiation and high-temperature heating. Core formula: Qdot=epsilonsigmaA*(Ts^4−Tsur^4). Suggested model: [Laboratory 10 — Gray surface thermal radiation](../labs/lab-10.html).

Why this problem appears in process plants

A hot processing tray cools to an enclosed room. A real surface emits less than a blackbody when its hemispherical emissivity is below unity; a surface facing surroundings exchanges net radiation. This topic is a decision about where energy travels, which mechanism controls its rate, and what evidence is necessary to justify a calculation. The same reported heater duty can correspond to distinct wall temperatures depending on geometry, flow and material properties. A credible engineer must therefore distinguish the energy rate demanded by a process from the thermal resistance that allows the equipment to deliver that rate.

A process operator experiences this distinction as an operating constraint rather than as an equation. When product throughput increases, an unchanged heating utility can be insufficient even when the external wall stays hot. A measurement campaign should record mass flow, inlet and outlet conditions, utility conditions, local wall temperatures when available, and the operating history that may affect fouling. Without these signals, increasing utility temperature may mask the actual cause of poor performance and worsen degradation.

Physical mechanism and assumptions

A real surface emits less than a blackbody when its hemispherical emissivity is below unity; a surface facing surroundings exchanges net radiation. The equation selected here, Qdot=epsilonsigmaA*(Ts^4−Tsur^4), summarizes a limited physical model. Every term must refer to a declared boundary, an appropriate unit system, and a physically relevant value of temperature or material property. Before using the equation, identify whether the system is steady or transient, whether the fluid undergoes phase change, and whether the heat-transfer coefficient is known from a defensible correlation or simply a fitted number.

The engineer's first drawing should show an inward or outward normal for the relevant surface and a clear sign convention. Heat transport is directed down a temperature driving force in a passive system, although a controlled refrigeration cycle can move heat from a colder source to a hotter sink by supplying work. A temperature sensor located in a mixing chamber is not necessarily the right measurement of the wall temperature immediately adjacent to a process fluid. A fixed temperature boundary also differs from an imposed heat-flux boundary and gives a different mathematical problem.

Derivation and dimensional reasoning

Start from the first-law energy balance, accumulation = net incoming enthalpy + supplied heat + work, with the appropriate sign conventions. Introduce the constitutive expression Qdot=epsilonsigmaA*(Ts^4−Tsur^4) only after identifying the relevant energy-transfer mechanism. If a rate equation gives watts, confirm that conductivity is in W/(m K), surface area in m² and temperature difference in kelvin for a planar slab. The analogous exercise for a convection coefficient uses W/(m² K). For radiation, temperatures must be absolute because emission depends on the fourth power of thermodynamic temperature.

Terms that look superficially similar can require fundamentally different models. A cylindrical pipe wall does not have a constant radial area; a nonlinear radiation exchange cannot be replaced by a temperature-independent resistance without specifying a linearization point. Correlations expressed with Reynolds and Prandtl numbers need fluid properties at the proper reference temperature and geometry. In a thermally developing flow, the local Nusselt number may differ from its fully developed average. Write these distinctions directly beside the calculation rather than after the final answer.

Worked investigation: gray surface thermal radiation

We use a numerical teaching model with the following documented inputs: surface temperature = 700; Tsur = 310; epsilon = 0.8; A = 3. The primary control surface temperature is measured in K. Other parameters are Tsur [K]; epsilon [fraction]; A [m²]. Using the model equation, the predicted net outward radiation is 31.4182 kW. This is an arithmetic result for assumed properties, not a measured industrial duty or a certified equipment rating.

Increase the primary control from 700 to 772 K while holding the other inputs fixed. The same model predicts 47.0816 kW. Compare the absolute difference (15.6634 kW) with the percentage difference, and interpret the sign using the physical mechanism. Does this change chiefly alter the driving force, the thermal resistance, the material storage, or the rate at which the process moves energy? Explain why interpreting the curve without checking the other controlled inputs could be misleading.

The control range is deliberately finite. At very large or small values a simplified correlation can violate its validity conditions even if the numerical program continues to return finite numbers. For this lab, the important limitation is: Small convex diffuse gray surface facing large isothermal enclosure, view factor≈1. Decide whether the default state is inside that limitation and state what kind of additional property data, geometry measurement, or experimental coefficient would be needed to increase confidence in the estimate.

Measurement strategy and uncertainty

Compare a polished and oxidized surface using Kelvin, and explain why Celsius to the fourth power is invalid. Select one variable from the worked model that could realistically be measured on a plant floor, one that would normally be inferred, and one that is liable to drift over months of operation. Suggest how each could be independently checked. Thermal couples need installation and dynamic-response consideration, while flow meters require correct fluid properties and calibration. A sensor can be accurate at its measurement point yet irrelevant to the heat-transfer boundary being modeled.

Develop a first-order sensitivity calculation. If the output Y depends on parameter p, estimate dY/dp from two nearby runs of the simulator. If the measured parameter has uncertainty u(p), its approximate output contribution is abs(dY/dp)*u(p). This is a local linear approximation; it does not characterize poorly known geometry, phase-change instability or systematic installation bias. Keep uncertainty in the output's physical units until presenting any final percentage. Where a temperature difference is small, uncertainty can become the dominant proportion of the inferred transfer rate.

Process decision and limitation

Compare a polished and oxidized surface using Kelvin, and explain why Celsius to the fourth power is invalid. An excellent model is still a screening tool if it ignores details that control real equipment performance. A preliminary calculation may support an experiment or identify the direction of a sensitivity, but it cannot substitute for a vendor curve, verified thermophysical properties, design margins, pressure-equipment requirements or an installed sensor audit. Present the conclusion as an evidence-backed statement with a named range of validity. Avoid claiming that a temperature or duty is safe solely because one steady-state expression has a positive numerical solution.

  2. Repeat the worked experiment with the control variable reduced by 10%. Compute the new output, the percent change, and compare the result to a first-order sensitivity estimate. Explain whether a linear approximation is justified.
  3. Compare a polished and oxidized surface using Kelvin, and explain why Celsius to the fourth power is invalid. Describe the minimum sensor set and a method for recording uncertainty in the field.
  4. Construct an alternative physical model for the same plant scenario by changing one boundary condition. State which observation could distinguish the two models.
  5. Design a sensitivity chart comparing two material-property assumptions. What input dominates the uncertainty at the baseline and why?

An excellent model is still a screening tool if it ignores details that control real equipment performance. Present the conclusion as an evidence-backed statement with a named range of validity. Avoid claiming that a temperature or duty is safe solely because one steady-state expression has a positive numerical solution.

§5.7 Industrial Engineering Case Study: Worked case 5.1: Kelvin conversion in radiation

Worked case 5.1: Kelvin conversion in radiation

Process situation and calculation

A steel tray at 500°C faces an enclosure at 50°C. Absolute temperatures are 773.15 K and 323.15 K. Net radiation depends on the difference 773.15^4−323.15^4, not on 500^4−50^4. The resulting fourth-power combination is physically related to thermal emission and cannot be evaluated in Celsius.

Physical interpretation and possible error

The emissivity of a rough oxidized steel tray can differ from that of polished metal. Surface condition, wavelength and geometry are often more uncertain than the constants in a calculator.

§5.8 Industrial Engineering Case Study: Worked case 5.2: Parallel plate radiation denominator

Worked case 5.2: Parallel plate radiation denominator

Process situation and calculation

Large diffuse gray surfaces at 700 K and 350 K have emissivities 0.75 and 0.65. Their net radiative exchange per unit area is σ(700^4−350^4)/(1/0.75+1/0.65−1), approximately 6.82 kW/m². A blackbody assumption would overstate the heat exchange because it omits both surface resistances.

Worked case 5.3: Shield effectiveness

Process situation and calculation

§5.10 Industrial Engineering Case Study: Worked case 5.4: Film-temperature limits

Worked case 5.4: Film-temperature limits

Process situation and calculation

A 200°C hot pipe in 20°C air loses energy by both convection and radiation. An analyst might calculate Qconv=hA×180 K, then Qrad=εσA(Ts^4−Tsur^4) using 473.15 K and 293.15 K. If h=8 W/(m² K) and A=1 m², convection is 1.44 kW before considering radiation.

Physical interpretation and possible error

The two contributions are not strictly independent if radiation modifies nearby surface temperatures or natural convection. Iterating the wall temperature with both pathways is more realistic than declaring the convective heat rate equal to the entire thermal balance.
