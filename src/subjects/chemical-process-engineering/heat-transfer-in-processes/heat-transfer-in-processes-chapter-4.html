§4.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Convection in Process Equipment

The engineering problem

Convection couples diffusion near a wall to bulk fluid motion. The film coefficient h is not a material constant. It summarizes the temperature field for a specific flow, geometry and property set. A published correlation can give a precise number while being inapplicable to a developing, fouled or multiphase stream.

Scope and learning outcomes

Select internal, external and natural-convection models by flow regime, geometry and property range, and quantify correlation uncertainty. Students should be able to select a governing equation, construct a measurable boundary condition, calculate a plausible output with its units, and explain a failure case before applying a correlation.

Concept map

4.1 Internal forced convection

For a fully developed turbulent flow, selected Nusselt correlations link h to Reynolds and Prandtl numbers; they are not substitutes for regime verification. A cooled solvent flows through an instrumented smooth tube. Check Re, Pr, heating/cooling exponent and whether entrance lengths invalidate the correlation. Representative relationship: Nu=0.023Re^0.8Pr^n; h=Nu*k/D.

4.2 External flow and boundary layers

An external boundary layer grows along the surface; average and local heat-transfer coefficients are distinct and depend on transition. Air crosses the exterior of a drying-tray cover. Evaluate changes in plate length and flow speed while keeping the laminar applicability limit explicit. Representative relationship: NuL=0.664ReL^0.5Pr^(1/3) (laminar plate).

4.3 Natural convection

Buoyancy drives motion when density variation couples to gravity; Grashof and Rayleigh numbers organize the flow, not an assumed pump velocity. A hot holding tank cools without forced ventilation. Contrast stable vertical-wall buoyancy with an enclosed horizontal gap where stratification matters. Representative relationship: Ra=gbetaabs(dT)L^3/(nualpha).

4.4 Fins and extended surfaces

Fins increase area but their effectiveness is limited by axial temperature gradients and contact resistance; extra area is not uniformly at base temperature. An air-cooled control-panel heat sink is compared with a flat plate. Evaluate fin efficiency, base temperature and whether airflow bypass limits useful area. Representative relationship: eta_fin=tanh(mL)/(mL); m=sqrt(hP/(kAc)).

A disciplined workflow for this unit

Start with a diagram of a process boundary, including fluid inlets, outlets, phase boundaries, hot and cold external surfaces and any energy storage. Record which values are measurements, which are assumed, and which are calculated. Write the conservation statement before inserting a transfer correlation. After obtaining a numerical result, test the limiting case: the heat flow should vanish with zero temperature difference in a passive linear resistance model, while doubling the wall area should increase conduction duty if all other conditions remain fixed. For nonlinear radiation a twofold temperature increase cannot be treated as a twofold radiation increase.

Report results with their uncertainty drivers. For a heated process tube, a thermocouple may measure external wall temperature but not internal wall temperature, and a flow meter may give volumetric rather than mass rate. An apparent discrepancy between measured and predicted duty may be the result of imperfect knowledge of heat capacities, uncontrolled bypass or fouling. If a correlation is used beyond the printed range, label the result an extrapolation instead of assigning spurious precision.

Unit investigation method

Use a blank process sheet with five sections: known properties, governing physics, calculation, validation, and operating decision. At minimum compare two physically plausible operating states and identify what extra measurement would most efficiently distinguish them. Do not rely on the number of digits printed by a simulation to argue that a real process is controlled to that precision.

§4.2 Unit 4, Lesson 1: Internal forced convection

Unit 4, Lesson 1: Internal forced convection

Unit: Convection in process equipment. Core formula: Nu=0.023Re^0.8Pr^n; h=Nu*k/D. Suggested model: [Laboratory 07 — Turbulent pipe-side convection](../labs/lab-07.html).

Why this problem appears in process plants

A cooled solvent flows through an instrumented smooth tube. For a fully developed turbulent flow, selected Nusselt correlations link h to Reynolds and Prandtl numbers; they are not substitutes for regime verification. This topic is a decision about where energy travels, which mechanism controls its rate, and what evidence is necessary to justify a calculation. The same reported heater duty can correspond to distinct wall temperatures depending on geometry, flow and material properties. A credible engineer must therefore distinguish the energy rate demanded by a process from the thermal resistance that allows the equipment to deliver that rate.

A process operator experiences this distinction as an operating constraint rather than as an equation. When product throughput increases, an unchanged heating utility can be insufficient even when the external wall stays hot. A measurement campaign should record mass flow, inlet and outlet conditions, utility conditions, local wall temperatures when available, and the operating history that may affect fouling. Without these signals, increasing utility temperature may mask the actual cause of poor performance and worsen degradation.

Physical mechanism and assumptions

For a fully developed turbulent flow, selected Nusselt correlations link h to Reynolds and Prandtl numbers; they are not substitutes for regime verification. The equation selected here, Nu=0.023Re^0.8Pr^n; h=Nu*k/D, summarizes a limited physical model. Every term must refer to a declared boundary, an appropriate unit system, and a physically relevant value of temperature or material property. Before using the equation, identify whether the system is steady or transient, whether the fluid undergoes phase change, and whether the heat-transfer coefficient is known from a defensible correlation or simply a fitted number.

The engineer's first drawing should show an inward or outward normal for the relevant surface and a clear sign convention. Heat transport is directed down a temperature driving force in a passive system, although a controlled refrigeration cycle can move heat from a colder source to a hotter sink by supplying work. A temperature sensor located in a mixing chamber is not necessarily the right measurement of the wall temperature immediately adjacent to a process fluid. A fixed temperature boundary also differs from an imposed heat-flux boundary and gives a different mathematical problem.

Derivation and dimensional reasoning

Start from the first-law energy balance, accumulation = net incoming enthalpy + supplied heat + work, with the appropriate sign conventions. Introduce the constitutive expression Nu=0.023Re^0.8Pr^n; h=Nu*k/D only after identifying the relevant energy-transfer mechanism. If a rate equation gives watts, confirm that conductivity is in W/(m K), surface area in m² and temperature difference in kelvin for a planar slab. The analogous exercise for a convection coefficient uses W/(m² K). For radiation, temperatures must be absolute because emission depends on the fourth power of thermodynamic temperature.

Terms that look superficially similar can require fundamentally different models. A cylindrical pipe wall does not have a constant radial area; a nonlinear radiation exchange cannot be replaced by a temperature-independent resistance without specifying a linearization point. Correlations expressed with Reynolds and Prandtl numbers need fluid properties at the proper reference temperature and geometry. In a thermally developing flow, the local Nusselt number may differ from its fully developed average. Write these distinctions directly beside the calculation rather than after the final answer.

Worked investigation: turbulent pipe-side convection

We use a numerical teaching model with the following documented inputs: bulk velocity = 2; D = 0.04; rho = 990; mu = 0.001; k = 0.6; cp = 4100. The primary control bulk velocity is measured in m/s. Other parameters are D [m]; rho [kg/m³]; mu [Pa s]; k [W/(m K)]; cp [J/(kg K)]. Using the model equation, the predicted estimated h is 6175.22 W/(m² K). This is an arithmetic result for assumed properties, not a measured industrial duty or a certified equipment rating.

Increase the primary control from 2 to 2.48 m/s while holding the other inputs fixed. The same model predicts 7334.82 W/(m² K). Compare the absolute difference (1159.61 W/(m² K)) with the percentage difference, and interpret the sign using the physical mechanism. Does this change chiefly alter the driving force, the thermal resistance, the material storage, or the rate at which the process moves energy? Explain why interpreting the curve without checking the other controlled inputs could be misleading.

The control range is deliberately finite. At very large or small values a simplified correlation can violate its validity conditions even if the numerical program continues to return finite numbers. For this lab, the important limitation is: Dittus–Boelter for smooth, fully developed turbulent internal flow (use only Re≥10000 and Pr≈0.7–160). Decide whether the default state is inside that limitation and state what kind of additional property data, geometry measurement, or experimental coefficient would be needed to increase confidence in the estimate.

Measurement strategy and uncertainty

Check Re, Pr, heating/cooling exponent and whether entrance lengths invalidate the correlation. Select one variable from the worked model that could realistically be measured on a plant floor, one that would normally be inferred, and one that is liable to drift over months of operation. Suggest how each could be independently checked. Thermal couples need installation and dynamic-response consideration, while flow meters require correct fluid properties and calibration. A sensor can be accurate at its measurement point yet irrelevant to the heat-transfer boundary being modeled.

Develop a first-order sensitivity calculation. If the output Y depends on parameter p, estimate dY/dp from two nearby runs of the simulator. If the measured parameter has uncertainty u(p), its approximate output contribution is abs(dY/dp)*u(p). This is a local linear approximation; it does not characterize poorly known geometry, phase-change instability or systematic installation bias. Keep uncertainty in the output's physical units until presenting any final percentage. Where a temperature difference is small, uncertainty can become the dominant proportion of the inferred transfer rate.

Process decision and limitation

Check Re, Pr, heating/cooling exponent and whether entrance lengths invalidate the correlation. An excellent model is still a screening tool if it ignores details that control real equipment performance. A preliminary calculation may support an experiment or identify the direction of a sensitivity, but it cannot substitute for a vendor curve, verified thermophysical properties, design margins, pressure-equipment requirements or an installed sensor audit. Present the conclusion as an evidence-backed statement with a named range of validity. Avoid claiming that a temperature or duty is safe solely because one steady-state expression has a positive numerical solution.

Exercises

  1. Starting with Nu=0.023Re^0.8Pr^n; h=Nu*k/D, identify the independent variables and produce a dimensionally consistent derivation or explanation of each term. Give an example where using the wrong unit would change the answer by at least a factor of 100.
  2. Repeat the worked experiment with the control variable reduced by 10%. Compute the new output, the percent change, and compare the result to a first-order sensitivity estimate. Explain whether a linear approximation is justified.
  3. Check Re, Pr, heating/cooling exponent and whether entrance lengths invalidate the correlation. Describe the minimum sensor set and a method for recording uncertainty in the field.
  4. Construct an alternative physical model for the same plant scenario by changing one boundary condition. State which observation could distinguish the two models.
  5. Design a sensitivity chart comparing two material-property assumptions. What input dominates the uncertainty at the baseline and why?

If the measured parameter has uncertainty u(p), its approximate output contribution is abs(dY/dp)*u(p). This is a local linear approximation; it does not characterize poorly known geometry, phase-change instability or systematic installation bias. Keep uncertainty in the output's physical units until presenting any final percentage. Where a temperature difference is small, uncertainty can become the dominant proportion of the inferred transfer rate.

Process decision and limitation

Evaluate fin efficiency, base temperature and whether airflow bypass limits useful area. An excellent model is still a screening tool if it ignores details that control real equipment performance. A preliminary calculation may support an experiment or identify the direction of a sensitivity, but it cannot substitute for a vendor curve, verified thermophysical properties, design margins, pressure-equipment requirements or an installed sensor audit. Present the conclusion as an evidence-backed statement with a named range of validity. Avoid claiming that a temperature or duty is safe solely because one steady-state expression has a positive numerical solution.

§4.10 Industrial Engineering Case Study: Worked case 4.4: Practical fin efficiency

Worked case 4.4: Practical fin efficiency

Process situation and calculation

For a slender rectangular aluminum fin, increased length initially increases heat-transfer area but also produces a larger axial temperature drop. At high h, a much longer fin can have lower efficiency because its tip approaches ambient temperature. The effective additional area is efficiency multiplied by fin area, not geometric fin area alone.
