§2.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Steady Conduction and Thermal Resistance Networks

The engineering problem

Conduction moves thermal energy through solids and stationary fluids because microscopic fluctuations and molecular interactions redistribute energy. For constant conductivity the temperature profile is linear in a one-dimensional slab with no generation, but cylindrical geometry changes the area available for flow with radius.

Scope and learning outcomes

Analyze planar and cylindrical conduction, composite media, contact resistance, and insulation economics while defending boundary conditions. Students should be able to select a governing equation, construct a measurable boundary condition, calculate a plausible output with its units, and explain a failure case before applying a correlation.

Concept map

2.1 Planar multilayer conduction

A slab, a refractory lining, and a steel wall experience the same steady heat rate only when side losses and heat generation are negligible. A dryer wall combines steel, refractory and fibrous insulation. Estimate layer-interface temperatures and flag conditions under which the stated k changes strongly with temperature. Representative relationship: Qdot=(T_hot−T_cold)/(Σ L/(k*A)).

2.2 Cylindrical walls and critical insulation radius

Radial area increases with radius, making cylindrical resistance logarithmic; added insulation can increase loss around a very fine wire before it reduces it. An instrument trace heater is surrounded by an insulating jacket. Explain why rcrit is not a universal rule for a large pipe and why radiation changes the optimum. Representative relationship: R_cyl=ln(r2/r1)/(2pik*L); rcrit=k/h.

2.3 Contact resistance and thermal bridges

Finite roughness, interface pressure, oxides and gaps introduce interfacial resistance not captured by bulk material conductivity. A bolted heated plate shows a high temperature drop across a gasket. Identify independent tests that can separate contact resistance from an incorrect substrate k. Representative relationship: DeltaT_interface=Qdot*R_contact.

2.4 Temperature-dependent conduction

Real conductivity can vary with temperature, and heat-generating solids can have an interior temperature maximum. An electrically heated polymer block is thickened for mechanical strength. Check whether heat-generation density or surface loss dominates peak temperature. Representative relationship: q_x=−k(T)dT/dx; d2T/dx2=−qvol/k.

A disciplined workflow for this unit

Start with a diagram of a process boundary, including fluid inlets, outlets, phase boundaries, hot and cold external surfaces and any energy storage. Record which values are measurements, which are assumed, and which are calculated. Write the conservation statement before inserting a transfer correlation. After obtaining a numerical result, test the limiting case: the heat flow should vanish with zero temperature difference in a passive linear resistance model, while doubling the wall area should increase conduction duty if all other conditions remain fixed. For nonlinear radiation a twofold temperature increase cannot be treated as a twofold radiation increase.

Report results with their uncertainty drivers. For a heated process tube, a thermocouple may measure external wall temperature but not internal wall temperature, and a flow meter may give volumetric rather than mass rate. An apparent discrepancy between measured and predicted duty may be the result of imperfect knowledge of heat capacities, uncontrolled bypass or fouling. If a correlation is used beyond the printed range, label the result an extrapolation instead of assigning spurious precision.

Unit investigation method

Use a blank process sheet with five sections: known properties, governing physics, calculation, validation, and operating decision. At minimum compare two physically plausible operating states and identify what extra measurement would most efficiently distinguish them. Do not rely on the number of digits printed by a simulation to argue that a real process is controlled to that precision.

§2.2 Unit 2, Lesson 1: Planar multilayer conduction

Unit 2, Lesson 1: Planar multilayer conduction

Unit: Steady conduction and insulation. Core formula: Qdot=(T_hot−T_cold)/(Σ L/(k*A)). Suggested model: [Laboratory 01 — Single-layer wall conduction](../labs/lab-01.html).

Why this problem appears in process plants

A dryer wall combines steel, refractory and fibrous insulation. A slab, a refractory lining, and a steel wall experience the same steady heat rate only when side losses and heat generation are negligible. This topic is a decision about where energy travels, which mechanism controls its rate, and what evidence is necessary to justify a calculation. The same reported heater duty can correspond to distinct wall temperatures depending on geometry, flow and material properties. A credible engineer must therefore distinguish the energy rate demanded by a process from the thermal resistance that allows the equipment to deliver that rate.

A process operator experiences this distinction as an operating constraint rather than as an equation. When product throughput increases, an unchanged heating utility can be insufficient even when the external wall stays hot. A measurement campaign should record mass flow, inlet and outlet conditions, utility conditions, local wall temperatures when available, and the operating history that may affect fouling. Without these signals, increasing utility temperature may mask the actual cause of poor performance and worsen degradation.

Physical mechanism and assumptions

A slab, a refractory lining, and a steel wall experience the same steady heat rate only when side losses and heat generation are negligible. The equation selected here, Qdot=(T_hot−T_cold)/(Σ L/(k*A)), summarizes a limited physical model. Every term must refer to a declared boundary, an appropriate unit system, and a physically relevant value of temperature or material property. Before using the equation, identify whether the system is steady or transient, whether the fluid undergoes phase change, and whether the heat-transfer coefficient is known from a defensible correlation or simply a fitted number.

The engineer's first drawing should show an inward or outward normal for the relevant surface and a clear sign convention. Heat transport is directed down a temperature driving force in a passive system, although a controlled refrigeration cycle can move heat from a colder source to a hotter sink by supplying work. A temperature sensor located in a mixing chamber is not necessarily the right measurement of the wall temperature immediately adjacent to a process fluid. A fixed temperature boundary also differs from an imposed heat-flux boundary and gives a different mathematical problem.

Derivation and dimensional reasoning

Start from the first-law energy balance, accumulation = net incoming enthalpy + supplied heat + work, with the appropriate sign conventions. Introduce the constitutive expression Qdot=(T_hot−T_cold)/(Σ L/(k*A)) only after identifying the relevant energy-transfer mechanism. If a rate equation gives watts, confirm that conductivity is in W/(m K), surface area in m² and temperature difference in kelvin for a planar slab. The analogous exercise for a convection coefficient uses W/(m² K). For radiation, temperatures must be absolute because emission depends on the fourth power of thermodynamic temperature.

Terms that look superficially similar can require fundamentally different models. A cylindrical pipe wall does not have a constant radial area; a nonlinear radiation exchange cannot be replaced by a temperature-independent resistance without specifying a linearization point. Correlations expressed with Reynolds and Prandtl numbers need fluid properties at the proper reference temperature and geometry. In a thermally developing flow, the local Nusselt number may differ from its fully developed average. Write these distinctions directly beside the calculation rather than after the final answer.

Worked investigation: single-layer wall conduction

We use a numerical teaching model with the following documented inputs: thickness = 0.08; k = 1.2; A = 2; dT = 55. The primary control thickness is measured in m. Other parameters are k [W/(m K)]; A [m²]; dT [K]. Using the model equation, the predicted conductive heat rate is 1650 W. This is an arithmetic result for assumed properties, not a measured industrial duty or a certified equipment rating.

Increase the primary control from 0.08 to 0.1072 m while holding the other inputs fixed. The same model predicts 1231.34 W. Compare the absolute difference (-418.657 W) with the percentage difference, and interpret the sign using the physical mechanism. Does this change chiefly alter the driving force, the thermal resistance, the material storage, or the rate at which the process moves energy? Explain why interpreting the curve without checking the other controlled inputs could be misleading.

The control range is deliberately finite. At very large or small values a simplified correlation can violate its validity conditions even if the numerical program continues to return finite numbers. For this lab, the important limitation is: Constant conductivity, 1D steady slab, fixed surface temperatures. Decide whether the default state is inside that limitation and state what kind of additional property data, geometry measurement, or experimental coefficient would be needed to increase confidence in the estimate.

Measurement strategy and uncertainty

Estimate layer-interface temperatures and flag conditions under which the stated k changes strongly with temperature. Select one variable from the worked model that could realistically be measured on a plant floor, one that would normally be inferred, and one that is liable to drift over months of operation. Suggest how each could be independently checked. Thermal couples need installation and dynamic-response consideration, while flow meters require correct fluid properties and calibration. A sensor can be accurate at its measurement point yet irrelevant to the heat-transfer boundary being modeled.

Develop a first-order sensitivity calculation. If the output Y depends on parameter p, estimate dY/dp from two nearby runs of the simulator. If the measured parameter has uncertainty u(p), its approximate output contribution is abs(dY/dp)*u(p). This is a local linear approximation; it does not characterize poorly known geometry, phase-change instability or systematic installation bias. Keep uncertainty in the output's physical units until presenting any final percentage. Where a temperature difference is small, uncertainty can become the dominant proportion of the inferred transfer rate.

Process decision and limitation

Estimate layer-interface temperatures and flag conditions under which the stated k changes strongly with temperature. An excellent model is still a screening tool if it ignores details that control real equipment performance. A preliminary calculation may support an experiment or identify the direction of a sensitivity, but it cannot substitute for a vendor curve, verified thermophysical properties, design margins, pressure-equipment requirements or an installed sensor audit. Present the conclusion as an evidence-backed statement with a named range of validity. Avoid claiming that a temperature or duty is safe solely because one steady-state expression has a positive numerical solution.

Exercises

  1. Starting with Qdot=(T_hot−T_cold)/(Σ L/(k*A)), identify the independent variables and produce a dimensionally consistent derivation or explanation of each term. Give an example where using the wrong unit would change the answer by at least a factor of 100.
  2. Repeat the worked experiment with the control variable reduced by 10%. Compute the new output, the percent change, and compare the result to a first-order sensitivity estimate. Explain whether a linear approximation is justified.
  3. Estimate layer-interface temperatures and flag conditions under which the stated k changes strongly with temperature. Describe the minimum sensor set and a method for recording uncertainty in the field.
  4. Construct an alternative physical model for the same plant scenario by changing one boundary condition. State which observation could distinguish the two models.
  5. Design a sensitivity chart comparing two material-property assumptions. What input dominates the uncertainty at the baseline and why?
