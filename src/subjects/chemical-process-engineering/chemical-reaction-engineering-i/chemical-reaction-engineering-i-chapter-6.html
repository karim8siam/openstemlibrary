§6.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Non-Isothermal Reactor Thermal Stability

Scope: Adiabatic operation, heat removal, ignition, stability.

Competencies

By the end of this unit, a reader should translate an operating observation into a defensible model, solve a numerical case using consistent units, identify the most influential assumption, and explain what evidence would invalidate the prediction. All calculations are teaching examples unless separately identified as standards-compliant design.

Applied design studio

Document assumed heat capacity, activation energy, jacket heat transfer, and numerical step size. Identify missing decomposition/relief data and give an explicit no-use restriction for real process safety decisions.

Measurement and reporting quality

A credible laboratory report separates raw data, corrected data, numerical predictions and observations. Preserve the original concentration basis and initial conditions. Label all time axes and distinguish a measured temperature from an assumed constant. Provide an uncertainty interval or at least a sensitivity range for the parameter controlling the decision. For safe desk exercises synthetic data are acceptable if labeled as such. Avoid suggesting unsupervised hazardous experiments or relying on classroom calculations for relief sizing.

§6.2 Unit 6, Lesson 1 — Heat-of-reaction coupling

Unit 6, Lesson 1 — Heat-of-reaction coupling

Scope: Thermal behavior. Prerequisites: chemical material balances, algebra, introductory calculus and unit conversions.

Physical model and meaning

The temperature rise is governed by heat release per mole and mixture heat capacity, with heat losses treated separately. In the present topic, heat-of-reaction coupling is an engineering interpretation problem before it is an algebra problem. Write the species, the observable state, the system boundary, and the governing time or length scale. The underlying quantity should be measured or inferred on an explicit basis. A change in what is held constant—reactor volume, inlet molar flow, heat capacity or concentration basis—can change the correct equation even when the equipment looks similar.

Use the working relationship ΔT_ad ≈ (-ΔH_r) C_{A0}X/(ρ C_p). First identify the independent variable and the dependent response. State whether it represents a local rate, an integrated outcome, or a steady-state closure condition. Perform a dimensional check before substituting numbers. For a measured plant variable, distinguish an instrument reading from a property calculated by a constitutive expression. In particular, a model may be exact inside an idealization yet wrong when its boundary conditions fail.

Process context and a falsifiable claim

Calculate an upper-bound adiabatic rise for a hypothetical liquid reaction. Check how heat capacity and incomplete conversion reduce or increase risk. A falsifiable statement predicts how the measured response changes under a controlled, safe perturbation. Select one operating variable and hold the competing causes approximately constant. Write down what should happen if the equation is valid, what opposite observation would reject the assumption, and what additional sensor or sampling measurement can separate the explanations. In kinetic work, a mismatch between prediction and measurement may reflect a bad rate law, nonuniform temperature, nonideal flow, unrecognized side reaction, or a sampling artefact. Check those mechanisms independently rather than adjusting one fitted parameter until the chart looks plausible.

The practical question is not simply whether a numerical answer can be calculated. It is whether the proposed intervention changes the relevant physical mechanism and whether the predicted magnitude is large relative to instrument uncertainty and normal process variation. A temperature variation, for example, may alter both kinetics and properties. A flow variation may change residence time, heat removal, and mixing simultaneously. A careful experiment identifies these confounders.

Worked engineering example

Given. A liquid contains 1.5 mol L⁻¹ A; exothermic reaction enthalpy is −70 kJ mol⁻¹. Let ρCp=3.5 kJ L⁻¹ K⁻¹ and X=0.80.

Solution. Ideal adiabatic temperature rise is 70×1.5×0.8/3.5 =24 K. Full conversion would instead give a screening upper bound of 30 K.

Interpretation. This numerical example is valid only within its stated assumptions and unit system. Carry at least two plausible significant digits through intermediate steps, then round to the resolution of the input evidence. If experimental observations differ, report the difference before modifying the calculation. Check that all flows are on comparable molar bases and that any time units in a rate constant are consistent with the residence or observation time used.

Four checks before accepting the result

1. Heat-release basis. Identify exactly which measurement or modeling choice controls this feature. Explain its physical role, record the correct units, and consider whether an independent observation could confirm it. A single agreement at one operating point does not demonstrate that the feature is represented correctly across the entire range.

2. Effective heat capacity. Ask what would change if this effect were twice as important as assumed. A meaningful sensitivity calculation changes one parameter while keeping the process basis fixed, then reports both the numerical response and any violated constraint. Include the consequence for experiment design.

3. Remaining reaction inventory. Separate information actually measured from information embedded in a model. A fitted coefficient may compensate for an omitted physical mechanism. Check whether different candidate models predict measurably different responses over the available operating range.

4. Thermal insulation assumptions. Revisit the design decision after accounting for uncertainty. Where a limit is approached, an apparently precise result must not justify operating beyond validated data. Keep a record of the parameter set and the approximation used to reach each conclusion.

Limits and engineering judgement

A heat of reaction should be based on the same stoichiometric reaction basis as conversion. When this limitation is significant, do not conceal it in a footnote: label the specific prediction as a screening estimate and state what would be required for a validated design. A reactor choice depends on chemistry, phase behavior, heat exchange, material compatibility, sensor reliability and operator response. No single idealized expression can substitute for thermal-hazard testing, validated kinetic data or codes applicable to the actual facility.

Extension for the reader. Construct a two-case comparison: one case follows the stated ideal assumptions; the other changes the most credible violated assumption. Sketch the expected shape of the dependent variable versus the relevant input. If the shape is qualitatively different, propose an experiment that will tell the models apart. Document what new evidence would make you reverse the original recommendation.

§6.6 Process Simulation & Industrial Design Case: Non-Isothermal Reactor Thermal Stability

Unit 6 project — Cooling-loss operability scenario

Decision brief

Develop a coupled nonisothermal CSTR model and simulate a coolant-temperature step. Document assumed heat capacity, activation energy, jacket heat transfer, and numerical step size. Identify missing decomposition/relief data and give an explicit no-use restriction for real process safety decisions.

Inputs and provenance

Use clearly labeled synthetic data or independently verified measurements. For kinetic data, provide temperature and concentration units and a method for estimating variability. For operating studies, identify volumetric flow reference states and a clearly bounded control volume. Each property value needs its source, temperature and composition. Do not use reaction safety screening as a substitute for a required hazard study.

Deliverables

  1. A process flow schematic with each boundary, measured variable and manipulated control.
  2. A transparent mathematical model: species balances, an energy balance where relevant, parameters and numerical solver tolerances.
  3. At least two physically feasible alternatives evaluated on identical input and output bases.
  4. Four numerical checks: conservation, dimensional consistency, a limiting case, and sensitivity to the most uncertain parameter.
  5. A testable prediction about operation under a realistic perturbation, with a criterion that would reject the model.
  6. A limitations register identifying missing kinetics, transport, physical-property and safeguards evidence.

Review questions

Explain why the selected architecture is robust to its dominant uncertainty. Identify an apparently attractive but misleading performance metric and calculate the consequence of improving it at the expense of another. Explain how a tracer experiment, thermal test, feed assay or controlled startup could resolve a key uncertainty. Finally, state why the desk-study result must not authorize equipment operation or process-safety decisions.

Suggested evaluation rubric

Mass/energy accounting 25%; model assumptions and evidence 20%; correct calculations 20%; exploration of alternatives 15%; uncertainty and limitations 15%; readable reports and reproducibility 5%.
