§4.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Chemical Reaction System Simulation

Scope: Select ideal reactor representations, solve material balances and test the consequences of residence time and rate laws.

End-to-end teaching case: A nonhazardous hypothetical liquid-phase irreversible A-to-B reaction using illustrative rate constants.

Engineering question driving this unit

Imagine that the simulated plant must answer a design question about reaction-system simulation. The answer should not be a screenshot of a flowsheet. It should be a transparent chain: defined physical boundary → fixed and calculated quantities → constitutive laws → numerical method → independent check → decision and evidence limitations. A nonhazardous hypothetical liquid-phase irreversible A-to-B reaction using illustrative rate constants. Your output should clearly distinguish an imposed operating condition from a calculated stream result.

Four-lesson learning sequence

1. [Reaction stoichiometry and kinetic bases](../lessons/unit-04-lesson-01.html) — Governing model: `n_i = n_i,0 + nu_i xi`. Lab: [Reaction extent and limiting-reagent inventory](../labs/lab-03.html).

2. [Continuous stirred-tank reactor conversion](../lessons/unit-04-lesson-02.html) — Governing model: `X_CSTR = k tau/(1+k tau), tau=V/F`. Lab: [Isothermal first-order CSTR conversion](../labs/lab-10.html).

3. [Plug-flow residence-time calculation](../lessons/unit-04-lesson-03.html) — Governing model: `X_PFR = 1−exp(−k tau)`. Lab: [Plug flow and CSTR conversion comparison](../labs/lab-11.html).

4. [Batch trajectories, time steps and model checks](../lessons/unit-04-lesson-04.html) — Governing model: `dC_A/dt = −k C_A; C_A(t)=C_A0 exp(−kt)`. Lab: [Batch reactor exact temporal conversion](../labs/lab-12.html).

Working with the central flowsheet

The main scenario is not a plant drawing with arbitrary numbers copied from an operating facility. It is the explicitly synthetic instructional case: A nonhazardous hypothetical liquid-phase irreversible A-to-B reaction using illustrative rate constants. Begin by constructing a stream register in which each row contains a stream identifier, the material phase, independent composition variables, total-flow units, temperature, pressure, source block and destination block. For every unit operation attach a balance that can be evaluated independently of the flowsheet iteration. Never assume that graphical arrows prove mass or energy conservation.

A reproducible simulation study also requires a model-boundary register. Label one assumption as a conservation law, one as a constitutive correlation and one as a numerical choice. Conservation laws remain valid as mathematical accounting, subject to correct species and energy definitions. Constitutive correlations need a range of physical applicability. A numerical choice, such as a time step or root tolerance, can change the error without changing the model physics. If a prediction is sensitive to a correlation or solver choice, that sensitivity belongs in the reported result.

Quantities that must be explicit

Use an absolute reference state when an energy balance depends on enthalpy. Identify whether flow is molar, mass-based, or volumetric before using a rate expression. Distinguish local stream variables from plant-wide targets. A measured product purity and a computed purity are not interchangeable evidence. In a unit involving two phases, write the overall composition and each phase composition separately; an overall component balance remains the same even when the phase states change.

Record property assumptions, numerical solver settings and the sign convention for heat and work. For a negative heater duty, the arithmetic may be correct while the configured utility is wrong. For an infeasible flash, the absence of a two-phase root is physically informative; the application must not draw a plausible curve by extrapolating outside a vetted coefficient interval.

Suggested independent audit

Complete an algebraic limit test, a numerical benchmark and a deliberately adverse case. For the limit test, select a zero-flow, zero-duty, zero-conversion or unity-equilibrium case appropriate to the model. For the benchmark, reproduce one displayed result by hand or a separate computation and write down the governing equation and units. For the adverse case, use a nonconvergent recycle, an out-of-range property condition, a negative inventory attempt or a conflicting specification to verify that the model warns rather than silently changes assumptions.

Connection to the interactive laboratory

Every interactive model is a simplified teaching kernel and is accompanied by its own equation, parameter ranges, limitation statement and analysis prompt. For this unit, begin with [Laboratory 03: Reaction extent and limiting-reagent inventory](../labs/lab-03.html) and check the labels before moving a slider. A plotted trend is a hypothesis to explain from the model, not measured evidence from an operating plant.

Unit mastery criteria

You can articulate why the selected model is mathematically closed, reproduce one calculated output, expose a parameter whose value matters to the result, describe the main omitted physical effect, and recommend what data or alternative model would be needed to use the estimate for a real design. Show the residual and a unit-bearing numerical result rather than only a completion mark.

§4.2 Unit 4, Lesson 1: Reaction stoichiometry and kinetic bases

Unit 4, Lesson 1: Reaction stoichiometry and kinetic bases

Part of: [Reaction-System Simulation](../units/unit-04.html). Related interactive laboratory: [Lab 03, Reaction extent and limiting-reagent inventory](../labs/lab-03.html). Lesson classification: synthetic teaching example, not a calibrated plant model.

What you will be able to demonstrate

After studying this lesson you should be able to define the quantities needed for reaction stoichiometry and kinetic bases, justify the selected model, work the reference arithmetic with consistent units, distinguish a numerical convergence problem from a physical-model error, and communicate a specific limitation. Your final calculation should identify both the output and the evidence required before it could inform a plant decision. Use the linked laboratory to demonstrate a nontrivial sensitivity, not merely to reproduce its default plot.

Physical interpretation and model boundary

A reaction model begins with stoichiometric coefficients and a rate basis. A conversion fraction is defined relative to a chosen limiting reactant feed, while extent of reaction measures the common chemical progress variable. Use coefficients with their signs; reactants have negative stoichiometric entries in the general vector relation. A correct rate expression must match concentration or activity units and a specified volume or catalyst mass basis.

The process example is deliberately constrained to a nonhazardous hypothetical liquid-phase irreversible a-to-b reaction using illustrative rate constants. In a modeling report, the relevant boundary must include any place where mass, energy or momentum can enter or leave, and the time basis must match every rate. Write separate entries for imposed specifications and unknown results; otherwise a flowsheet editor may overconstrain the model and resolve conflicting values in an unexpected way. This distinction is central to reaction stoichiometry and kinetic bases because several different assumptions can yield similar plotted curves while representing different physical devices.

Governing relation: n_i = n_i,0 + nu_i xi. This compact relation is useful only with a defined variable list. Describe which quantities are known, which are solved, and how the physical units cancel. If a value supplied to the relationship is a concentration, specify whether it is molar, mass-based or dimensionless and do not use a rate parameter calibrated on a different basis. The governing relation is a simplifying model; the lesson checks whether the simplification is visible and auditable.

Worked reference exercise

Given and claimed result (synthetic inputs): For A+2B→C with feeds n_A=3 kmol/h and n_B=4 kmol/h, maximum extent is 2 kmol/h, leaving A=1 and B=0.

Start the calculation by writing down the input values, their dimensions, and the full equation. Establish a consistent unit system before computing a result: use seconds for flows quoted per second, hours for rate coefficients quoted per hour, Kelvin for any absolute-temperature dependence, and concentration fractions only when the selected equation expects fractions. If a temperature rise appears in a sensible-heat expression, the increment is numerically the same in K and °C, but an absolute-temperature exponent requires Kelvin.

Substitute the values into the symbolic expression before rounding intermediate results. Then perform a deliberately independent calculation: for a linear balance, reconstruct the corresponding inflow and outflow totals; for a phase equilibrium, reconstruct the sum of liquid and vapor component fractions; for a reactor, evaluate the zero-time or zero-rate limit; for a numerical solver, compare with an analytic case. Use more digits in intermediate values than in the reported engineering result. A residual smaller than machine rounding is reassuring, but it does not validate an incorrect underlying assumption.

Next change one independent input and predict the direction of the response before looking at the updated model output. This step matters because a numerical graph may look smooth even when a sign convention has been reversed. Record the parameter change, the new output, the difference relative to baseline, and whether a conservation limit or an operating constraint becomes active. The result should be evaluated as a comparison within the stated model, not as a prediction of unseen equipment.

A reproducible simulation workflow

1 — Define the task. For reaction stoichiometry and kinetic bases, describe the required output and the acceptable numerical or physical error. Decide whether the result is a state, a rate, a duty, a composition or an economic metric. An error in this classification propagates into the entire flowsheet.

2 — Enumerate inputs. Use the lesson equation n_i = n_i,0 + nu_i xi to identify independent conditions. Place the measured or stipulated inputs in one column and solver-derived values in another. Inspect whether any input is absent, contradictory, extrapolated or implicitly supplied by an inaccessible property library.

3 — Build the minimum physical model. Start with the simplest conservation or equilibrium statement that describes the scenario. Before adding extra unit operations, establish a hand benchmark from the declared reference exercise. If the benchmark cannot be repeated, reduce the model instead of adding more parameters.

4 — Solve with diagnostics. Select direct substitution for explicit formulas, a bracketed root finder for a single well-behaved nonlinear root, and an integration method appropriate to an initial-value problem. Record the termination condition. Reject nonfinite values, negative physical inventories or phase classifications that violate the declared domains. A converged calculation can still be physically invalid.

5 — Challenge the answer. Compute feasible extent at three feed ratios; explain how the limiting component changes and which product yield definition remains comparable. Change the model coefficients within the stated range and repeat the conservation test. Distinguish sensitivity caused by engineering physics from sensitivity caused by a numerical tolerance, plotting interval or initial guess.

Numerical and physical verification

Model-specific benchmark: All component flows must remain nonnegative and n_A+ξ=n_A0, n_B+2ξ=n_B0.

Most important restriction: A reaction can be kinetic-rate-limited, equilibrium-limited or heat-transfer-limited; the stoichiometric maximum alone does not establish achievable conversion.

A useful validation hierarchy has several levels. First, dimensionally balance the equation and confirm that signed terms can be interpreted physically. Second, test one limiting value such as no flow, unity separation selectivity, zero temperature difference or negligible reaction time. Third, run the model at two different numerical resolutions and check convergence of the answer rather than the smoothness of the drawn line. Fourth, compare against an independent measured data set—but only if such data have actually been provided and their measurement uncertainty is understood.

For nonlinear or highly coupled problems, a small residual may coexist with a wrong root. Check phase validity, conservation and operating boundaries. For a strongly temperature-sensitive equilibrium, bracketing and property-correlation validity are separate tests. For a recycle loop, compare the new tear stream with the previous guess and compute physical flow residuals across the complete loop. For an optimization result, verify both the objective and the physical feasibility of the candidate solution.

Where this model stops being defensible

A reaction can be kinetic-rate-limited, equilibrium-limited or heat-transfer-limited; the stoichiometric maximum alone does not establish achievable conversion. Record this exclusion prominently if the model is exported, reused or embedded in another page. If the omitted effect is expected to be large, replace the current model with a more detailed formulation and obtain appropriate parameter data. Adding decimal places cannot repair missing physics. Likewise, a chart generated using synthetic parameters must never be described as a measurement or a fitted empirical trend.

The operational interpretation is: Test nonnegative species inventories throughout the modeled extent and confirm atom and component-basis accounting before fitting kinetics. Before making a final decision, identify a practical observation capable of distinguishing this explanation from an alternative. Such observations might include simultaneous inlet and outlet compositions, a pressure profile, a verified utility duty, a time-dependent level record or temperature-dependent pure-component data. Choose observations appropriate to the specific equipment, and document the measurement range and data quality.

Open [the interactive Reaction extent and limiting-reagent inventory model](../labs/lab-03.html). Its sliders expose the primary assumptions; its output is computed locally in the browser, with no network dependency. Read the displayed equation and variable units before changing parameters. Export CSV to preserve numerical values for independent checking. The primary task is: Compute feasible extent at three feed ratios; explain how the limiting component changes and which product yield definition remains comparable.

The laboratory also states: Single reaction, no side reactions or phases, 100% desired stoichiometry. Investigate a plausible operating setting and a limiting setting. Identify the predicted trend, the mechanism producing it, and one reason why the same trend may fail in a real plant. Do not call a laboratory “validated” merely because the curve responds to input changes; validation needs independent comparison and a defined intended use.

What to include in your solution notebook

Your notebook entry should contain a one-line physical question, a labeled sketch or stream inventory, the model equation, a variable-and-unit table, the reference case calculation, one sensitivity experiment, a balance or limiting-case residual and a transparent limitation. Finish with a claim that is no stronger than the evidence. Physical interpretation and model boundary

For a perfectly mixed isothermal reactor at steady state, outlet concentration equals the tank concentration. Substituting a first-order rate into the component balance gives a rational conversion formula. At finite residence time a CSTR retains reactant because the whole vessel operates at the exit concentration. A single ideal CSTR therefore usually achieves less conversion than an ideal plug-flow device of equal mean residence time for simple positive-order kinetics.

§4.4 Unit 4, Lesson 3: Plug-flow residence-time calculation

Unit 4, Lesson 3: Plug-flow residence-time calculation

Part of: [Reaction-System Simulation](../units/unit-04.html). Related interactive laboratory: [Lab 11, Plug flow and CSTR conversion comparison](../labs/lab-11.html). Lesson classification: synthetic teaching example, not a calibrated plant model.

Physical interpretation and model boundary

An ideal plug-flow reactor has no axial mixing, so each fluid element progresses through the reaction with its travel time. Under constant density, first-order kinetics and constant temperature, the axial differential balance integrates to an exponential form. This simplification enables a transparent comparison of reactor types but hides axial dispersion, radial temperature variation and pressure drop. A lab graph plotting conversion versus residence time shows when increasing volume has diminishing returns.

§4.5 Unit 4, Lesson 4: Batch trajectories, time steps and model checks

Unit 4, Lesson 4: Batch trajectories, time steps and model checks

Part of: [Reaction-System Simulation](../units/unit-04.html). Related interactive laboratory: [Lab 12, Batch reactor exact temporal conversion](../labs/lab-12.html). Lesson classification: synthetic teaching example, not a calibrated plant model.

What you will be able to demonstrate

After studying this lesson you should be able to define the quantities needed for batch trajectories, time steps and model checks, justify the selected model, work the reference arithmetic with consistent units, distinguish a numerical convergence problem from a physical-model error, and communicate a specific limitation. Your final calculation should identify both the output and the evidence required before it could inform a plant decision. Use the linked laboratory to demonstrate a nontrivial sensitivity, not merely to reproduce its default plot.

Physical interpretation and model boundary

A batch reactor follows a temporal initial-value problem rather than a once-through steady-state flow balance. The initial concentration is part of the state definition and cannot be filled in by a downstream flowsheet automatically. The exponential first-order reference solution permits a strong numerical verification test: compute the time trajectory using a numerical integrator and compare it with the exact expression at the same timestamps.

The process example is deliberately constrained to a nonhazardous hypothetical liquid-phase irreversible a-to-b reaction using illustrative rate constants. In a modeling report, the relevant boundary must include any place where mass, energy or momentum can enter or leave, and the time basis must match every rate. Write separate entries for imposed specifications and unknown results; otherwise a flowsheet editor may overconstrain the model and resolve conflicting values in an unexpected way. This distinction is central to batch trajectories, time steps and model checks because several different assumptions can yield similar plotted curves while representing different physical devices.

Governing relation: dC_A/dt = −k C_A; C_A(t)=C_A0 exp(−kt). This compact relation is useful only with a defined variable list. Describe which quantities are known, which are solved, and how the physical units cancel. If a value supplied to the relationship is a concentration, specify whether it is molar, mass-based or dimensionless and do not use a rate parameter calibrated on a different basis. The governing relation is a simplifying model; the lesson checks whether the simplification is visible and auditable.

Worked reference exercise

Given and claimed result (synthetic inputs): At k=0.1 min^-1 and t=10 min, unreacted fraction is exp(−1)≈0.3679.

Start the calculation by writing down the input values, their dimensions, and the full equation. Establish a consistent unit system before computing a result: use seconds for flows quoted per second, hours for rate coefficients quoted per hour, Kelvin for any absolute-temperature dependence, and concentration fractions only when the selected equation expects fractions. If a temperature rise appears in a sensible-heat expression, the increment is numerically the same in K and °C, but an absolute-temperature exponent requires Kelvin.

Substitute the values into the symbolic expression before rounding intermediate results. Then perform a deliberately independent calculation: for a linear balance, reconstruct the corresponding inflow and outflow totals; for a phase equilibrium, reconstruct the sum of liquid and vapor component fractions; for a reactor, evaluate the zero-time or zero-rate limit; for a numerical solver, compare with an analytic case. Use more digits in intermediate values than in the reported engineering result. A residual smaller than machine rounding is reassuring, but it does not validate an incorrect underlying assumption.

Next change one independent input and predict the direction of the response before looking at the updated model output. This step matters because a numerical graph may look smooth even when a sign convention has been reversed. Record the parameter change, the new output, the difference relative to baseline, and whether a conservation limit or an operating constraint becomes active. The result should be evaluated as a comparison within the stated model, not as a prediction of unseen equipment.

A reproducible simulation workflow

1 — Define the task. For batch trajectories, time steps and model checks, describe the required output and the acceptable numerical or physical error. Decide whether the result is a state, a rate, a duty, a composition or an economic metric. An error in this classification propagates into the entire flowsheet.

2 — Enumerate inputs. Use the lesson equation dC_A/dt = −k C_A; C_A(t)=C_A0 exp(−kt) to identify independent conditions. Place the measured or stipulated inputs in one column and solver-derived values in another. Inspect whether any input is absent, contradictory, extrapolated or implicitly supplied by an inaccessible property library.

3 — Build the minimum physical model. Start with the simplest conservation or equilibrium statement that describes the scenario. Before adding extra unit operations, establish a hand benchmark from the declared reference exercise. If the benchmark cannot be repeated, reduce the model instead of adding more parameters.

4 — Solve with diagnostics. Select direct substitution for explicit formulas, a bracketed root finder for a single well-behaved nonlinear root, and an integration method appropriate to an initial-value problem. Record the termination condition. Reject nonfinite values, negative physical inventories or phase classifications that violate the declared domains. A converged calculation can still be physically invalid.

5 — Challenge the answer. Repeat the trajectory with half the integration step and report the maximum difference from the analytic reference. Change the model coefficients within the stated range and repeat the conservation test. Distinguish sensitivity caused by engineering physics from sensitivity caused by a numerical tolerance, plotting interval or initial guess.

Numerical and physical verification

Model-specific benchmark: X(0)=0, conversion never exceeds one and remains nondecreasing.

Most important restriction: If reaction rate depends on temperature or species activities, the exact exponential check no longer describes the physical model.

A useful validation hierarchy has several levels. First, dimensionally balance the equation and confirm that signed terms can be interpreted physically. Second, test one limiting value such as no flow, unity separation selectivity, zero temperature difference or negligible reaction time. Third, run the model at two different numerical resolutions and check convergence of the answer rather than the smoothness of the drawn line. Fourth, compare against an independent measured data set—but only if such data have actually been provided and their measurement uncertainty is understood.

For nonlinear or highly coupled problems, a small residual may coexist with a wrong root. Check phase validity, conservation and operating boundaries. For a strongly temperature-sensitive equilibrium, bracketing and property-correlation validity are separate tests. For a recycle loop, compare the new tear stream with the previous guess and compute physical flow residuals across the complete loop. For an optimization result, verify both the objective and the physical feasibility of the candidate solution.

Where this model stops being defensible

If reaction rate depends on temperature or species activities, the exact exponential check no longer describes the physical model. Record this exclusion prominently if the model is exported, reused or embedded in another page. If the omitted effect is expected to be large, replace the current model with a more detailed formulation and obtain appropriate parameter data. Adding decimal places cannot repair missing physics. Likewise, a chart generated using synthetic parameters must never be described as a measurement or a fitted empirical trend.

The operational interpretation is: Demand a stable time-step refinement pattern. A plausible-shaped graph does not prove that numerical error is negligible. Before making a final decision, identify a practical observation capable of distinguishing this explanation from an alternative. Such observations might include simultaneous inlet and outlet compositions, a pressure profile, a verified utility duty, a time-dependent level record or temperature-dependent pure-component data. Choose observations appropriate to the specific equipment, and document the measurement range and data quality.
