§6.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Flowsheet Recycle Loops and Numerical Convergence

Scope: Resolve process loops without treating an unconverged iteration as a valid plant prediction.

End-to-end teaching case: A solvent/reagent recycle system with a purge, a splitter and a unit requiring repeated solution passes.

Engineering question driving this unit

Imagine that the simulated plant must answer a design question about recycles, numerical solvers and convergence. The answer should not be a screenshot of a flowsheet. It should be a transparent chain: defined physical boundary → fixed and calculated quantities → constitutive laws → numerical method → independent check → decision and evidence limitations. A solvent/reagent recycle system with a purge, a splitter and a unit requiring repeated solution passes. Your output should clearly distinguish an imposed operating condition from a calculated stream result.

Four-lesson learning sequence

1. [Recycle and purge as component balances](../lessons/unit-06-lesson-01.html) — Governing model: `I_loop = I_fresh/[1−r(1−p)]`. Lab: [Inert accumulation versus recycle purge](../labs/lab-16.html).

2. [Tear streams and fixed-point convergence](../lessons/unit-06-lesson-02.html) — Governing model: `x_(n+1)=b+a x_n; x*=b/(1−a)`. Lab: [Fixed-point recycle iteration and divergence](../labs/lab-17.html).

3. [Numerical error: Euler versus Runge–Kutta](../lessons/unit-06-lesson-03.html) — Governing model: `dy/dt=−k y; y_exact=exp(−kt)`. Lab: [Forward Euler versus fourth-order Runge–Kutta](../labs/lab-18.html).

4. [Diagnosing oscillation, divergence and false closure](../lessons/unit-06-lesson-04.html) — Governing model: `relative change = |x_(n+1)−x_n| / max(1,|x_n|)`. Lab: [Fixed-point recycle iteration and divergence](../labs/lab-17.html).

Working with the central flowsheet

The main scenario is not a plant drawing with arbitrary numbers copied from an operating facility. It is the explicitly synthetic instructional case: A solvent/reagent recycle system with a purge, a splitter and a unit requiring repeated solution passes. Begin by constructing a stream register in which each row contains a stream identifier, the material phase, independent composition variables, total-flow units, temperature, pressure, source block and destination block. For every unit operation attach a balance that can be evaluated independently of the flowsheet iteration. Never assume that graphical arrows prove mass or energy conservation.

A reproducible simulation study also requires a model-boundary register. Label one assumption as a conservation law, one as a constitutive correlation and one as a numerical choice. Conservation laws remain valid as mathematical accounting, subject to correct species and energy definitions. Constitutive correlations need a range of physical applicability. A numerical choice, such as a time step or root tolerance, can change the error without changing the model physics. If a prediction is sensitive to a correlation or solver choice, that sensitivity belongs in the reported result.

Quantities that must be explicit

Use an absolute reference state when an energy balance depends on enthalpy. Identify whether flow is molar, mass-based, or volumetric before using a rate expression. Distinguish local stream variables from plant-wide targets. A measured product purity and a computed purity are not interchangeable evidence. In a unit involving two phases, write the overall composition and each phase composition separately; an overall component balance remains the same even when the phase states change.

Record property assumptions, numerical solver settings and the sign convention for heat and work. For a negative heater duty, the arithmetic may be correct while the configured utility is wrong. For an infeasible flash, the absence of a two-phase root is physically informative; the application must not draw a plausible curve by extrapolating outside a vetted coefficient interval.

Suggested independent audit

Complete an algebraic limit test, a numerical benchmark and a deliberately adverse case. For the limit test, select a zero-flow, zero-duty, zero-conversion or unity-equilibrium case appropriate to the model. For the benchmark, reproduce one displayed result by hand or a separate computation and write down the governing equation and units. For the adverse case, use a nonconvergent recycle, an out-of-range property condition, a negative inventory attempt or a conflicting specification to verify that the model warns rather than silently changes assumptions.

Connection to the interactive laboratory

Every interactive model is a simplified teaching kernel and is accompanied by its own equation, parameter ranges, limitation statement and analysis prompt. For this unit, begin with [Laboratory 16: Inert accumulation versus recycle purge](../labs/lab-16.html) and check the labels before moving a slider. A plotted trend is a hypothesis to explain from the model, not measured evidence from an operating plant.

Unit mastery criteria

You can articulate why the selected model is mathematically closed, reproduce one calculated output, expose a parameter whose value matters to the result, describe the main omitted physical effect, and recommend what data or alternative model would be needed to use the estimate for a real design. Show the residual and a unit-bearing numerical result rather than only a completion mark.

§6.2 Unit 6, Lesson 1: Recycle and purge as component balances

Unit 6, Lesson 1: Recycle and purge as component balances

Part of: [Recycles, Numerical Solvers and Convergence](../units/unit-06.html). Related interactive laboratory: [Lab 16, Inert accumulation versus recycle purge](../labs/lab-16.html). Lesson classification: synthetic teaching example, not a calibrated plant model.

What you will be able to demonstrate

After studying this lesson you should be able to define the quantities needed for recycle and purge as component balances, justify the selected model, work the reference arithmetic with consistent units, distinguish a numerical convergence problem from a physical-model error, and communicate a specific limitation. Your final calculation should identify both the output and the evidence required before it could inform a plant decision. Use the linked laboratory to demonstrate a nontrivial sensitivity, not merely to reproduce its default plot.

Physical interpretation and model boundary

A purge prevents a nonreacting component from accumulating indefinitely in a closed recycle. If a fraction r of a mixed process stream is returned and a fraction p of that return is purged, the effective inert return fraction is r(1−p). The steady-state inert balance is a geometric series. The result is sensitive when the return fraction approaches one: tiny parameter changes can greatly alter the inert flow.

The process example is deliberately constrained to a solvent/reagent recycle system with a purge, a splitter and a unit requiring repeated solution passes. In a modeling report, the relevant boundary must include any place where mass, energy or momentum can enter or leave, and the time basis must match every rate. Write separate entries for imposed specifications and unknown results; otherwise a flowsheet editor may overconstrain the model and resolve conflicting values in an unexpected way. This distinction is central to recycle and purge as component balances because several different assumptions can yield similar plotted curves while representing different physical devices.

Governing relation: I_loop = I_fresh/[1−r(1−p)]. This compact relation is useful only with a defined variable list. Describe which quantities are known, which are solved, and how the physical units cancel. If a value supplied to the relationship is a concentration, specify whether it is molar, mass-based or dimensionless and do not use a rate parameter calibrated on a different basis. The governing relation is a simplifying model; the lesson checks whether the simplification is visible and auditable.

Worked reference exercise

Given and claimed result (synthetic inputs): An inert fresh input of 1 kmol/h, recycle fraction r=0.8 and purge fraction p=0.1 yield I_loop=1/0.28≈3.571 kmol/h.

Start the calculation by writing down the input values, their dimensions, and the full equation. Establish a consistent unit system before computing a result: use seconds for flows quoted per second, hours for rate coefficients quoted per hour, Kelvin for any absolute-temperature dependence, and concentration fractions only when the selected equation expects fractions. If a temperature rise appears in a sensible-heat expression, the increment is numerically the same in K and °C, but an absolute-temperature exponent requires Kelvin.

Substitute the values into the symbolic expression before rounding intermediate results. Then perform a deliberately independent calculation: for a linear balance, reconstruct the corresponding inflow and outflow totals; for a phase equilibrium, reconstruct the sum of liquid and vapor component fractions; for a reactor, evaluate the zero-time or zero-rate limit; for a numerical solver, compare with an analytic case. Use more digits in intermediate values than in the reported engineering result. A residual smaller than machine rounding is reassuring, but it does not validate an incorrect underlying assumption.

Next change one independent input and predict the direction of the response before looking at the updated model output. This step matters because a numerical graph may look smooth even when a sign convention has been reversed. Record the parameter change, the new output, the difference relative to baseline, and whether a conservation limit or an operating constraint becomes active. The result should be evaluated as a comparison within the stated model, not as a prediction of unseen equipment.

A reproducible simulation workflow

1 — Define the task. For recycle and purge as component balances, describe the required output and the acceptable numerical or physical error. Decide whether the result is a state, a rate, a duty, a composition or an economic metric. An error in this classification propagates into the entire flowsheet.

2 — Enumerate inputs. Use the lesson equation I_loop = I_fresh/[1−r(1−p)] to identify independent conditions. Place the measured or stipulated inputs in one column and solver-derived values in another. Inspect whether any input is absent, contradictory, extrapolated or implicitly supplied by an inaccessible property library.

3 — Build the minimum physical model. Start with the simplest conservation or equilibrium statement that describes the scenario. Before adding extra unit operations, establish a hand benchmark from the declared reference exercise. If the benchmark cannot be repeated, reduce the model instead of adding more parameters.

4 — Solve with diagnostics. Select direct substitution for explicit formulas, a bracketed root finder for a single well-behaved nonlinear root, and an integration method appropriate to an initial-value problem. Record the termination condition. Reject nonfinite values, negative physical inventories or phase classifications that violate the declared domains. A converged calculation can still be physically invalid.

5 — Challenge the answer. Calculate the loop inventory for purge fractions 0, 0.05, 0.1 and 0.2 and explain the steepness near a closed loop. Change the model coefficients within the stated range and repeat the conservation test. Distinguish sensitivity caused by engineering physics from sensitivity caused by a numerical tolerance, plotting interval or initial guess.

Numerical and physical verification

Model-specific benchmark: At purge 100%, loop inert equals fresh; for r<1 and p=0, result remains finite.

Most important restriction: This single-inert equation is not a model of reaction conversion, phase separation or pressure-driven recycle feasibility.

A useful validation hierarchy has several levels. First, dimensionally balance the equation and confirm that signed terms can be interpreted physically. Second, test one limiting value such as no flow, unity separation selectivity, zero temperature difference or negligible reaction time. Third, run the model at two different numerical resolutions and check convergence of the answer rather than the smoothness of the drawn line. Fourth, compare against an independent measured data set—but only if such data have actually been provided and their measurement uncertainty is understood.

For nonlinear or highly coupled problems, a small residual may coexist with a wrong root. Check phase validity, conservation and operating boundaries. For a strongly temperature-sensitive equilibrium, bracketing and property-correlation validity are separate tests. For a recycle loop, compare the new tear stream with the previous guess and compute physical flow residuals across the complete loop. For an optimization result, verify both the objective and the physical feasibility of the candidate solution.

Where this model stops being defensible

This single-inert equation is not a model of reaction conversion, phase separation or pressure-driven recycle feasibility. Record this exclusion prominently if the model is exported, reused or embedded in another page. If the omitted effect is expected to be large, replace the current model with a more detailed formulation and obtain appropriate parameter data. Adding decimal places cannot repair missing physics. Likewise, a chart generated using synthetic parameters must never be described as a measurement or a fitted empirical trend.

The operational interpretation is: Track where the purge is located, define the split basis, and verify the inert fresh input equals the final inert loss from the entire process boundary. Before making a final decision, identify a practical observation capable of distinguishing this explanation from an alternative. Such observations might include simultaneous inlet and outlet compositions, a pressure profile, a verified utility duty, a time-dependent level record or temperature-dependent pure-component data. Choose observations appropriate to the specific equipment, and document the measurement range and data quality.

The primary task is: Calculate the loop inventory for purge fractions 0, 0.05, 0.1 and 0.2 and explain the steepness near a closed loop.

Identify the predicted trend, the mechanism producing it, and one reason why the same trend may fail in a real plant. §6.4 Unit 6, Lesson 3: Numerical error: Euler versus Runge–Kutta

Unit 6, Lesson 3: Numerical error: Euler versus Runge–Kutta

Forward Euler advances using the derivative at the beginning of a step; classic fourth-order Runge–Kutta evaluates additional intermediate slopes. The order is not an unconditional guarantee of physical accuracy: discontinuities, positivity requirements and stiffness can defeat a convenient explicit step size. Use a refinement study and compare end-point errors.

What you will be able to demonstrate

After studying this lesson you should be able to define the quantities needed for diagnosing oscillation, divergence and false closure, justify the selected model, work the reference arithmetic with consistent units, distinguish a numerical convergence problem from a physical-model error, and communicate a specific limitation. Your final calculation should identify both the output and the evidence required before it could inform a plant decision. Use the linked laboratory to demonstrate a nontrivial sensitivity, not merely to reproduce its default plot.

Physical interpretation and model boundary

A solver can stop because an iteration limit is reached, a limit cycle forms, derivatives are undefined or a secondary criterion appears satisfied. Distinguish solver status from physical closure. Detect repeated states, monotonic divergence and values moving outside acceptable domains. Separate machine precision issues from nonlinear conditioning: shrinking a tolerance cannot repair an inconsistent flowsheet.

The process example is deliberately constrained to a solvent/reagent recycle system with a purge, a splitter and a unit requiring repeated solution passes. In a modeling report, the relevant boundary must include any place where mass, energy or momentum can enter or leave, and the time basis must match every rate. Write separate entries for imposed specifications and unknown results; otherwise a flowsheet editor may overconstrain the model and resolve conflicting values in an unexpected way. This distinction is central to diagnosing oscillation, divergence and false closure because several different assumptions can yield similar plotted curves while representing different physical devices.

Governing relation: relative change = |x_(n+1)−x_n| / max(1,|x_n|). This compact relation is useful only with a defined variable list. Describe which quantities are known, which are solved, and how the physical units cancel. If a value supplied to the relationship is a concentration, specify whether it is molar, mass-based or dimensionless and do not use a rate parameter calibrated on a different basis. The governing relation is a simplifying model; the lesson checks whether the simplification is visible and auditable.

Worked reference exercise

Given and claimed result (synthetic inputs): An iteration at x_n=1000 and x_(n+1)=1000.01 has a small relative update yet may violate a 10 kmol/h component balance.

Start the calculation by writing down the input values, their dimensions, and the full equation. Establish a consistent unit system before computing a result: use seconds for flows quoted per second, hours for rate coefficients quoted per hour, Kelvin for any absolute-temperature dependence, and concentration fractions only when the selected equation expects fractions. If a temperature rise appears in a sensible-heat expression, the increment is numerically the same in K and °C, but an absolute-temperature exponent requires Kelvin.

Substitute the values into the symbolic expression before rounding intermediate results. Then perform a deliberately independent calculation: for a linear balance, reconstruct the corresponding inflow and outflow totals; for a phase equilibrium, reconstruct the sum of liquid and vapor component fractions; for a reactor, evaluate the zero-time or zero-rate limit; for a numerical solver, compare with an analytic case. Use more digits in intermediate values than in the reported engineering result. A residual smaller than machine rounding is reassuring, but it does not validate an incorrect underlying assumption.

Next change one independent input and predict the direction of the response before looking at the updated model output. This step matters because a numerical graph may look smooth even when a sign convention has been reversed. Record the parameter change, the new output, the difference relative to baseline, and whether a conservation limit or an operating constraint becomes active. The result should be evaluated as a comparison within the stated model, not as a prediction of unseen equipment.

A reproducible simulation workflow

1 — Define the task. For diagnosing oscillation, divergence and false closure, describe the required output and the acceptable numerical or physical error. Decide whether the result is a state, a rate, a duty, a composition or an economic metric. An error in this classification propagates into the entire flowsheet.

2 — Enumerate inputs. Use the lesson equation relative change = |x_(n+1)−x_n| / max(1,|x_n|) to identify independent conditions. Place the measured or stipulated inputs in one column and solver-derived values in another. Inspect whether any input is absent, contradictory, extrapolated or implicitly supplied by an inaccessible property library.

3 — Build the minimum physical model. Start with the simplest conservation or equilibrium statement that describes the scenario. Before adding extra unit operations, establish a hand benchmark from the declared reference exercise. If the benchmark cannot be repeated, reduce the model instead of adding more parameters.

4 — Solve with diagnostics. Select direct substitution for explicit formulas, a bracketed root finder for a single well-behaved nonlinear root, and an integration method appropriate to an initial-value problem. Record the termination condition. Reject nonfinite values, negative physical inventories or phase classifications that violate the declared domains. A converged calculation can still be physically invalid.

5 — Challenge the answer. Describe a three-part diagnostic using iteration trend, balance residual and sensitivity to initial guess. Change the model coefficients within the stated range and repeat the conservation test. Distinguish sensitivity caused by engineering physics from sensitivity caused by a numerical tolerance, plotting interval or initial guess.

Numerical and physical verification

Model-specific benchmark: If |a|<1, x_n approaches b/(1−a); otherwise do not present terminal value as converged.

Most important restriction: A falsely converged recycle may produce believable energy consumption numbers while violating conservation upstream of the tear.

A useful validation hierarchy has several levels. First, dimensionally balance the equation and confirm that signed terms can be interpreted physically. Second, test one limiting value such as no flow, unity separation selectivity, zero temperature difference or negligible reaction time. Third, run the model at two different numerical resolutions and check convergence of the answer rather than the smoothness of the drawn line. Fourth, compare against an independent measured data set—but only if such data have actually been provided and their measurement uncertainty is understood.

For nonlinear or highly coupled problems, a small residual may coexist with a wrong root. Check phase validity, conservation and operating boundaries. For a strongly temperature-sensitive equilibrium, bracketing and property-correlation validity are separate tests. For a recycle loop, compare the new tear stream with the previous guess and compute physical flow residuals across the complete loop. For an optimization result, verify both the objective and the physical feasibility of the candidate solution.
