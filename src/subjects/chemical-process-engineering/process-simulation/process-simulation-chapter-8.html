Model Verification, Sensitivity, Safety and Economic Sizing

Comprehensive university textbook unit covering fundamental theory, mathematical derivations, numerical modeling, and process engineering validation of Model Verification, Sensitivity, Safety and Economic Sizing. Developed for undergraduate chemical engineering study.

§8.1 Foundational Principles & Theory: Model Verification, Sensitivity, Safety and Economic Sizing

Scope: Separate verification from validation, propagate uncertainty and compare alternatives with explicit objective and constraints.

End-to-end teaching case: An energy-recovery retrofit whose uncertain reaction performance, heat transfer and annualized costs must be assessed.

Engineering question driving this unit

Imagine that the simulated plant must answer a design question about verification, uncertainty and economic decisions. The answer should not be a screenshot of a flowsheet. It should be a transparent chain: defined physical boundary → fixed and calculated quantities → constitutive laws → numerical method → independent check → decision and evidence limitations. An energy-recovery retrofit whose uncertain reaction performance, heat transfer and annualized costs must be assessed. Your output should clearly distinguish an imposed operating condition from a calculated stream result.

Four-lesson learning sequence

1. [Verification, validation and numerical audits](../lessons/unit-08-lesson-01.html) — Governing model: `error = simulation result − independent benchmark`. Lab: [Forward Euler versus fourth-order Runge–Kutta](../labs/lab-18.html).

2. [Thermal resistances and exchanger-model uncertainty](../lessons/unit-08-lesson-02.html) — Governing model: `1/U = 1/h_hot + delta/k_wall + 1/h_cold`. Lab: [Composite wall thermal resistance](../labs/lab-22.html).

3. [Economic objectives and constrained optima](../lessons/unit-08-lesson-03.html) — Governing model: `C(A)=a A^0.65 + b/A²`. Lab: [Synthetic capital–energy tradeoff optimum](../labs/lab-23.html).

4. [Uncertainty propagation and credible reporting](../lessons/unit-08-lesson-04.html) — Governing model: `X = 1−exp(−k tau), with uncertain k`. Lab: [Monte Carlo conversion uncertainty](../labs/lab-24.html).

Working with the central flowsheet

The main scenario is not a plant drawing with arbitrary numbers copied from an operating facility. It is the explicitly synthetic instructional case: An energy-recovery retrofit whose uncertain reaction performance, heat transfer and annualized costs must be assessed. Begin by constructing a stream register in which each row contains a stream identifier, the material phase, independent composition variables, total-flow units, temperature, pressure, source block and destination block. For every unit operation attach a balance that can be evaluated independently of the flowsheet iteration. Never assume that graphical arrows prove mass or energy conservation.

A reproducible simulation study also requires a model-boundary register. Label one assumption as a conservation law, one as a constitutive correlation and one as a numerical choice. Conservation laws remain valid as mathematical accounting, subject to correct species and energy definitions. Constitutive correlations need a range of physical applicability. A numerical choice, such as a time step or root tolerance, can change the error without changing the model physics. If a prediction is sensitive to a correlation or solver choice, that sensitivity belongs in the reported result.

Quantities that must be explicit

Use an absolute reference state when an energy balance depends on enthalpy. Identify whether flow is molar, mass-based, or volumetric before using a rate expression. Distinguish local stream variables from plant-wide targets. A measured product purity and a computed purity are not interchangeable evidence. In a unit involving two phases, write the overall composition and each phase composition separately; an overall component balance remains the same even when the phase states change.

Record property assumptions, numerical solver settings and the sign convention for heat and work. For a negative heater duty, the arithmetic may be correct while the configured utility is wrong. For an infeasible flash, the absence of a two-phase root is physically informative; the application must not draw a plausible curve by extrapolating outside a vetted coefficient interval.

Suggested independent audit

Complete an algebraic limit test, a numerical benchmark and a deliberately adverse case. For the limit test, select a zero-flow, zero-duty, zero-conversion or unity-equilibrium case appropriate to the model. For the benchmark, reproduce one displayed result by hand or a separate computation and write down the governing equation and units. For the adverse case, use a nonconvergent recycle, an out-of-range property condition, a negative inventory attempt or a conflicting specification to verify that the model warns rather than silently changes assumptions.

Connection to the interactive laboratory

Every interactive model is a simplified teaching kernel and is accompanied by its own equation, parameter ranges, limitation statement and analysis prompt. For this unit, begin with [Laboratory 18: Forward Euler versus fourth-order Runge–Kutta](../labs/lab-18.html) and check the labels before moving a slider. A plotted trend is a hypothesis to explain from the model, not measured evidence from an operating plant.

Unit mastery criteria

You can articulate why the selected model is mathematically closed, reproduce one calculated output, expose a parameter whose value matters to the result, describe the main omitted physical effect, and recommend what data or alternative model would be needed to use the estimate for a real design. Show the residual and a unit-bearing numerical result rather than only a completion mark.

§8.2 Unit 8, Lesson 1: Verification, validation and numerical audits

Unit 8, Lesson 1: Verification, validation and numerical audits

Part of: [Verification, Uncertainty and Economic Decisions](../units/unit-08.html). Related interactive laboratory: [Lab 18, Forward Euler versus fourth-order Runge–Kutta](../labs/lab-18.html). Lesson classification: synthetic teaching example, not a calibrated plant model.

What you will be able to demonstrate

After studying this lesson you should be able to define the quantities needed for verification, validation and numerical audits, justify the selected model, work the reference arithmetic with consistent units, distinguish a numerical convergence problem from a physical-model error, and communicate a specific limitation. Your final calculation should identify both the output and the evidence required before it could inform a plant decision. Use the linked laboratory to demonstrate a nontrivial sensitivity, not merely to reproduce its default plot.

Physical interpretation and model boundary

Verification asks whether the mathematical model has been solved as intended. Validation asks whether the model represents the physical system adequately for a specific purpose. An exact benchmark is powerful for verification but cannot validate a property correlation against laboratory measurements. A credible workflow documents units, conservation residuals, limiting cases, grid or time-step independence, and independent reference calculations before fitting data.

The process example is deliberately constrained to an energy-recovery retrofit whose uncertain reaction performance, heat transfer and annualized costs must be assessed. In a modeling report, the relevant boundary must include any place where mass, energy or momentum can enter or leave, and the time basis must match every rate. Write separate entries for imposed specifications and unknown results; otherwise a flowsheet editor may overconstrain the model and resolve conflicting values in an unexpected way. This distinction is central to verification, validation and numerical audits because several different assumptions can yield similar plotted curves while representing different physical devices.

Governing relation: error = simulation result − independent benchmark. This compact relation is useful only with a defined variable list. Describe which quantities are known, which are solved, and how the physical units cancel. If a value supplied to the relationship is a concentration, specify whether it is molar, mass-based or dimensionless and do not use a rate parameter calibrated on a different basis. The governing relation is a simplifying model; the lesson checks whether the simplification is visible and auditable.

Worked reference exercise

Given and claimed result (synthetic inputs): When an analytic first-order conversion is 0.63212 and a solver reports 0.63210, the absolute error is approximately 0.00002.

Start the calculation by writing down the input values, their dimensions, and the full equation. Establish a consistent unit system before computing a result: use seconds for flows quoted per second, hours for rate coefficients quoted per hour, Kelvin for any absolute-temperature dependence, and concentration fractions only when the selected equation expects fractions. If a temperature rise appears in a sensible-heat expression, the increment is numerically the same in K and °C, but an absolute-temperature exponent requires Kelvin.

Substitute the values into the symbolic expression before rounding intermediate results. Then perform a deliberately independent calculation: for a linear balance, reconstruct the corresponding inflow and outflow totals; for a phase equilibrium, reconstruct the sum of liquid and vapor component fractions; for a reactor, evaluate the zero-time or zero-rate limit; for a numerical solver, compare with an analytic case. Use more digits in intermediate values than in the reported engineering result. A residual smaller than machine rounding is reassuring, but it does not validate an incorrect underlying assumption.

Next change one independent input and predict the direction of the response before looking at the updated model output. This step matters because a numerical graph may look smooth even when a sign convention has been reversed. Record the parameter change, the new output, the difference relative to baseline, and whether a conservation limit or an operating constraint becomes active. The result should be evaluated as a comparison within the stated model, not as a prediction of unseen equipment.

A reproducible simulation workflow

1 — Define the task. For verification, validation and numerical audits, describe the required output and the acceptable numerical or physical error. Decide whether the result is a state, a rate, a duty, a composition or an economic metric. An error in this classification propagates into the entire flowsheet.

2 — Enumerate inputs. Use the lesson equation error = simulation result − independent benchmark to identify independent conditions. Place the measured or stipulated inputs in one column and solver-derived values in another. Inspect whether any input is absent, contradictory, extrapolated or implicitly supplied by an inaccessible property library.

3 — Build the minimum physical model. Start with the simplest conservation or equilibrium statement that describes the scenario. Before adding extra unit operations, establish a hand benchmark from the declared reference exercise. If the benchmark cannot be repeated, reduce the model instead of adding more parameters.

4 — Solve with diagnostics. Select direct substitution for explicit formulas, a bracketed root finder for a single well-behaved nonlinear root, and an integration method appropriate to an initial-value problem. Record the termination condition. Reject nonfinite values, negative physical inventories or phase classifications that violate the declared domains. A converged calculation can still be physically invalid.

5 — Challenge the answer. Specify three tests for a new flash solver, including one two-phase mass balance and one single-phase classification. Change the model coefficients within the stated range and repeat the conservation test. Distinguish sensitivity caused by engineering physics from sensitivity caused by a numerical tolerance, plotting interval or initial guess.

Numerical and physical verification

Model-specific benchmark: RK4 should be more accurate for the selected nonstiff test; y(0)=1.

Most important restriction: A passing unit test is evidence about the tested case only. It is not an assertion of exhaustive correctness or industrial suitability.

A useful validation hierarchy has several levels. First, dimensionally balance the equation and confirm that signed terms can be interpreted physically. Second, test one limiting value such as no flow, unity separation selectivity, zero temperature difference or negligible reaction time. Third, run the model at two different numerical resolutions and check convergence of the answer rather than the smoothness of the drawn line. Fourth, compare against an independent measured data set—but only if such data have actually been provided and their measurement uncertainty is understood.

For nonlinear or highly coupled problems, a small residual may coexist with a wrong root. Check phase validity, conservation and operating boundaries. For a strongly temperature-sensitive equilibrium, bracketing and property-correlation validity are separate tests. For a recycle loop, compare the new tear stream with the previous guess and compute physical flow residuals across the complete loop. For an optimization result, verify both the objective and the physical feasibility of the candidate solution.

Where this model stops being defensible

A passing unit test is evidence about the tested case only. It is not an assertion of exhaustive correctness or industrial suitability. Record this exclusion prominently if the model is exported, reused or embedded in another page. If the omitted effect is expected to be large, replace the current model with a more detailed formulation and obtain appropriate parameter data. Adding decimal places cannot repair missing physics. Likewise, a chart generated using synthetic parameters must never be described as a measurement or a fitted empirical trend.

The operational interpretation is: Use a test hierarchy: algebraic invariants, numerical benchmarks, visual browser behavior, and then independent physical comparison if real data exist. Before making a final decision, identify a practical observation capable of distinguishing this explanation from an alternative. Such observations might include simultaneous inlet and outlet compositions, a pressure profile, a verified utility duty, a time-dependent level record or temperature-dependent pure-component data. Choose observations appropriate to the specific equipment, and document the measurement range and data quality.

Guided laboratory investigation

Open [the interactive Forward Euler versus fourth-order Runge–Kutta model](../labs/lab-18.html). Its sliders expose the primary assumptions; its output is computed locally in the browser, with no network dependency. Read the displayed equation and variable units before changing parameters. Export CSV to preserve numerical values for independent checking. The primary task is: Specify three tests for a new flash solver, including one two-phase mass balance and one single-phase classification.

The laboratory also states: Fixed 4 s horizon, positive single-state nonstiff test; no adaptive or implicit solver. Investigate a plausible operating setting and a limiting setting. Identify the predicted trend, the mechanism producing it, and one reason why the same trend may fail in a real plant. Do not call a laboratory “validated” merely because the curve responds to input changes; validation needs independent comparison and a defined intended use.

What to include in your solution notebook

Your notebook entry should contain a one-line physical question, a labeled sketch or stream inventory, the model equation, a variable-and-unit table, the reference case calculation, one sensitivity experiment, a balance or limiting-case residual and a transparent limitation. Finish with a claim that is no stronger than the evidence. For verification, validation and numerical audits, distinguish a computed value from a specified value and explain whether extra physical observations would change confidence in the result.

  1. Specify three tests for a new flash solver, including one two-phase mass balance and one single-phase classification.

§8.5 Unit 8, Lesson 4: Uncertainty propagation and credible reporting

Unit 8, Lesson 4: Uncertainty propagation and credible reporting

Part of: [Verification, Uncertainty and Economic Decisions](../units/unit-08.html). Related interactive laboratory: [Lab 24, Monte Carlo conversion uncertainty](../labs/lab-24.html). Lesson classification: synthetic teaching example, not a calibrated plant model.

What you will be able to demonstrate

After studying this lesson you should be able to define the quantities needed for uncertainty propagation and credible reporting, justify the selected model, work the reference arithmetic with consistent units, distinguish a numerical convergence problem from a physical-model error, and communicate a specific limitation. Your final calculation should identify both the output and the evidence required before it could inform a plant decision. Use the linked laboratory to demonstrate a nontrivial sensitivity, not merely to reproduce its default plot.

Physical interpretation and model boundary

Uncertainty comes from parameters, measurement quality, input variability and model-form limitations. A Monte Carlo sample propagates a specified input distribution through a deterministic model; the shape of its output does not certify that the assumed distribution matches reality. Fixed random seeds make computational teaching examples reproducible. Distinguish confidence intervals for estimated parameters from physical variability among operating scenarios.

The process example is deliberately constrained to an energy-recovery retrofit whose uncertain reaction performance, heat transfer and annualized costs must be assessed. In a modeling report, the relevant boundary must include any place where mass, energy or momentum can enter or leave, and the time basis must match every rate. Write separate entries for imposed specifications and unknown results; otherwise a flowsheet editor may overconstrain the model and resolve conflicting values in an unexpected way. This distinction is central to uncertainty propagation and credible reporting because several different assumptions can yield similar plotted curves while representing different physical devices.

Governing relation: X = 1−exp(−k tau), with uncertain k. This compact relation is useful only with a defined variable list. Describe which quantities are known, which are solved, and how the physical units cancel. If a value supplied to the relationship is a concentration, specify whether it is molar, mass-based or dimensionless and do not use a rate parameter calibrated on a different basis. The governing relation is a simplifying model; the lesson checks whether the simplification is visible and auditable.

Worked reference exercise

Given and claimed result (synthetic inputs): If k varies across plausible scenarios, report the resulting distribution of predicted conversion instead of presenting one rounded conversion as measured fact.

Start the calculation by writing down the input values, their dimensions, and the full equation. Establish a consistent unit system before computing a result: use seconds for flows quoted per second, hours for rate coefficients quoted per hour, Kelvin for any absolute-temperature dependence, and concentration fractions only when the selected equation expects fractions. If a temperature rise appears in a sensible-heat expression, the increment is numerically the same in K and °C, but an absolute-temperature exponent requires Kelvin.

Substitute the values into the symbolic expression before rounding intermediate results. Then perform a deliberately independent calculation: for a linear balance, reconstruct the corresponding inflow and outflow totals; for a phase equilibrium, reconstruct the sum of liquid and vapor component fractions; for a reactor, evaluate the zero-time or zero-rate limit; for a numerical solver, compare with an analytic case. Use more digits in intermediate values than in the reported engineering result. A residual smaller than machine rounding is reassuring, but it does not validate an incorrect underlying assumption.

Next change one independent input and predict the direction of the response before looking at the updated model output. This step matters because a numerical graph may look smooth even when a sign convention has been reversed. Record the parameter change, the new output, the difference relative to baseline, and whether a conservation limit or an operating constraint becomes active. The result should be evaluated as a comparison within the stated model, not as a prediction of unseen equipment.

A reproducible simulation workflow

1 — Define the task. For uncertainty propagation and credible reporting, describe the required output and the acceptable numerical or physical error. Decide whether the result is a state, a rate, a duty, a composition or an economic metric. An error in this classification propagates into the entire flowsheet.

2 — Enumerate inputs. Use the lesson equation X = 1−exp(−k tau), with uncertain k to identify independent conditions. Place the measured or stipulated inputs in one column and solver-derived values in another. Inspect whether any input is absent, contradictory, extrapolated or implicitly supplied by an inaccessible property library.

3 — Build the minimum physical model. Start with the simplest conservation or equilibrium statement that describes the scenario. Before adding extra unit operations, establish a hand benchmark from the declared reference exercise. If the benchmark cannot be repeated, reduce the model instead of adding more parameters.

4 — Solve with diagnostics. Select direct substitution for explicit formulas, a bracketed root finder for a single well-behaved nonlinear root, and an integration method appropriate to an initial-value problem. Record the termination condition. Reject nonfinite values, negative physical inventories or phase classifications that violate the declared domains. A converged calculation can still be physically invalid.

5 — Challenge the answer. Double the assumed kinetic uncertainty at fixed mean and explain what changed in conversion variability without claiming measured plant risk. Change the model coefficients within the stated range and repeat the conservation test. Distinguish sensitivity caused by engineering physics from sensitivity caused by a numerical tolerance, plotting interval or initial guess.

Numerical and physical verification

Model-specific benchmark: All conversions remain 0–1; repeated inputs give exactly the same seeded sample and chart.

Most important restriction: Normal-distribution tails can create unphysical negative kinetic constants; a defensible sampling rule must handle those draws transparently.

A useful validation hierarchy has several levels. First, dimensionally balance the equation and confirm that signed terms can be interpreted physically. Second, test one limiting value such as no flow, unity separation selectivity, zero temperature difference or negligible reaction time. Third, run the model at two different numerical resolutions and check convergence of the answer rather than the smoothness of the drawn line. Fourth, compare against an independent measured data set—but only if such data have actually been provided and their measurement uncertainty is understood.

For nonlinear or highly coupled problems, a small residual may coexist with a wrong root. Check phase validity, conservation and operating boundaries. For a strongly temperature-sensitive equilibrium, bracketing and property-correlation validity are separate tests. For a recycle loop, compare the new tear stream with the previous guess and compute physical flow residuals across the complete loop. For an optimization result, verify both the objective and the physical feasibility of the candidate solution.

Where this model stops being defensible

Normal-distribution tails can create unphysical negative kinetic constants; a defensible sampling rule must handle those draws transparently. Record this exclusion prominently if the model is exported, reused or embedded in another page. If the omitted effect is expected to be large, replace the current model with a more detailed formulation and obtain appropriate parameter data. Adding decimal places cannot repair missing physics. Likewise, a chart generated using synthetic parameters must never be described as a measurement or a fitted empirical trend.

The operational interpretation is: Present the median and central interval together with the assumed input distribution, sample count and model caveats. Use sensitivity analysis before committing money to more data. Before making a final decision, identify a practical observation capable of distinguishing this explanation from an alternative. Such observations might include simultaneous inlet and outlet compositions, a pressure profile, a verified utility duty, a time-dependent level record or temperature-dependent pure-component data. Choose observations appropriate to the specific equipment, and document the measurement range and data quality.
