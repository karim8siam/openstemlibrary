§7.1 Guiding question: How do rock–water interactions and geological deposits affect seafloor chemistry and environmental responsibilities?

Synthetic case: A hypothetical seabed investigation finds metal enrichment near a ridge and patchy sediment chemistry on an abyssal plain. The team cannot infer recoverable ore from concentrations alone, nor ecosystem impacts from one baseline transect. It designs a transparent, minimally invasive sampling plan.

§7.2 Geological framework: questions, scales and competing evidence

Marine mineralization involves heat, chemistry, oxidation state, sediment supply and geologic time. A high metal assay is not a measured ore reserve: continuity, grade, recoverability, costs, governance and ecosystem effects must also be established. Hydrothermal sulfides, manganese nodules and crusts arise through distinct geological processes. Pore-water reactions may add or remove elements long after sediment deposition. Baseline environmental studies should document natural geological heterogeneity before they attribute concentration changes to human activity. Neither an acoustically reflective patch nor an enrichment ratio proves contamination or economically valuable mineralization. Conservative sampling avoids overclaiming both commercial opportunity and environmental harm.

§7.3 Five lessons with evidence notebooks

Each lesson joins a physical principle, an observable, a competing explanation, and a quantified classroom calculation. A numerical result is conditional on the stated synthetic inputs. Details such as local hazard probability, legal extraction requirements or physical-property inversion cannot be derived from the simple cases.

§7.4 Seabed erosion and infrastructure corridors

Geological principle: what produces the pattern?

Currents, waves, storms and slope processes can erode sediment around subsea structures, while burial exposes pipelines and cables to different mechanical and thermal environments. Geological reconnaissance identifies susceptibility but is not a complete design review.

Observation plan: what is actually measured?

Collect repeat bathymetry, near-bed flow data, sediment grading and geotechnical measurements. Map boulders, active sand waves, slopes and mobile bedforms at engineering-relevant scales.

Alternative hypotheses and what would refute them

A stationary-looking sand wave in one survey may migrate seasonally. Regional bathymetry and coarse geologic maps cannot quantify local burial depth or scour limits for a specific asset.

Design choice: Separate event from background. Estimate whether a measured layer or acoustic feature records a short-lived high-intensity event or the integration of many smaller processes. A process can dominate preserved sediment while occupying little elapsed time. Search for event boundaries, independent dates and reworked materials before extending the inference beyond one location.

§7.5 Hydrothermal circulation at spreading centers

Geological principle: what produces the pattern?

Seawater can circulate through fractured hot crust, exchange heat and elements with rock, and vent at the seafloor. Hydrothermal precipitates and alteration halos reveal integrated histories, not instantaneous fluid conditions.

Observation plan: what is actually measured?

Map heat-flow anomalies, vent chimneys, sulfide textures, dissolved chemistry and crustal permeability proxies. Compare active plume detection with mineralized deposits that may be fossil.

Alternative hypotheses and what would refute them

Metal-rich chimney material does not establish a mineable deposit. The geometry and chemistry of a vent field may evolve rapidly when circulation paths seal or reopen.

Worked numerical investigation

A focused field or laboratory investigation

Use a conservative fluid-mixing model to estimate an endmember concentration and list the assumptions that would fail under precipitation and reaction.

Design choice: Apply a conservation or kinematic constraint. Define what enters and leaves the chosen region, and distinguish a material inventory from a flux. Where the subject is tectonic rather than depositional, check whether distances, ages and rate units are consistent. A residual indicates something unmeasured or a violated assumption; it does not automatically verify a preferred mechanism.

§7.6 Marine authigenic minerals and diagenesis

Geological principle: what produces the pattern?

Glauconite, phosphorites, carbonates and sulfide minerals may form or transform near the sediment–water interface or during burial. Their mineral assemblages often reflect redox, pore-fluid exchange and sedimentation rate.

Observation plan: what is actually measured?

Use microscopy, phase identification, bulk and micro-scale geochemistry, and stratigraphic context. Separate detrital grains from minerals formed after deposition.

Alternative hypotheses and what would refute them

A mineral's presence alone may not specify unique environmental conditions. Late diagenetic growth can overprint initial depositional signals, producing apparent geochemical events that are actually post-burial.

Worked numerical investigation

A focused field or laboratory investigation

Design choice: Replicate the observation. For this topic, describe which parts of a figure are directly measured and which were inferred from calibration, interpolation, geological priors or visual classification. The observation may be convincing even if the preferred explanation remains uncertain. Design a check in which a second observer can reproduce the result using the same input files and clear processing parameters.

§7.7 Manganese nodules, crusts and marine resources

Geological principle: what produces the pattern?

Metal-rich nodules and ferromanganese crusts accrete through combinations of hydrogenetic and diagenetic processes at very slow rates. Spatial coverage, grade and accessibility are distinct from economic or legally recoverable reserves.

Observation plan: what is actually measured?

Document sediment setting, substrate, size distribution, chemical assays, survey density and ecological co-occurrence. Note selection bias of photographed sampling locations.

Alternative hypotheses and what would refute them

High surface abundance does not show continuous subsurface grade, ore recovery feasibility or acceptable environmental impact. A resource estimate needs representative sampling, regulatory context and economic assumptions.

Worked numerical investigation

Synthetic problem — Nodule growth time. A conceptual layer grows at 3 mm/Ma; how long would 15 mm require at constant rate?

Interpretation: Extremely slow net growth is consistent with a long geological process. What this does not establish: Growth is episodic and can include dissolution and detrital mixing.

A focused field or laboratory investigation

Compare a photographed transect with a probability-based sample design and explain why neither justifies a commercial extraction recommendation without much more evidence.

Design choice: Choose a competing model. List two geologically distinct processes that produce a similar measured pattern, then predict a variable on which those explanations disagree. Prefer the prediction with a measurable sign, geometry, age ordering or composition rather than a vague expectation of greater complexity. Specify both an inexpensive screening observation and a decisive but costly validation.

§7.8 Marine sediment contamination and baseline monitoring

Geological principle: what produces the pattern?

Trace elements, hydrocarbons and other contaminants in seabed deposits reflect natural backgrounds, anthropogenic sources, particle sorting and postdepositional chemistry. Concentration changes cannot be interpreted without comparable grain size and analytical methods.

Observation plan: what is actually measured?

Use field blanks, certified reference materials, grain-size normalization, replicate cores and geospatially balanced reference sites. Preserve detection limits and lab batch metadata.

Alternative hypotheses and what would refute them

An apparent hotspot can arise from natural mineral enrichment or differences in organic content. Single-date sampling cannot prove a temporal impact caused by a new activity.

Worked numerical investigation

A focused field or laboratory investigation

Design an environmental baseline that samples temporal variability and control locations before attributing observed differences to a proposed marine intervention.

Design choice: Build a scale-aware cross section. Identify the physical and temporal scale of the process. Specify what a one-dimensional profile hides in the horizontal direction, and what a surface map cannot show about burial history. Represent uncertainty as an interval or alternate boundaries rather than a decorative confidence label; annotate the map with each instrument footprint.

Research deep dive: environmental meaning of a mineral-rich sample

A remotely operated vehicle images a cluster of nodules on the seabed, and a selective grab yields high concentrations of manganese, nickel and other elements. This is a strong reason to study the geological setting, but it is weak evidence for a mineable reserve. The grab may preferentially collect large visible nodules while omitting barren sediment. Grade continuity, areal coverage, thickness, chemical heterogeneity, technical recovery and processing are unmeasured. A resource claim needs probabilistic survey design and geological continuity, plus economic, legal and environmental analysis.

Hydrothermal fields involve fluids modified by reactions with heated crust. Metal-rich vent deposits may form where chemical conditions permit sulfide precipitation, but the surface chimney shape is not a direct inventory of ore beneath it. A fluid sample must be interpreted with mixing and precipitation in mind: conservative end-member calculations are useful benchmarks precisely because real vent systems often deviate from that simple behaviour. Document whether measured concentrations refer to dissolved, particulate or total material.

Sediment contamination studies face a related problem. If a sample is enriched in a metal relative to an arbitrary baseline, that numerical ratio alone does not establish industrial pollution. Natural volcanic input, heavy-mineral sorting, grain-size shifts and redox changes can alter concentration. An environmental baseline should include reference locations, temporal replication, sedimentological context, detection limits and quality-control standards. A paired before-and-after/control design is often more informative than collecting many samples at one impacted-looking location.

A geologically thoughtful study also asks what organisms live on or within the sampled substrate. Ferromanganese crusts, seamount rock surfaces, seeps and sediment plains may host different biological communities. Geology contributes habitat context but cannot replace species observations. Reproducible impact assessment must therefore connect biological transects to representative seabed geological units without implying that a high acoustic backscatter class is automatically a rare habitat.

§7.9 Unit investigation: linking map, core and mechanism

Aim. Use the forty synthetic stations in datasets/unit-07.csv to test a question involving marine geochemistry, resources and environments. Treat the CSV as invented material for practicing statistical quality control, not as an actual seabed dataset. Plot water depth and acoustic sediment thickness along the transect; add markers for the review flags and record how the conclusions change after excluding them. The along-track separation is a schematic distance, not latitude or longitude, and therefore cannot establish the orientation or full area of a geological body.

Design brief. A hypothetical seabed investigation finds metal enrichment near a ridge and patchy sediment chemistry on an abyssal plain. The team cannot infer recoverable ore from concentrations alone, nor ecosystem impacts from one baseline transect. It designs a transparent, minimally invasive sampling plan. Specify one observation that you expect to see if your mechanism is correct and a different one expected under a plausible alternative. Identify three layers of evidence: a survey observation, a physical sample, and a geophysical or chronologic constraint. Give each evidence layer a separate confidence score and justification. Do not average those scores unless their dependence is explicitly justified. Two products derived from the same ship track are not fully independent corroboration.

Procedure, Part A — reconstruct what was measured. Load the CSV and confirm that depth, thickness and size values are positive where expected. Calculate the smallest and largest water depths, the range of recovered thicknesses, and the number of flagged rows. Make two alternative maps: one preserving all points with quality flags visible, another excluding flagged stations. Label this contrast a sensitivity study. The second view should not be automatically judged more correct; removal can itself bias the spatial pattern if poor observations cluster in difficult terrain.

Procedure, Part B — inspect model dependency. Examine the plotted gradient at an interval of two, five and ten stations, and identify which short features are averaged away. Estimate a simple slope in metres per kilometer using a defined pair of samples. State whether this is a geometric seabed slope or a sediment thickness gradient; the distinction affects interpretation. If you attempt uncertainty propagation, specify assumed error correlations rather than selecting a root-sum-square formula by habit.

Procedure, Part C — compare competing histories. Write two plausible geological narratives that fit the synthetic pattern. One should emphasize a structural or long-timescale explanation; the other should emphasize deposition, redistribution or erosional overprinting where meaningful. List predictions that differ: reflector geometry, sediment type, age ordering, fault offsets, pore-fluid signals or mineralogical provenance. Rank possible next observations by ability to distinguish those predictions, not by how dramatic their imagery would look.

Procedure, Part D — communicate limitations. Prepare a four-panel reproducible report: data provenance, primary measurement plot, competing explanation diagram and uncertainty ledger. In the ledger, list unknown vertical datum, unrealistically simplified synthetic values, unmeasured lithology, timing uncertainty and any relevant instrument bias. Explicitly describe which claims the report cannot make. For marine hazards, resource assessment and navigational uses, do not substitute this exercise for official data or specialist review.

Deliverables and grading: 20% clean reproducible analysis, 20% physically coherent interpretations, 20% alternative hypotheses, 20% quantitative unit and sensitivity checks, and 20% uncertainty communication. A technically polished visualization that hides the fact that the data are synthetic cannot receive full credit. Submit the processing steps, plots, an evidence matrix and a revision note explaining which conclusion changed after reviewing uncertainties.

§7.10 Unit assessment: reasoning rather than memorization

Question A: Design a way to disprove the leading hypothesis for marine geochemistry, resources and environments while spending no more than two new stations on the fictional cruise. Justify each station by the predicted difference between models.

Question B: Explain how an erroneous vertical datum, poor coring recovery, or inappropriate age tie point would affect at least two of the results presented in this unit. Distinguish changed numerical answers from changed geological conclusions.

Question C: Propose a figure caption for an unfamiliar scientific reader. It must name what was measured, the units, geographic or stratigraphic coverage, important transformations, and one explicit limitation.

Question D: Choose one of the unit models and identify which environmental or geological variable is intentionally held constant. Explain how failure of that constant would change the interpretation but not necessarily invalidate the model as a teaching aid.

§7.11 Further investigation and practical extension

To extend the fictional unit project into real research, start with the references and select a genuine open dataset with clear acquisition date, license, method, processing version and coordinate reference system. For marine geochemistry, resources and environments, seek at least one direct measurement that does not originate from the same inversion as the map. Compare two spatial resolutions before discussing morphology. Keep real observations strictly separated from the invented training CSV. A reproducible report should link each plotted feature to a raw record, show how missing values were handled, and make failed interpretations visible rather than deleting them from the final narrative.
