§3.1 Guiding question

How are mechanical ground motions turned into trustworthy digital observations?

Fictional unit investigation. A fictional broadband station is upgraded during a month of local seismicity; the replacement sensor has different sensitivity, clipping behavior and data gaps. Students must avoid inventing a trend from the hardware change.

§3.2 Learning workflow

Before beginning, distinguish the scientific question, the measured quantity and the latent parameter that the student hopes to infer. Assign physical units, name a plausible alternate hypothesis, and state what data are fictional. For instruments, preserve original digital counts as well as corrected SI motion. Timing drift, channel orientation, response changes, and acquisition gaps can masquerade as evolving seismicity if ignored. The experiments are deliberately simplified so their calculations can be independently checked. They are not validated field instruments, real-time forecasts, or site-specific hazard assessments.

§3.3 Sensors and instrument response

Learning goals. Explain the mechanism, translate the problem into measurable quantities, test an alternative interpretation and defend an uncertainty statement.

Physical foundation. Velocity sensors, accelerometers and broadband systems convert motion into signals with frequency-dependent gain and phase. Digitized counts require instrument metadata to be expressed as physical velocity, displacement or acceleration. In the broader unit, Reliable seismology starts with traceable measurement. Digitized amplitudes, timing systems and instrument calibration are not interchangeable; processing decisions alter the observability of phases and amplitudes. Archive station metadata with the actual waveform interval rather than assuming today’s response applied historically.

Observational method. Compare response-corrected and raw synthetic traces; record the poles, zeros, calibration factor and orientation metadata used. Specify the coordinate frame, observation period, relevant units, acquisition and calibration limitations before interpreting the resulting figure.

Common interpretive trap. The tallest digital count is not necessarily the strongest actual motion when instruments or gains differ. A neat numeric solution does not remove this interpretive ambiguity. Ask which further independent measurement could reveal whether the preferred explanation is incorrect.

Applied investigation. Document a corrected waveform with units and a record of the response-removal procedure. Provide a labeled sketch, a short reproducible table and a paragraph separating observed evidence from inferred process.

§3.4 Sampling rates, Nyquist limits, and aliasing

Learning goals. Explain the mechanism, translate the problem into measurable quantities, test an alternative interpretation and defend an uncertainty statement.

Physical foundation. Sample interval defines the Nyquist frequency, one half the sampling rate. Frequencies above it may alias unless appropriately filtered before sampling, and a high sample rate does not repair a noisy sensor. In the broader unit, Reliable seismology starts with traceable measurement. Digitized amplitudes, timing systems and instrument calibration are not interchangeable; processing decisions alter the observability of phases and amplitudes. Archive station metadata with the actual waveform interval rather than assuming today’s response applied historically.

Observational method. Generate two analytic sinusoids sampled at different rates and compare their discrete waveforms and apparent peaks. Specify the coordinate frame, observation period, relevant units, acquisition and calibration limitations before interpreting the resulting figure.

Common interpretive trap. Interpolating an undersampled waveform can make a smooth but wrong picture; it cannot recover unknown lost frequencies. A neat numeric solution does not remove this interpretive ambiguity. Ask which further independent measurement could reveal whether the preferred explanation is incorrect.

Evidence design and falsification

A strong evidence chain identifies what the observer physically controls. Specify waveform or map provenance, the acquisition time standard, the instrument or field method, the adopted calibration, and an independent comparison. Is the observed pattern repeatable across genuinely separate records or did it arise after multiple views of the same data? Where an observation is transformed into a calculated quantity, retain its original units and values. Write an audit note on which data would be decisive and which are merely compatible. Here, the principal source of ambiguity is stated explicitly: Interpolating an undersampled waveform can make a smooth but wrong picture; it cannot recover unknown lost frequencies. Create a four-row evidence matrix: observed quantity, inferred physical quantity, principal confounder, and proposed falsifying observation. Mark an entry as measured only when acquisition metadata is known. Repeat the matrix for a second hypothesis instead of dismissing it without calculation. This discipline prevents a tidy narrative from substituting for testable physical interpretation. §3.5 Filtering, baselines, and spectral interpretation

Learning goals. Explain the mechanism, translate the problem into measurable quantities, test an alternative interpretation and defend an uncertainty statement.

Physical foundation. Bandpass filtering can improve visibility of selected phases but also distort amplitude, timing and apparent onset, especially with sharp corners or short records. Integration of acceleration magnifies baseline offsets. In the broader unit, Reliable seismology starts with traceable measurement. Digitized amplitudes, timing systems and instrument calibration are not interchangeable; processing decisions alter the observability of phases and amplitudes. Archive station metadata with the actual waveform interval rather than assuming today’s response applied historically.

Observational method. Record raw and filtered synthetic traces together, specify filter order and corner frequencies, and inspect transients around gaps. Specify the coordinate frame, observation period, relevant units, acquisition and calibration limitations before interpreting the resulting figure.

Common interpretive trap. A cleaner picture is not proof of a cleaner measurement; phase shifts and ringing may create false arrivals. §3.8 Unit 3 capstone: Conduct a synthetic instrument changeover audit. Recompute a sample-rate requirement, expose one aliased sinusoid, flag clipped traces and compare a causal with a zero-phase filter. Produce a methods log that a second student could execute without oral instructions.

Research scenario: A fictional broadband station is upgraded during a month of local seismicity; the replacement sensor has different sensitivity, clipping behavior and data gaps. Students must avoid inventing a trend from the hardware change. The goal is to produce a reasoned comparison of at least two defensible models, not to reverse-engineer a predetermined answer. Start by recording each input variable with units, a confidence grade and a plausible range. Name the forward calculation associated with each inference and outline how the inputs enter the formula. Preserve the fabricated dataset in its supplied CSV form and cite its generation method; these records do not correspond to real earthquakes or real sensor networks.

Procedure. First, draw a map or schematic that makes geometric assumptions visible. Second, check station or sample identifiers and the relationship of the observation period to the question. Third, compute baseline statistics and at least one worked relation by hand before using the browser model. Fourth, investigate a competing hypothesis using an independently chosen measurement or a perturbation of a consequential parameter. Fifth, repeat after withholding one doubtful observation. Compare whether the scientific conclusion survives that choice. Trace all signal processing decisions to an observable consequence. If the spectrum changes after response removal, show the original count spectrum alongside the corrected SI spectrum. If a pick moves after filtering, report both times. If the sensor clipped, do not label amplitude restoration as a measurement unless an independent sensor constrains it.

Required outputs. Submit a two-page technical brief with a hypothesis table, raw and corrected values, an equation inventory, an uncertainty budget, a labelled graphic and a short limitations section. Include all model parameters. Do not omit unsuccessful tests and do not represent computer-generated data as measured field observations. Students should record their own explanations rather than copying one another's interpretation. Discuss how different station distributions or sampling choices could cause an apparently significant spatial pattern without a corresponding physical change.

Extensions. Reliable seismology starts with traceable measurement. Digitized amplitudes, timing systems and instrument calibration are not interchangeable; processing decisions alter the observability of phases and amplitudes. Archive station metadata with the actual waveform interval rather than assuming today’s response applied historically. Write an explicit counterfactual: what observation would undermine the main hypothesis? Consider what a larger dataset could resolve and what it would leave uncertain. Finally, design an accessible presentation of the result with a descriptive figure caption, a plain-language explanation of each symbol, and a table of numerical values for readers who cannot interpret a colored graph. Re-evaluate the result against professional source and public-safety boundaries before suggesting a consequential application.

Peer-review rubric. Assign 0–4 points each for correctness of units, explicit assumptions, data provenance, comparison with alternatives, uncertainty treatment, computational reproducibility and ethical communication. A polished figure without units, or a convincing narrative without a falsifiable test, should not receive full credit. Explain in prose why the least certain input affects confidence, and identify at least one result that could change with additional observations.
