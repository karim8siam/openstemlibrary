§5.1 Guiding question

Why do different sites shake differently during the same earthquake?

Fictional unit investigation. A synthetic urban transect crosses bedrock, alluvium and a sedimentary basin. Stations recorded the same distant event but display different frequency content, durations and amplitudes.

§5.2 Learning workflow

Before beginning, distinguish the scientific question, the measured quantity and the latent parameter that the student hopes to infer. Assign physical units, name a plausible alternate hypothesis, and state what data are fictional. For ground motion, source, travel path, near-surface stratigraphy and structural response all matter. An average amplification ratio has frequency and motion-level dependence; do not apply simplified models as design coefficients. The experiments are deliberately simplified so their calculations can be independently checked. They are not validated field instruments, real-time forecasts, or site-specific hazard assessments.

§5.3 Acceleration, velocity, and displacement

Learning goals. Explain the mechanism, translate the problem into measurable quantities, test an alternative interpretation and defend an uncertainty statement.

Physical foundation. Ground motion records may be expressed as acceleration, velocity or displacement and converted by differentiation or integration with careful baseline treatment. Peak values refer to different physical measures. In the broader unit, Source, path, site and engineered system each contribute to observed impact. A magnitude is not a direct measure of local shaking or expected losses. Teaching oscillators are valuable for reasoning about resonance but cannot certify real structures or replace seismic design standards.

Observational method. Compare toy acceleration pulses with different durations; numerically integrate after removing an explicitly defined offset. Specify the coordinate frame, observation period, relevant units, acquisition and calibration limitations before interpreting the resulting figure.

Common interpretive trap. High PGA does not guarantee the greatest permanent damage; duration and structural response change the outcome. A neat numeric solution does not remove this interpretive ambiguity. Ask which further independent measurement could reveal whether the preferred explanation is incorrect.

Applied investigation. Present a table of PGA, PGV and period content with units and preprocessing notes. Provide a labeled sketch, a short reproducible table and a paragraph separating observed evidence from inferred process.

§5.4 Site amplification and resonance

Learning goals. Explain the mechanism, translate the problem into measurable quantities, test an alternative interpretation and defend an uncertainty statement.

Physical foundation. Soft sediments and basins can amplify or prolong motion at some frequencies through impedance contrast, trapped waves and resonance. Local conditions matter alongside event magnitude and distance. In the broader unit, Source, path, site and engineered system each contribute to observed impact. A magnitude is not a direct measure of local shaking or expected losses. Teaching oscillators are valuable for reasoning about resonance but cannot certify real structures or replace seismic design standards.

Observational method. Compare transfer functions from two fictional sites and estimate quarter-wavelength resonance for a uniform surface layer. Specify the coordinate frame, observation period, relevant units, acquisition and calibration limitations before interpreting the resulting figure.

Common interpretive trap. Every soft soil site does not amplify every frequency, and a one-dimensional resonance model can miss basin edges. A neat numeric solution does not remove this interpretive ambiguity. Ask which further independent measurement could reveal whether the preferred explanation is incorrect.

§5.5 Magnitude, distance, and source-to-site path

Learning goals. Explain the mechanism, translate the problem into measurable quantities, test an alternative interpretation and defend an uncertainty statement.

Physical foundation. Ground-motion prediction equations relate motion to magnitude, distance, faulting and site parameters using empirically calibrated models. Their uncertainties include natural variability as well as parameter estimation. In the broader unit, Source, path, site and engineered system each contribute to observed impact. A magnitude is not a direct measure of local shaking or expected losses. Teaching oscillators are valuable for reasoning about resonance but cannot certify real structures or replace seismic design standards.

Observational method. Use a toy attenuation curve for a fixed scenario and compare it with invented station observations at multiple distances. Specify the coordinate frame, observation period, relevant units, acquisition and calibration limitations before interpreting the resulting figure.

Common interpretive trap. A classroom curve is not a building code map or a substitute for local ground-motion models and engineering review. A neat numeric solution does not remove this interpretive ambiguity. Ask which further independent measurement could reveal whether the preferred explanation is incorrect.

Evidence design and falsification

Reconstruct the inference backwards. Start with the headline result and locate every conversion, classification and assumption required to obtain it. An estimate based on a chosen velocity, phase pick or site parameter should be labelled conditional on those inputs. Review the same steps after making one intentional mistake in the input metadata; document which output would become misleading and why. Here, the principal source of ambiguity is stated explicitly: A classroom curve is not a building code map or a substitute for local ground-motion models and engineering review. Method A: compute a baseline result using the stated relation, preserving all units. Method B: perturb one relevant input and record the change. Method C: replace the simplest assumption with an alternative that would be plausible at the study scale; if you cannot recalculate it with the available information, state what is missing. Method D: compare with an independent kind of observation, if available. This order avoids hiding assumptions under additional significant figures. §5.6 Response spectra and structural periods

Learning goals. Explain the mechanism, translate the problem into measurable quantities, test an alternative interpretation and defend an uncertainty statement.

Physical foundation. A response spectrum summarizes the peak response of idealized oscillators at varying natural periods and damping ratios. It is not an earthquake forecast or a unique waveform description. In the broader unit, Source, path, site and engineered system each contribute to observed impact. A magnitude is not a direct measure of local shaking or expected losses. Teaching oscillators are valuable for reasoning about resonance but cannot certify real structures or replace seismic design standards.

Observational method. Compute the natural period of a toy spring-mass system and interpret how changing stiffness shifts the oscillator response. Specify the coordinate frame, observation period, relevant units, acquisition and calibration limitations before interpreting the resulting figure.

Common interpretive trap. A response spectrum does not directly identify damage without structural capacity, ductility and configuration. A neat numeric solution does not remove this interpretive ambiguity. Ask which further independent measurement could reveal whether the preferred explanation is incorrect.

Evidence design and falsification

A strong evidence chain identifies what the observer physically controls. Specify waveform or map provenance, the acquisition time standard, the instrument or field method, the adopted calibration, and an independent comparison. Is the observed pattern repeatable across genuinely separate records or did it arise after multiple views of the same data? Where an observation is transformed into a calculated quantity, retain its original units and values. Write an audit note on which data would be decisive and which are merely compatible. Here, the principal source of ambiguity is stated explicitly: A response spectrum does not directly identify damage without structural capacity, ductility and configuration. Create a four-row evidence matrix: observed quantity, inferred physical quantity, principal confounder, and proposed falsifying observation. Mark an entry as measured only when acquisition metadata is known. Repeat the matrix for a second hypothesis instead of dismissing it without calculation. This discipline prevents a tidy narrative from substituting for testable physical interpretation. §5.8 Unit 5 capstone: Evaluate the same synthetic pulse at bedrock and basin sites. Compute an illustrative resonance frequency and spectral comparison, then list the unmodeled effects such as nonlinear soil behavior, basin geometry, structural degradation and spatially correlated ground motion.

Research scenario: A synthetic urban transect crosses bedrock, alluvium and a sedimentary basin. Stations recorded the same distant event but display different frequency content, durations and amplitudes. The goal is to produce a reasoned comparison of at least two defensible models, not to reverse-engineer a predetermined answer. Start by recording each input variable with units, a confidence grade and a plausible range. Name the forward calculation associated with each inference and outline how the inputs enter the formula. Preserve the fabricated dataset in its supplied CSV form and cite its generation method; these records do not correspond to real earthquakes or real sensor networks.

Procedure. First, draw a map or schematic that makes geometric assumptions visible. Second, check station or sample identifiers and the relationship of the observation period to the question. Third, compute baseline statistics and at least one worked relation by hand before using the browser model. Fourth, investigate a competing hypothesis using an independently chosen measurement or a perturbation of a consequential parameter. Fifth, repeat after withholding one doubtful observation. Compare whether the scientific conclusion survives that choice. The shaking at a point contains contributions from rupture direction, travel distance, attenuation and local stratigraphy. Compare frequency content and duration as well as one peak amplitude. Required outputs. Submit a two-page technical brief with a hypothesis table, raw and corrected values, an equation inventory, an uncertainty budget, a labelled graphic and a short limitations section. Include all model parameters. Do not omit unsuccessful tests and do not represent computer-generated data as measured field observations. Students should record their own explanations rather than copying one another's interpretation. Discuss how different station distributions or sampling choices could cause an apparently significant spatial pattern without a corresponding physical change.

Extensions. Source, path, site and engineered system each contribute to observed impact. A magnitude is not a direct measure of local shaking or expected losses. Teaching oscillators are valuable for reasoning about resonance but cannot certify real structures or replace seismic design standards. Write an explicit counterfactual: what observation would undermine the main hypothesis? Consider what a larger dataset could resolve and what it would leave uncertain. Finally, design an accessible presentation of the result with a descriptive figure caption, a plain-language explanation of each symbol, and a table of numerical values for readers who cannot interpret a colored graph. Re-evaluate the result against professional source and public-safety boundaries before suggesting a consequential application.

Peer-review rubric. Assign 0–4 points each for correctness of units, explicit assumptions, data provenance, comparison with alternatives, uncertainty treatment, computational reproducibility and ethical communication. A polished figure without units, or a convincing narrative without a falsifiable test, should not receive full credit. Explain in prose why the least certain input affects confidence, and identify at least one result that could change with additional observations.

  • Robert, C.P., & Casella, G. (2004). Monte Carlo Statistical Methods (2nd ed.): Definitive theoretical treatise on rejection sampling, Markov chain Monte Carlo, Metropolis-Hastings algorithms, and Gibbs sampling
  • Ripley, B.D. (1987). Stochastic Simulation: Foundational text on pseudorandom number generators, spatial simulation, and variate generation algorithms
  • Efron, B., & Tibshirani, R.J. (1993). An Introduction to the Bootstrap: Canonical reference for bootstrap standard errors, percentile confidence intervals, BCa intervals, and jackknife methods
  • Gentle, J.E. (2003). Random Number Generation and Monte Carlo Methods: Authoritative manual on uniform generation tests, spectral lattice tests, and variance reduction techniques
