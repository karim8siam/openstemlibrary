Earthquake Location, Depth, and Magnitude

Earthquake locations are inverse estimates obtained through physical models, arrival times and network geometry. The best-fitting coordinates may not be unique, while reported magnitude scales address different observables. A complete solution states phase picks, velocity assumptions, error model and station coverage.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Seismology study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§4.1 Guiding question

How can station recordings constrain an event in space, time and source size?

Fictional unit investigation. A fictional network surrounds a small event cluster, but two stations have uncertain clocks and one station lies far outside the network. Students compare a best-fit solution with uncertainty and network geometry.

§4.2 Learning workflow

Before beginning, distinguish the scientific question, the measured quantity and the latent parameter that the student hopes to infer. Assign physical units, name a plausible alternate hypothesis, and state what data are fictional. For locating an earthquake, a small residual is not proof of correct origin time or depth. P and S phase association, azimuthal coverage and velocity-model uncertainty can dominate. The experiments are deliberately simplified so their calculations can be independently checked. They are not validated field instruments, real-time forecasts, or site-specific hazard assessments.

§4.3 Arrival-time picking and phase association

Learning goals. Explain the mechanism, translate the problem into measurable quantities, test an alternative interpretation and defend an uncertainty statement.

Physical foundation. Picking P and S onsets converts waveforms to measured arrival times. Association groups related arrivals into candidate events, but low signal-to-noise ratios and overlapping earthquakes can make assignments ambiguous. In the broader unit, Earthquake locations are inverse estimates obtained through physical models, arrival times and network geometry. The best-fitting coordinates may not be unique, while reported magnitude scales address different observables. A complete solution states phase picks, velocity assumptions, error model and station coverage.

Observational method. Label synthetic arrival traces with picks and uncertainty windows; test whether alternate association tables are feasible. Specify the coordinate frame, observation period, relevant units, acquisition and calibration limitations before interpreting the resulting figure.

Common interpretive trap. The first visually prominent wiggle need not be the direct P onset; impulsive noise can bias an event location. A neat numeric solution does not remove this interpretive ambiguity. Ask which further independent measurement could reveal whether the preferred explanation is incorrect.

Applied investigation. Build a pick table including onset quality, phase code and absolute UTC time. Provide a labeled sketch, a short reproducible table and a paragraph separating observed evidence from inferred process.

§4.4 Epicenters using differential arrival times

Learning goals. Explain the mechanism, translate the problem into measurable quantities, test an alternative interpretation and defend an uncertainty statement.

Physical foundation. P–S separation constrains distance to a station in simple velocity models. Multiple ranges or absolute travel times are used for localization; geometry and model error determine ambiguities. In the broader unit, Earthquake locations are inverse estimates obtained through physical models, arrival times and network geometry. The best-fitting coordinates may not be unique, while reported magnitude scales address different observables. Formal covariance can understate systematic velocity-model error. In the broader unit, Earthquake locations are inverse estimates obtained through physical models, arrival times and network geometry. §4.6 Magnitude scales and seismic moment

Learning goals. Explain the mechanism, translate the problem into measurable quantities, test an alternative interpretation and defend an uncertainty statement.

Physical foundation. Local magnitude ML, body-wave mb, surface-wave Ms and moment magnitude Mw use different measurement windows and can saturate or be inappropriate across ranges. Mw connects logarithmically to scalar seismic moment. In the broader unit, Earthquake locations are inverse estimates obtained through physical models, arrival times and network geometry. The best-fitting coordinates may not be unique, while reported magnitude scales address different observables. A complete solution states phase picks, velocity assumptions, error model and station coverage.

Observational method. Convert synthetic M0 to Mw using a declared SI relation; compare it with a conceptual local-magnitude estimate. Specify the coordinate frame, observation period, relevant units, acquisition and calibration limitations before interpreting the resulting figure.

Common interpretive trap. A magnitude change of one unit is not a change of one joule, and intensity differs across sites for one earthquake. A neat numeric solution does not remove this interpretive ambiguity. Ask which further independent measurement could reveal whether the preferred explanation is incorrect.
