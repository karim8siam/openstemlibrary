Unit 3 — Fold geometry and histories

Recognize and measure folded strata without assuming a unique cause. Use structural observations and reproducible geometric tests to evaluate competing interpretations.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Structural Geology study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§3.1 Central question

Recognize and measure folded strata without assuming a unique cause. Use structural observations and reproducible geometric tests to evaluate competing interpretations.

§3.2 Extended unit perspective

Folds are not merely a series of anticline and syncline symbols on a map. A fold has a geometry in three dimensions and a history that may vary along its hinge. It can be upright or overturned, gently or tightly closed, cylindrical or changing plunge. Recognition of bed younging is essential when deciding whether the older rocks occur in the centre of a structure. In a complete structural report, record the orientation of bedding, facing, cleavage and lineation separately, then sketch how each would be observed on the two limbs. Remember that a map trace can look like a closure simply because the contact intersects topography. Likewise, an asymmetric fold may indicate vergence but not by itself give an unambiguous stress axis. Mechanics enter through competence contrasts and deformation partitioning: buckle folds, flexural-slip folds and passive folds do not preserve bed thickness and internal markers in the same way. Apparent bed-thickness changes should be checked using measurements perpendicular to layering rather than along a conveniently exposed surface. Where a second cleavage cuts an earlier fold, relative timing matters more than a textbook classification of interference shape. The unit's mathematical problems are small pieces of an overall testable cross-section; their purpose is to flag inconsistent geometry rather than reconstruct a unique regional history.

Field and scientific objectives

Describe relevant structural objects with location and orientation, translate them between map and section, construct a physically and chronologically consistent interpretation, and test at least one alternative. The aim is to communicate exactly which information is observed, which result follows by geometric calculation and which regional mechanism is inferred. Avoid interpreting one structural indicator as proof of a single tectonic event where earlier deformation, reactivation or anisotropic rock properties remain plausible.

§3.3 Fold limbs, hinges and axial surfaces

Mechanism and observational scale

Folds bend formerly planar layering; hinge zones are regions of greatest curvature, limbs are flanks and axial surfaces connect hinge lines. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Younging indicators, facing data, fold wavelength, interlimb angles and hinge traces help classify geometry. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with stratigraphic younging, projected section azimuth and hinge-axis continuity and illustrate mapped limbs, possible interference and inferred hinge geometry. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

An anticline need not have older rocks in the centre if the fold is overturned or the exposed units are repeated by faults. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Construct a cross-section from multiple bedding attitudes, face-up criteria and mapped contacts, then check whether the restored stratigraphy is internally consistent. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For fold limbs, hinges and axial surfaces, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

Limitation, cross-check and synthesis

Incomplete exposure and erosion can hide the true closure of a fold. Consider the alternative that changes the interpretation most while retaining the measured observations. Make a two-column field record. Put verifiable geometric and material facts in the left column and causal or historical interpretation in the right. For each interpreted event, cite the specific measured feature and its location. An interpretation without such a link is a hypothesis to test rather than a supported result.

Describe fold geometry first; infer anticline or syncline stratigraphy only with reliable age order. A defensible conclusion therefore has five components: observed feature, ideal relation used, leading interpretation, decisive contrary test and residual uncertainty. Indicate whether each is local to the measured exposure or can legitimately be extended across the mapped domain.

§3.4 Fold classification and three-dimensional variation

Mechanism and observational scale

Upright, inclined, recumbent, plunging and noncylindrical folds are geometric descriptions sensitive to the chosen reference plane and line. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Map closures, hinge line trajectories and interlimb angles distinguish simple cylindrical cartoons from variable real folds. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with stratigraphic younging, projected section azimuth and hinge-axis continuity and illustrate mapped limbs, possible interference and inferred hinge geometry. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

A map closure can also be produced by a dome, basin, topographic relief or fault-bounded repetition. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Compare a fold-axis prediction from poles to bedding with independently measured hinge orientations along strike. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. §3.5 Layer-parallel shortening and flexural mechanisms

Mechanism and observational scale

Layers may fold through flexural slip, flexural flow, passive shear or buckling, with differing thickness and fabric consequences. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Slip on bedding surfaces, hinge-zone cleavage, bed thickness changes and competency contrasts can constrain mechanisms. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with stratigraphic younging, projected section azimuth and hinge-axis continuity and illustrate mapped limbs, possible interference and inferred hinge geometry. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

A thick hinge relative to a limb can reflect original thickness changes or later erosion, not only a particular folding mechanism. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Measure thickness normal to bedding at hinge and limb, inspect bedding-parallel slip indicators, and compare with an ideal constant-thickness geometry. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For layer-parallel shortening and flexural mechanisms, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

§3.6 Cleavage, axial-planar fabric and facing

Mechanism and observational scale

Slaty cleavage or spaced cleavage may develop during shortening and is often, but not universally, near axial-planar to folds. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

The cleavage–bedding intersection line can align with fold hinges; cleavage refraction across lithologic boundaries records rheological contrast. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with stratigraphic younging, projected section azimuth and hinge-axis continuity and illustrate mapped limbs, possible interference and inferred hinge geometry. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

Cleavage can form in a later event and cut an older fold, yielding misleading single-event interpretations. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Map cleavage and bedding intersection directions on both limbs and examine thin sections for cross-cutting generation relationships. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For cleavage, axial-planar fabric and facing, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

§3.7 Interference patterns and polyphase folding

Mechanism and observational scale

Successive deformation events can refold earlier folds and fabrics, producing dome-and-basin, mushroom or complex interference geometries. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Curved hinges, cross-cutting cleavage sets and refolded earlier lineations indicate overprinting where preserved clearly. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with stratigraphic younging, projected section azimuth and hinge-axis continuity and illustrate mapped limbs, possible interference and inferred hinge geometry. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

Variably plunging single-phase folds can resemble a textbook interference pattern in limited outcrop. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Trace one early marker through three dimensions, identify which fabric cuts another, and build alternative sequential geometries. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For interference patterns and polyphase folding, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

Limitation, cross-check and synthesis

Erosion and incomplete structural domain mapping can mimic complex interference. Consider the alternative that changes the interpretation most while retaining the measured observations. List which surfaces or fabrics predate which others using direct cut, fold, overprint or growth relations. Leave pairs unordered where no observation establishes priority. Reactivation means a single surface may occur more than once in the event graph.

Require chronological evidence, not shape alone, to establish multiple folding episodes. A defensible conclusion therefore has five components: observed feature, ideal relation used, leading interpretation, decisive contrary test and residual uncertainty. Indicate whether each is local to the measured exposure or can legitimately be extended across the mapped domain.

§3.8 Integrated investigation 3 — A disputed fold interpretation

A fictitious map shows two closure-like traces, one cleavage set and sparse facing observations. Construct both a single plunging-fold interpretation and a two-phase refolding interpretation. For each model predict bedding attitudes on two yet-unmapped ridge faces. Restore a marker bed without artificially changing its thickness. Specify what evidence would establish younger rock in a fold core. Write a short field-report note explaining why cleavage orientation alone is not proof of one shortening direction.

Analysis protocol and deliverables

The following integrated investigation uses fictional measurements expressly manufactured to demonstrate methods. It is not a published field survey or a substitute for collecting data. Begin with original measurements and metadata; present interpreted structures in separate layers. Compute at least one simple quantity with its units, make two competing predictions, and decide which additional piece of evidence would be most informative. Report any failure of the data to distinguish models. Submit a map or diagram, annotated calculations, a brief uncertainty table and a paragraph explaining whether the result has any practical limitations. The first panel of the submitted figure should contain observations only. The second may show an inferred geometry, but include a legend differentiating solid observation lines from dashed extrapolations. The third should compare an alternative model and identify the measurement that would select between them. A model should be judged against the data it did not use for fitting whenever possible.

Interpreting the synthetic dataset

Open datasets/unit-03.csv from the offline reader. All station names, orientations, distances and categories in this CSV are fictional. Plot the values and report which trend, if any, is robust against removal of two observations. Where the data do not contain enough directional or temporal context to support a tectonic claim, write that limitation explicitly. Do not claim a physical trend is established just because a graphing tool draws a smooth line.

Independent review

Exchange interpretations with another student. Ask them to identify one term or symbol whose meaning is unclear, one unstated convention and one observation that could disprove the preferred reconstruction. Revise the final diagram to make these items visible. Grade the work on measurement transparency and falsifiability, not on whether every student selected the same visually attractive fault or fold model.
