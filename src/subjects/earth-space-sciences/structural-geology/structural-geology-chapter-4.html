Unit 4 — Brittle faults and fault-zone architecture

Relate fault surfaces and markers to slip, damage and reactivation. Use structural observations and reproducible geometric tests to evaluate competing interpretations.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university structural geology courses. Eight units with five solved questions per unit and 16 interactive simulations illustrating Mohr circle shear stress, fault dip slip components, fold interlimb angles, strain ellipse axial ratios, and lithostatic pressure depth relations. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§4.1 Central question

Relate fault surfaces and markers to slip, damage and reactivation. Use structural observations and reproducible geometric tests to evaluate competing interpretations.

§4.2 Extended unit perspective

A fault is not identified solely by a crack. The essential distinction is relative displacement across a surface or distributed zone. In field work this requires matched markers and a specified reference direction. Apparent stratigraphic separation observed in an oblique section can differ greatly from the actual slip vector. A rake measured on a fault plane can resolve a net slip into strike-parallel and dip-parallel components, but polished lineations may record just one late episode of movement. Faults also have thickness and internal anatomy: a gouge-rich core can be bordered by a damage zone with variable fracture intensity, permeability and mechanical properties. Some old fault zones are reactivated under new stress conditions and record contradictory slip indicators unless event timing is considered. Simple cartoons of normal and reverse movement aid terminology, but natural faults commonly curve with depth or branch into multiple strands. Slip estimates must therefore distinguish measured offset, geological separation and the amount restored under a model. The best restoration is one that satisfies multiple independent marker matches and stratigraphic ordering, not merely a visually smooth cross-section. Class exercises here use intentionally simple planar geometry; they are not seismic-hazard maps and cannot be used to estimate contemporary operational fault activity.

Field and scientific objectives

Describe relevant structural objects with location and orientation, translate them between map and section, construct a physically and chronologically consistent interpretation, and test at least one alternative. The aim is to communicate exactly which information is observed, which result follows by geometric calculation and which regional mechanism is inferred. Avoid interpreting one structural indicator as proof of a single tectonic event where earlier deformation, reactivation or anisotropic rock properties remain plausible.

§4.3 Normal, reverse and thrust faults

Mechanism and observational scale

The relative displacement of hanging wall and footwall along a dipping fault plane distinguishes normal from reverse separation when true slip is constrained. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Cutoff lines, drag folds, fault-bend geometry and matched marker units reveal displacement across a fault. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with rake convention, fault plane orientation and reliability of each piercing marker and illustrate measured separation, decomposed slip and restoration choice. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

Apparent stratigraphic omission can also result from an unconformity, and repetition can reflect folding rather than thrusting. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Map both sides, estimate three-dimensional slip direction, and reconstruct marker continuity under explicit hanging-wall geometry. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For normal, reverse and thrust faults, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

Limitation, cross-check and synthesis

Fault dip may vary at depth and growth strata may record several phases of motion. Consider the alternative that changes the interpretation most while retaining the measured observations. Make a two-column field record. Put verifiable geometric and material facts in the left column and causal or historical interpretation in the right. For each interpreted event, cite the specific measured feature and its location. An interpretation without such a link is a hypothesis to test rather than a supported result.

Use multiple offset markers to determine actual movement instead of naming a fault from one section. A defensible conclusion therefore has five components: observed feature, ideal relation used, leading interpretation, decisive contrary test and residual uncertainty. Indicate whether each is local to the measured exposure or can legitimately be extended across the mapped domain.

  5. Write a short final paragraph reporting what remains uncertain and which measurement would reduce the uncertainty.

§4.4 Strike-slip fault systems and stepovers

Mechanism and observational scale

Lateral slip occurs mainly along the strike of a near-vertical plane, with releasing or restraining bends arising from the combination of sense and bend geometry. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Offset streams, piercing points, en echelon folds and pull-apart basins constrain kinematics in well-exposed systems. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with rake convention, fault plane orientation and reliability of each piercing marker and illustrate measured separation, decomposed slip and restoration choice. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

A river bend may predate a fault, and a single offset channel can be abandoned or modified by erosion. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Compare the lateral shift of two independent pre-slip piercing features and evaluate whether inferred slip agrees in magnitude and sign. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For strike-slip fault systems and stepovers, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

§4.5 Rake, slickensides and oblique slip

Mechanism and observational scale

Rake is an angle measured within a plane relative to a reference direction, allowing a measured net slip vector to be decomposed into strike and dip components. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Polished steps, mineral fibres and offset lineations can combine to constrain slip direction and, sometimes, sense. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with rake convention, fault plane orientation and reliability of each piercing marker and illustrate measured separation, decomposed slip and restoration choice. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

Slickenside grooves can record the most recent slip rather than the total displacement of an older fault. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Compute strike-parallel and dip-parallel components from a known slip and rake, then compare with independently restored marker offsets. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For rake, slickensides and oblique slip, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

Limitation, cross-check and synthesis

Rake conventions and lineation polarity must be specified; unknown polarity prevents a unique sense determination. Identify the least certain input, vary it by a physically plausible amount, and show its effect on the computed result. Variation in a trigonometric angle may produce a nonlinear uncertainty even where angle readings appear precise. Report significant figures in proportion to the instrument and assumptions.

Reconstruct oblique slip with component vectors and uncertainty rather than a single apparent offset. §4.6 Fault rocks, gouge and damage zones

Mechanism and observational scale

Slip may localize in cataclasite, gouge or foliated fault rock while fracture density and permeability vary through a wider damage zone. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Clast angularity, grain size, vein cutting relations and localized shear fabrics record fault-zone processes. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with rake convention, fault plane orientation and reliability of each piercing marker and illustrate measured separation, decomposed slip and restoration choice. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

A broad fractured zone may be a joint corridor or lithologic contrast, not necessarily an active fault core. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Measure fracture intensity transects across an interpreted core and document lithology and weathering controls. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For fault rocks, gouge and damage zones, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

Limitation, cross-check and synthesis

Weathering and exposure bias can overstate damage width or obscure distributed ductile shear. Consider the alternative that changes the interpretation most while retaining the measured observations. Translate the interpretation between a map, a cross-section and a three-dimensional conceptual diagram. Check that all three representations show compatible orientations and movement directions. A model that works in one section but fails in map view needs revision.

Fault-zone classification must integrate microstructures, spatial patterns and mapped displacement. A defensible conclusion therefore has five components: observed feature, ideal relation used, leading interpretation, decisive contrary test and residual uncertainty. Indicate whether each is local to the measured exposure or can legitimately be extended across the mapped domain.

§4.7 Fault growth, linkage and restoration

Mechanism and observational scale

Fault segments grow, interact and can link, changing displacement distributions through time; balanced restoration enforces geometry and material conservation assumptions. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Displacement–distance profiles, relay ramps, growth strata and fault tip geometries test histories of segment propagation. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with rake convention, fault plane orientation and reliability of each piercing marker and illustrate measured separation, decomposed slip and restoration choice. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

A displacement low may reflect missing data or an unrecognized cross fault rather than a genuine relay segment. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Trace slip markers at several elevations, restore sequential cross-sections and note where bed-length balance fails. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For fault growth, linkage and restoration, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

Limitation, cross-check and synthesis

Restoration assumes specific deformation mechanisms and may be nonunique even when perfectly balanced. Consider the alternative that changes the interpretation most while retaining the measured observations. List which surfaces or fabrics predate which others using direct cut, fold, overprint or growth relations. Leave pairs unordered where no observation establishes priority. Reactivation means a single surface may occur more than once in the event graph.

Treat balancing as a necessary geometric check under assumptions, not proof of one tectonic history. A defensible conclusion therefore has five components: observed feature, ideal relation used, leading interpretation, decisive contrary test and residual uncertainty. Indicate whether each is local to the measured exposure or can legitimately be extended across the mapped domain.

§4.8 Integrated investigation 4 — Restoring three faulted horizons

Three invented stratigraphic contacts cross two steeply dipping fault zones. Calculate ideal throw and heave in a section perpendicular to one fault, resolve oblique net slip into strike- and dip-parallel components and state the assumptions for both. Draw at least two restorable cross-sections that honour marker continuity. Show how one interpretation fails when a fourth independent piercing point is revealed. Document the distinction between fault separation, offset and net slip.

Analysis protocol and deliverables

The following integrated investigation uses fictional measurements expressly manufactured to demonstrate methods. It is not a published field survey or a substitute for collecting data. Begin with original measurements and metadata; present interpreted structures in separate layers. Compute at least one simple quantity with its units, make two competing predictions, and decide which additional piece of evidence would be most informative. Report any failure of the data to distinguish models. Submit a map or diagram, annotated calculations, a brief uncertainty table and a paragraph explaining whether the result has any practical limitations. The first panel of the submitted figure should contain observations only. The second may show an inferred geometry, but include a legend differentiating solid observation lines from dashed extrapolations. The third should compare an alternative model and identify the measurement that would select between them. A model should be judged against the data it did not use for fitting whenever possible.

Interpreting the synthetic dataset

Open datasets/unit-04.csv from the offline reader. All station names, orientations, distances and categories in this CSV are fictional. Plot the values and report which trend, if any, is robust against removal of two observations. Where the data do not contain enough directional or temporal context to support a tectonic claim, write that limitation explicitly. Do not claim a physical trend is established just because a graphing tool draws a smooth line.

Independent review

Exchange interpretations with another student. Ask them to identify one term or symbol whose meaning is unclear, one unstated convention and one observation that could disprove the preferred reconstruction. Revise the final diagram to make these items visible. Grade the work on measurement transparency and falsifiability, not on whether every student selected the same visually attractive fault or fold model.
