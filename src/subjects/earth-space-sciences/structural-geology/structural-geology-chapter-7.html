Unit 7 — Tectonic reconstruction and field synthesis

Combine structural observations into falsifiable regional histories. Use structural observations and reproducible geometric tests to evaluate competing interpretations.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university structural geology courses. Eight units with five solved questions per unit and 16 interactive simulations illustrating Mohr circle shear stress, fault dip slip components, fold interlimb angles, strain ellipse axial ratios, and lithostatic pressure depth relations. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§7.1 Central question

Combine structural observations into falsifiable regional histories. Use structural observations and reproducible geometric tests to evaluate competing interpretations.

§7.2 Extended unit perspective

Regional structural reconstruction links geometrical measurements into a chronological sequence. This often means making two cross-sections that honour the same sparse observations but differ in their interpretation of the hidden subsurface. An apparently balanced section can still be wrong if its pin line is incorrect, if stratigraphic thickness varies laterally or if a detachment is placed for convenience rather than evidence. Similarly, extension across a rift cannot automatically be computed from the sum of apparent throws on one section. The geometry must be compatible with the strike of faults, plunge of markers, rotation of blocks and the selected restoration method. In contractional settings, fault-bend and fault-propagation folds predict different hinge histories; growth strata may discriminate them if deposition is dated. In strike-slip settings, transpression and transtension can produce complicated mixtures of folds and faults even where the overall displacement is lateral. The scientific objective is not to select the most visually persuasive tectonic picture, but to state predictions before consulting independent observations. An effective regional study contains a data inventory, scale, explicit uncertainty range, alternative geometry and criteria by which a new measurement would overturn the model.

Field and scientific objectives

Describe relevant structural objects with location and orientation, translate them between map and section, construct a physically and chronologically consistent interpretation, and test at least one alternative. The aim is to communicate exactly which information is observed, which result follows by geometric calculation and which regional mechanism is inferred. Avoid interpreting one structural indicator as proof of a single tectonic event where earlier deformation, reactivation or anisotropic rock properties remain plausible.

§7.3 Cross-section construction and balancing

Mechanism and observational scale

An admissible cross-section respects contact geometry and stratigraphic order; length and area balancing depend on assumptions about deformation and compaction. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Surface attitudes, well control, seismic picks and repeatable stratigraphic thickness estimates anchor a section. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with pin-line assumptions, section azimuth and independent subsurface constraints and illustrate unrestored section, restored section and held-out prediction. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

A plausible balanced geometry may still be wrong if unseen faults or layer-thickness changes exist. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Restore beds using stated pin lines and detachment assumptions, then compare predicted subsurface intersections against held-out constraints. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For cross-section construction and balancing, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

Limitation, cross-check and synthesis

Cross-section balancing does not uniquely determine fault geometry or slip history. Consider the alternative that changes the interpretation most while retaining the measured observations. Make a two-column field record. Put verifiable geometric and material facts in the left column and causal or historical interpretation in the right. For each interpreted event, cite the specific measured feature and its location. An interpretation without such a link is a hypothesis to test rather than a supported result.

Present restored and unrestored sections together with explicit limitations. A defensible conclusion therefore has five components: observed feature, ideal relation used, leading interpretation, decisive contrary test and residual uncertainty. Indicate whether each is local to the measured exposure or can legitimately be extended across the mapped domain.

§7.4 Fault-fold relationships in contractional belts

Mechanism and observational scale

Fault-bend folding, fault-propagation folding and detachment folding generate different patterns of hinge migration, thickness and vergence. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Ramp-flat geometry, cutoffs, growth strata and cleavage relations help distinguish mechanisms at multiple scales. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with pin-line assumptions, section azimuth and independent subsurface constraints and illustrate unrestored section, restored section and held-out prediction. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

A fault-related fold may overprint an earlier buckle fold, complicating simple forward models. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Construct two forward kinematic cartoons and predict contrasting limb-dip changes across a mapped transect. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For fault-fold relationships in contractional belts, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

Limitation, cross-check and synthesis

Propagation-to-slip ratios vary and subsurface detachments are rarely fully exposed. Consider the alternative that changes the interpretation most while retaining the measured observations. Write two incompatible predictions before inspecting another observation. Make one prediction spatial, such as a contact location or expected change in orientation, and the other material, such as fault rock or crosscutting microstructure. Explain which result could falsify the leading model.

Rank competing models by their independent predictions, not by visual resemblance to an ideal cartoon. §7.5 Extensional basin architecture

Mechanism and observational scale

Normal fault displacement and rotation create tilted blocks, accommodation variation and stratigraphic wedges within rifts. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Growth strata thickening toward an active fault, onlap surfaces and footwall uplift patterns support syn-depositional movement. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with pin-line assumptions, section azimuth and independent subsurface constraints and illustrate unrestored section, restored section and held-out prediction. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

Differential compaction or sediment supply changes can also create thickness asymmetry without active faulting. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Measure wedge thickness and dated horizons along transects and restore rotation separately from displacement. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For extensional basin architecture, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

§7.6 Transpressional and transtensional systems

Mechanism and observational scale

Oblique convergence or divergence with lateral shear partitions strain into strike-slip faults, reverse or normal components and distributed folds. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Flower structures, en echelon ridges, offset markers and subsiding pull-apart elements together constrain mixed kinematics. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with pin-line assumptions, section azimuth and independent subsurface constraints and illustrate unrestored section, restored section and held-out prediction. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

An apparent flower structure could be a set of unrelated faults shown in an oblique seismic section. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Build a three-dimensional block diagram and compare predicted orientations of folds, strike-slip faults and extension fractures. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For transpressional and transtensional systems, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

Limitation, cross-check and synthesis

Local stress rotates around bends and inherited anisotropy, so no one universal map pattern applies. Consider the alternative that changes the interpretation most while retaining the measured observations. Translate the interpretation between a map, a cross-section and a three-dimensional conceptual diagram. Check that all three representations show compatible orientations and movement directions. A model that works in one section but fails in map view needs revision.

Match multiple independent structural families and the timing of their activity. A defensible conclusion therefore has five components: observed feature, ideal relation used, leading interpretation, decisive contrary test and residual uncertainty. Indicate whether each is local to the measured exposure or can legitimately be extended across the mapped domain.

§7.7 Structural uncertainty, hazards and responsible reporting

Mechanism and observational scale

Geologic interpretations are inferences constrained by observations; mapping accuracy and uncertainty affect hazard and subsurface decisions. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Competing cross sections, model residuals, paleoseismic evidence and instrument limitations establish confidence in different claims. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with pin-line assumptions, section azimuth and independent subsurface constraints and illustrate unrestored section, restored section and held-out prediction. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

A precise-looking map can still be inaccurate because its data are sparse, selected or from incompatible times. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Withhold one independent observation, predict it under each model, update the interpretation and document remaining ambiguity. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For structural uncertainty, hazards and responsible reporting, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

Limitation, cross-check and synthesis

Classroom displacement-rate and strength models are never adequate for operational earthquake, tunnel or slope-safety decisions. Consider the alternative that changes the interpretation most while retaining the measured observations. List which surfaces or fabrics predate which others using direct cut, fold, overprint or growth relations. Leave pairs unordered where no observation establishes priority. Reactivation means a single surface may occur more than once in the event graph.

Report assumptions, reproducibility, alternatives and uncertainty before recommending an application. A defensible conclusion therefore has five components: observed feature, ideal relation used, leading interpretation, decisive contrary test and residual uncertainty. Indicate whether each is local to the measured exposure or can legitimately be extended across the mapped domain.

§7.8 Integrated investigation 7 — Testing a balanced mountain section

Given an imaginary stratigraphic panel, topographic profile and three dips, build one thrust-belt cross-section with a ramp-flat fault and another with a detachment fold. Restore both while preserving stratigraphic thickness where assumed. Calculate a length residual and investigate whether it reflects geometry, compaction or an unrecognized hidden fault. Identify two forward predictions for a newly drilled well. Include a figure key differentiating directly measured from extrapolated surfaces.

Analysis protocol and deliverables

The following integrated investigation uses fictional measurements expressly manufactured to demonstrate methods. It is not a published field survey or a substitute for collecting data. Begin with original measurements and metadata; present interpreted structures in separate layers. Compute at least one simple quantity with its units, make two competing predictions, and decide which additional piece of evidence would be most informative. Report any failure of the data to distinguish models. Submit a map or diagram, annotated calculations, a brief uncertainty table and a paragraph explaining whether the result has any practical limitations. The first panel of the submitted figure should contain observations only. The second may show an inferred geometry, but include a legend differentiating solid observation lines from dashed extrapolations. The third should compare an alternative model and identify the measurement that would select between them. A model should be judged against the data it did not use for fitting whenever possible.

Interpreting the synthetic dataset

Open datasets/unit-07.csv from the offline reader. All station names, orientations, distances and categories in this CSV are fictional. Plot the values and report which trend, if any, is robust against removal of two observations. Where the data do not contain enough directional or temporal context to support a tectonic claim, write that limitation explicitly. Do not claim a physical trend is established just because a graphing tool draws a smooth line.

Independent review

Exchange interpretations with another student. Ask them to identify one term or symbol whose meaning is unclear, one unstated convention and one observation that could disprove the preferred reconstruction. Revise the final diagram to make these items visible. Grade the work on measurement transparency and falsifiability, not on whether every student selected the same visually attractive fault or fold model.
