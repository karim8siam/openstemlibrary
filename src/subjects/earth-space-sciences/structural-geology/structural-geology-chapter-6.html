Unit 6 — Ductile structures, shear zones and fabrics

Recognize finite deformation in distributed, often heterogeneous zones. Use structural observations and reproducible geometric tests to evaluate competing interpretations.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university structural geology courses. Eight units with five solved questions per unit and 16 interactive simulations illustrating Mohr circle shear stress, fault dip slip components, fold interlimb angles, strain ellipse axial ratios, and lithostatic pressure depth relations. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§6.1 Central question

Recognize finite deformation in distributed, often heterogeneous zones. Use structural observations and reproducible geometric tests to evaluate competing interpretations.

§6.2 Extended unit perspective

A ductile shear zone is a volume in which deformation is distributed rather than a perfectly thin sliding interface. Macroscopic foliations and lineations become much more powerful when coupled with thin-section observations and strain markers measured along a traverse. A crystallographic or grain-size fabric can indicate particular mechanisms, but not one precise deformation temperature without calibration and mineral-specific constraints. Shear sense is commonly inferred using asymmetric objects such as porphyroclast tails or shear bands. Such indicators depend on the plane of section, timing of mineral growth and any later overprinting; students should draw the viewing direction and make sure independent indicators agree. Competency contrasts produce boudinage and variable stretching across adjacent layers, while the matrix can accommodate deformation that the stronger layer does not. Pure shear and simple shear are useful end-members of flow models, yet many real rocks undergo mixed kinematics and successive changes of strain path. Even a perfectly determined finite strain ellipse cannot uniquely reconstruct its evolution. To explain a shear zone credibly, combine grain-scale mechanisms, field-scale geometry, regional movement constraints and an explicit statement of nonuniqueness.

Field and scientific objectives

Describe relevant structural objects with location and orientation, translate them between map and section, construct a physically and chronologically consistent interpretation, and test at least one alternative. The aim is to communicate exactly which information is observed, which result follows by geometric calculation and which regional mechanism is inferred. Avoid interpreting one structural indicator as proof of a single tectonic event where earlier deformation, reactivation or anisotropic rock properties remain plausible.

§6.3 Lesson 6.1 — Ductile shear zones and strain gradients

Mechanism and observational scale

Crystal plasticity, pressure solution and grain-boundary processes can distribute deformation across a shear zone at elevated temperature or suitable strain rate. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Grain-size gradients, stretching lineation, foliated margins and asymmetric porphyroclasts map spatial strain heterogeneity. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with orientation of the X–Z section, grain-scale mechanism and strain partitioning and illustrate fabric asymmetry, finite strain and deformation phase. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

Inherited compositional layering can produce an apparent gradient without a shear-zone centre. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Measure fabric intensity and oriented marker distortion across a perpendicular transect with uniform sample spacing. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For ductile shear zones and strain gradients, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

Limitation, cross-check and synthesis

Strain localization and later annealing complicate point estimates and comparisons among minerals. Consider the alternative that changes the interpretation most while retaining the measured observations. Make a two-column field record. Put verifiable geometric and material facts in the left column and causal or historical interpretation in the right. For each interpreted event, cite the specific measured feature and its location. An interpretation without such a link is a hypothesis to test rather than a supported result.

A shear zone is a volume with a deformation history, not just one planar discontinuity. A defensible conclusion therefore has five components: observed feature, ideal relation used, leading interpretation, decisive contrary test and residual uncertainty. Indicate whether each is local to the measured exposure or can legitimately be extended across the mapped domain.

§6.4 Lesson 6.2 — S–C fabrics and shear-sense indicators

Mechanism and observational scale

Planar foliations, C surfaces and oblique S fabrics can indicate relative shear sense when viewed in the correct section and with timing constrained. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Porphyroclast tails, mica fish and shear bands provide multiple indicators that should agree in oriented sections. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with orientation of the X–Z section, grain-scale mechanism and strain partitioning and illustrate fabric asymmetry, finite strain and deformation phase. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

Apparent asymmetric objects can reverse sense depending on observation plane or superposed shearing. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Collect oriented samples and compare at least three independent indicators in sections perpendicular to foliation and parallel to lineation. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For s–c fabrics and shear-sense indicators, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

§6.5 Lesson 6.3 — Boudinage and competence contrasts

Mechanism and observational scale

Competent layers embedded in a weaker matrix may segment during layer-parallel extension, producing necks, pinch-and-swell or separated blocks. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Boudin aspect ratios, neck fractures and matrix flow geometry constrain rheological contrast and finite strain. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with orientation of the X–Z section, grain-scale mechanism and strain partitioning and illustrate fabric asymmetry, finite strain and deformation phase. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

Primary sedimentary pinch-outs or magmatic layering may imitate boudin outlines without tectonic extension. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Trace one competent layer along strike and search for complementary neck structures in the matrix. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For boudinage and competence contrasts, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

Original layer thickness and three-dimensional block shapes are commonly unknown. Consider the alternative that changes the interpretation most while retaining the measured observations. §6.6 Lesson 6.4 — Mylonites and recrystallization mechanisms

Mechanism and observational scale

Dynamic recrystallization and grain-shape preferred orientation can produce fine-grained foliated mylonites in ductile shear zones. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Quartz subgrains, feldspar porphyroclasts and mineral-specific fabrics may distinguish grain-boundary migration from brittle grain crushing. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with orientation of the X–Z section, grain-scale mechanism and strain partitioning and illustrate fabric asymmetry, finite strain and deformation phase. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

Fine-grained fault rock is not necessarily mylonite; cataclasites can also be strongly foliated. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Document microstructural features of at least two minerals and compare their likely deformation conditions with independent thermometry. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For mylonites and recrystallization mechanisms, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

§6.7 Lesson 6.5 — Vorticity and pure versus simple shear

Mechanism and observational scale

Simple shear involves rotational components; coaxial pure shear can change shape without bulk rigid-body rotation of principal axes in a homogeneous model. Draw a basic geometric sketch and define the coordinate system, sample orientation and reference state if one is needed.

Evidence and documentation

Rotating clasts and asymmetric markers can help constrain flow style, though each responds to its own mechanical contrast. Document the exposure, instrument or measurement technique, scale and uncertainty before discussing tectonic implications. Maintain an observation/inference table with orientation of the X–Z section, grain-scale mechanism and strain partitioning and illustrate fabric asymmetry, finite strain and deformation phase. If your evidence is a two-dimensional trace of a three-dimensional surface, state exactly what its projection does and does not resolve.

Competing interpretation

Finite foliation orientation alone cannot discriminate simple from pure shear, nor uniquely recover a vorticity history. Record expected results for each explanation before carrying out the test. A model requiring hidden geometry must mark that geometry as inferred.

Test and scientific protocol

Compare predicted marker rotations under end-member shear simulations and identify which observations would discriminate them. State the predicted direction, location, quantity or material signature under both models. Use at least one measurement not previously employed to construct the model. For vorticity and pure versus simple shear, define sample selection criteria, coordinate conventions and an acceptable range of observation error.

Limitation, cross-check and synthesis

Many mixed-flow paths produce equivalent finite strain ellipses. Consider the alternative that changes the interpretation most while retaining the measured observations. List which surfaces or fabrics predate which others using direct cut, fold, overprint or growth relations. Leave pairs unordered where no observation establishes priority. Reactivation means a single surface may occur more than once in the event graph.

A flow-model label should always declare whether it concerns instantaneous kinematics or finite strain. A defensible conclusion therefore has five components: observed feature, ideal relation used, leading interpretation, decisive contrary test and residual uncertainty. Indicate whether each is local to the measured exposure or can legitimately be extended across the mapped domain.

§6.8 Integrated investigation 6 — Shear-zone transect and microscopy

Construct a virtual transect from undeformed granite through mylonitic rock into a high-strain core. Plot representative grain size, foliation orientation and marker axial ratio from a fictional table. Show the simple shear calculation for one marker displacement over zone thickness. Interpret oriented S–C fabric in a stated viewing direction, then propose an incompatible shear sense and the microscopic feature that would overturn the initial assessment. Explicitly consider recrystallization, inherited foliation and variable viscosity.

Analysis protocol and deliverables

The following integrated investigation uses fictional measurements expressly manufactured to demonstrate methods. It is not a published field survey or a substitute for collecting data. Begin with original measurements and metadata; present interpreted structures in separate layers. Compute at least one simple quantity with its units, make two competing predictions, and decide which additional piece of evidence would be most informative. Report any failure of the data to distinguish models. Submit a map or diagram, annotated calculations, a brief uncertainty table and a paragraph explaining whether the result has any practical limitations. The first panel of the submitted figure should contain observations only. The second may show an inferred geometry, but include a legend differentiating solid observation lines from dashed extrapolations. The third should compare an alternative model and identify the measurement that would select between them. A model should be judged against the data it did not use for fitting whenever possible.

Interpreting the synthetic dataset

Open datasets/unit-06.csv from the offline reader. All station names, orientations, distances and categories in this CSV are fictional. Plot the values and report which trend, if any, is robust against removal of two observations. Where the data do not contain enough directional or temporal context to support a tectonic claim, write that limitation explicitly. Do not claim a physical trend is established just because a graphing tool draws a smooth line.

Independent review

Exchange interpretations with another student. Ask them to identify one term or symbol whose meaning is unclear, one unstated convention and one observation that could disprove the preferred reconstruction. Revise the final diagram to make these items visible. Grade the work on measurement transparency and falsifiability, not on whether every student selected the same visually attractive fault or fold model.
