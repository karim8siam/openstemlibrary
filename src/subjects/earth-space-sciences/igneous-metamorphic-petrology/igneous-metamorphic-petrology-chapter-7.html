Unit 7 — Microtextures and quantitative metamorphic petrology

Link thin-section evidence with models and explicit uncertainty.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university petrology courses. Eight units with five solved questions per unit and 16 interactive simulations illustrating eutectic melting fractions, Rayleigh fractional crystallization, mineral modal abundance, geobarometric pressure gradients, and metamorphic reaction kinetics. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§7.1 Central question

Link thin-section evidence with models and explicit uncertainty.

§7.2 Unit outcomes and perspective

Practice petrographic description, mineral chemistry, compositional plots, pressure–temperature estimates and mass balance. The central approach is to separate measurement, interpretation, and uncertainty before assigning any historical meaning to a rock. Petrography identifies minerals, textures and overprinting relationships; geochemistry expresses chemical patterns; field mapping constrains geometry and chronology; quantitative models test whether a proposed history is physically plausible. No one method can normally reconstruct the entire history of a complex terrain. The unit therefore uses deliberately simplified calculations alongside explicit alternatives and checks.

§7.3 Prior knowledge and the logic of the lesson sequence

Recall that composition is not the same as mineral assemblage: two rocks can have similar bulk chemistry yet different minerals after differing thermal or pressure histories. Revisit the distinction between an intensive variable such as temperature or pressure and an extensive quantity such as mass or volume. When sketching diagrams, annotate axis scales and units and include the assumptions that make a boundary meaningful. For any result, ask whether equilibrium, a closed reservoir or ideal geometry was used. These assumptions should be stated before applying equations to natural rocks.

§7.4 Evidence ladder for this unit

Begin with repeatable descriptions of a specimen or synthetic dataset. Next ask which process could account for those descriptions under plausible Earth conditions. Finally test a process model against a competing explanation. Even a good process model does not automatically identify a unique geographic origin. A strong field interpretation shows where each independent line of evidence enters, what could overturn it and which uncertainty is irreducible without new sampling. Use contextual evidence from the surrounding stratigraphy and structures to prevent a microscope-scale feature from being overinterpreted.

§7.5 Porphyroblast inclusion trails

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Inclusions trapped during crystal growth may preserve earlier fabric orientations relative to external matrix foliation. To reason quantitatively about porphyroblast inclusion trails, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Garnet spiral trails, straight internal fabrics and truncation relationships can indicate growth and deformation overlap. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

A single apparent spiral in two-dimensional section may reflect cutting orientation rather than rotation. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Inspect sections at several orientations, compare multiple grains and test alternative three-dimensional geometries. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Use two samples at contrasting spatial scales where possible: a local thin section and a regional outcrop pattern. Agreement across scales is more informative than repeated estimates of a single proxy.

§7.6 Metamorphic mineral chemistry

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Solid-solution minerals record chemical partitioning influenced by bulk chemistry, temperature, pressure and evolving phase contacts. To reason quantitatively about metamorphic mineral chemistry, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Garnet Fe-Mg-Mn-Ca zoning and amphibole composition can be analysed along measured traverses. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

An iron-rich garnet core could reflect growth sequence or subsequent diffusion and re-equilibration. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Use calibrated analyses, compositional maps and phase relations instead of treating endmembers as independent phases. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

An independent mineralogical check can be more valuable than simply increasing precision of an already biased major-element measurement. Plan discrimination first, then precision.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

A single exchange thermometer can produce plausible values despite non-equilibrium grains. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Document negative evidence as carefully as positive evidence. A predicted reaction product that is absent from an adequately sampled rock deserves an explanation rather than being silently ignored.

§7.8 Pseudosections and equilibrium assemblage modeling

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

A calculated phase diagram for a measured bulk composition predicts stable assemblages and proportions across a P–T region. To reason quantitatively about pseudosections and equilibrium assemblage modeling, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Observed mineral modes, mineral chemistry and textures can be matched against computed isopleths. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

Agreement with one mineral assemblage is not unique evidence for a particular P–T path. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Test multiple bulk compositions and activity models; separately evaluate retrograde adjustment. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Do not infer time from spatial distance without independent constraints; a cooling front, reaction isograd and intrusive contact are different physical objects.

§7.9 Mass transfer and fluid–rock reaction

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Metasomatism moves chemical components between domains, changing mineralogy and sometimes volume. To reason quantitatively about mass transfer and fluid–rock reaction, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Reaction fronts, skarn zoning and systematic gains/losses across a contact can show transfer. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

A bulk concentration difference may be caused by original layering rather than infiltration. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Use an immobile-element framework and replicated transects for approximate isocon analysis. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Change one assumption at a time when constructing an alternative model. This approach demonstrates whether the conclusion is stable, or whether a single unmeasured parameter controls the interpretation.

§7.10 Unit 7 summary and assessment

The five lessons in this unit share a logic of observation → explanation → discrimination → calculation → qualified conclusion. Submit the five worked calculations with their units, three alternative-hypothesis comparisons and an annotated conceptual diagram. The diagram should distinguish arrows that represent measured directions from arrows that indicate proposed process pathways. Your final statement should name the observation that would most efficiently falsify your preferred account. A final confidence label is required, but the label is not a substitute for the written justification.
