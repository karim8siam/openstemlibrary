Unit 6 — Metamorphic facies, fabric and pressure–temperature paths

Reconstruct metamorphic histories rather than memorize rock names.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Igneous and Metamorphic Petrology study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§6.1 Central question

Reconstruct metamorphic histories rather than memorize rock names.

§6.2 Unit outcomes and perspective

Bring together mineral reactions, deformation and geochronology to infer how crust moves and changes. The central approach is to separate measurement, interpretation, and uncertainty before assigning any historical meaning to a rock. Petrography identifies minerals, textures and overprinting relationships; geochemistry expresses chemical patterns; field mapping constrains geometry and chronology; quantitative models test whether a proposed history is physically plausible. No one method can normally reconstruct the entire history of a complex terrain. The unit therefore uses deliberately simplified calculations alongside explicit alternatives and checks.

§6.3 Prior knowledge and the logic of the lesson sequence

Recall that composition is not the same as mineral assemblage: two rocks can have similar bulk chemistry yet different minerals after differing thermal or pressure histories. Revisit the distinction between an intensive variable such as temperature or pressure and an extensive quantity such as mass or volume. When sketching diagrams, annotate axis scales and units and include the assumptions that make a boundary meaningful. For any result, ask whether equilibrium, a closed reservoir or ideal geometry was used. These assumptions should be stated before applying equations to natural rocks.

§6.4 Evidence ladder for this unit

Begin with repeatable descriptions of a specimen or synthetic dataset. Next ask which process could account for those descriptions under plausible Earth conditions. Finally test a process model against a competing explanation. Even a good process model does not automatically identify a unique geographic origin. A strong field interpretation shows where each independent line of evidence enters, what could overturn it and which uncertainty is irreducible without new sampling. Use contextual evidence from the surrounding stratigraphy and structures to prevent a microscope-scale feature from being overinterpreted.

§6.5 Facies and the role of protolith

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Metamorphic facies group broadly comparable stability conditions inferred from mineral assemblages, with exact phases depending on composition. To reason quantitatively about facies and the role of protolith, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Mafic rocks progressing from greenschist toward amphibolite may develop systematic changes in amphibole and plagioclase. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

A mica-rich metapelite does not contain the same index assemblage as a basalt at equal P and T. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Cross-check mineral chemistry, protolith indicators and mapped regional gradients. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Change one assumption at a time when constructing an alternative model. This approach demonstrates whether the conclusion is stable, or whether a single unmeasured parameter controls the interpretation.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Measure transects and simulate transient conduction with variable thermal properties. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

An independent mineralogical check can be more valuable than simply increasing precision of an already biased major-element measurement. Plan discrimination first, then precision.

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Schistosity, crenulation, stretched pebbles and mineral lineations may mark different deformation stages. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

A planar mineral fabric is not necessarily parallel to original bedding or the latest shortening direction. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Use oriented samples and overprinting relations to separate fabrics in a relative deformation history. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Document negative evidence as carefully as positive evidence. A predicted reaction product that is absent from an adequately sampled rock deserves an explanation rather than being silently ignored.

§6.9 Clockwise and counterclockwise P–T paths

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Mineral inclusions, reaction rims and diffusion profiles can reveal segments of burial–heating–exhumation histories. To reason quantitatively about clockwise and counterclockwise p–t paths, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Core-to-rim zoning, reaction boundaries and dated minerals anchor parts of a pressure–temperature trajectory. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

Similar peak conditions can arise along distinct clockwise or counterclockwise paths. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Combine thermobarometry and geochronology with petrographic sequence, retaining alternate path families. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Do not infer time from spatial distance without independent constraints; a cooling front, reaction isograd and intrusive contact are different physical objects.

§6.10 Unit 6 summary and assessment

The five lessons in this unit share a logic of observation → explanation → discrimination → calculation → qualified conclusion. Submit the five worked calculations with their units, three alternative-hypothesis comparisons and an annotated conceptual diagram. The diagram should distinguish arrows that represent measured directions from arrows that indicate proposed process pathways. Your final statement should name the observation that would most efficiently falsify your preferred account. A final confidence label is required, but the label is not a substitute for the written justification.
