Unit 5 — Metamorphic reactions and mineral stability

Read mineral assemblages as contingent records of pressure, temperature and bulk chemistry.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university petrology courses. Eight units with five solved questions per unit and 16 interactive simulations illustrating eutectic melting fractions, Rayleigh fractional crystallization, mineral modal abundance, geobarometric pressure gradients, and metamorphic reaction kinetics. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§5.1 Central question

Read mineral assemblages as contingent records of pressure, temperature and bulk chemistry.

§5.2 Unit outcomes and perspective

Identify protolith, reaction progress and equilibrium domains before assigning metamorphic grade or tectonic setting. The central approach is to separate measurement, interpretation, and uncertainty before assigning any historical meaning to a rock. Petrography identifies minerals, textures and overprinting relationships; geochemistry expresses chemical patterns; field mapping constrains geometry and chronology; quantitative models test whether a proposed history is physically plausible. No one method can normally reconstruct the entire history of a complex terrain. The unit therefore uses deliberately simplified calculations alongside explicit alternatives and checks.

§5.3 Prior knowledge and the logic of the lesson sequence

Recall that composition is not the same as mineral assemblage: two rocks can have similar bulk chemistry yet different minerals after differing thermal or pressure histories. Revisit the distinction between an intensive variable such as temperature or pressure and an extensive quantity such as mass or volume. When sketching diagrams, annotate axis scales and units and include the assumptions that make a boundary meaningful. For any result, ask whether equilibrium, a closed reservoir or ideal geometry was used. These assumptions should be stated before applying equations to natural rocks.

§5.4 Evidence ladder for this unit

Begin with repeatable descriptions of a specimen or synthetic dataset. Next ask which process could account for those descriptions under plausible Earth conditions. Finally test a process model against a competing explanation. Even a good process model does not automatically identify a unique geographic origin. A strong field interpretation shows where each independent line of evidence enters, what could overturn it and which uncertainty is irreducible without new sampling. Use contextual evidence from the surrounding stratigraphy and structures to prevent a microscope-scale feature from being overinterpreted.

§5.5 What counts as metamorphism

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Metamorphism changes mineralogy, microstructure or composition largely in the solid state in response to new conditions. To reason quantitatively about what counts as metamorphism, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Recrystallized sedimentary structures, reaction rims and newly grown minerals can coexist in one specimen. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

Hydrothermal alteration, weathering and deformation can overlap metamorphic signatures. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Use paragenesis and field context to infer whether minerals grew during burial, contact heating or later fluids. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Do not infer time from spatial distance without independent constraints; a cooling front, reaction isograd and intrusive contact are different physical objects.

§5.7 Index minerals and metamorphic zones

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Certain minerals appear over characteristic conditions only when the bulk chemistry can support them. To reason quantitatively about index minerals and metamorphic zones, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Mapped first appearances of chlorite, biotite, garnet, staurolite and sillimanite can organize pelitic sequences. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

Absence of garnet does not prove low temperature in a calcium-rich or magnesium-poor protolith. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Compare similar bulk-composition samples across a transect and map reactions with their uncertainty. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Use two samples at contrasting spatial scales where possible: a local thin section and a regional outcrop pattern. Agreement across scales is more informative than repeated estimates of a single proxy.

§5.8 Dehydration and decarbonation reactions

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Heating or pressure changes may destabilize hydrous and carbonate-bearing minerals, releasing fluid as new phases form. To reason quantitatively about dehydration and decarbonation reactions, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Reaction textures around chlorite, muscovite or calcite can preserve incomplete transformations. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

A fluid inclusion does not alone establish fluid production during the observed mineral reaction. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Balance reactants and products, examine reaction rims and test fluid sources. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

An independent mineralogical check can be more valuable than simply increasing precision of an already biased major-element measurement. Plan discrimination first, then precision.

§5.9 Metamorphic equilibrium and kinetic limits

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Even if a mineral assemblage is thermodynamically stable, it may not form without nucleation, diffusion and sufficient time. To reason quantitatively about metamorphic equilibrium and kinetic limits, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Zoned garnet, metastable relics and corona structures provide kinetic evidence. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

An apparently incompatible pair might be a preserved sequential assemblage rather than analytical error. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Map crystal domains and contact relations at multiple scales before solving for conditions. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Document negative evidence as carefully as positive evidence. A predicted reaction product that is absent from an adequately sampled rock deserves an explanation rather than being silently ignored.

§5.10 Unit 5 summary and assessment

The five lessons in this unit share a logic of observation → explanation → discrimination → calculation → qualified conclusion. Submit the five worked calculations with their units, three alternative-hypothesis comparisons and an annotated conceptual diagram. The diagram should distinguish arrows that represent measured directions from arrows that indicate proposed process pathways. Your final statement should name the observation that would most efficiently falsify your preferred account. A final confidence label is required, but the label is not a substitute for the written justification.
