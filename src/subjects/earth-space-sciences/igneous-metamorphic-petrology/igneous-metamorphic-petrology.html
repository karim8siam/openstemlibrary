Unit 1 — Melt origins and the thermodynamics of melting

Why partially molten rock behaves differently from its unmelted source.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university petrology courses. Eight units with five solved questions per unit and 16 interactive simulations illustrating eutectic melting fractions, Rayleigh fractional crystallization, mineral modal abundance, geobarometric pressure gradients, and metamorphic reaction kinetics. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§1.1 Central question

Why partially molten rock behaves differently from its unmelted source.

§1.2 Unit outcomes and perspective

Construct pressure–temperature–composition arguments without confusing a model line with a universal geological boundary. The central approach is to separate measurement, interpretation, and uncertainty before assigning any historical meaning to a rock. Petrography identifies minerals, textures and overprinting relationships; geochemistry expresses chemical patterns; field mapping constrains geometry and chronology; quantitative models test whether a proposed history is physically plausible. No one method can normally reconstruct the entire history of a complex terrain. The unit therefore uses deliberately simplified calculations alongside explicit alternatives and checks.

§1.3 Prior knowledge and the logic of the lesson sequence

Recall that composition is not the same as mineral assemblage: two rocks can have similar bulk chemistry yet different minerals after differing thermal or pressure histories. Revisit the distinction between an intensive variable such as temperature or pressure and an extensive quantity such as mass or volume. When sketching diagrams, annotate axis scales and units and include the assumptions that make a boundary meaningful. For any result, ask whether equilibrium, a closed reservoir or ideal geometry was used. These assumptions should be stated before applying equations to natural rocks.

§1.4 Evidence ladder for this unit

Begin with repeatable descriptions of a specimen or synthetic dataset. Next ask which process could account for those descriptions under plausible Earth conditions. Finally test a process model against a competing explanation. Even a good process model does not automatically identify a unique geographic origin. A strong field interpretation shows where each independent line of evidence enters, what could overturn it and which uncertainty is irreducible without new sampling. Use contextual evidence from the surrounding stratigraphy and structures to prevent a microscope-scale feature from being overinterpreted.

§1.5 Lesson 1: Sources of crustal and mantle melts

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Melting begins where the local geotherm and a composition-dependent solidus intersect; a rock can partially melt while most minerals remain solid. To reason quantitatively about sources of crustal and mantle melts, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Peridotite residues, xenoliths, basalt compositions and seismic anomalies provide evidence with different spatial scales. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

A mafic lava might reflect a mantle source, crustal mixing or fractionation of a different starting melt. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Compare trace-element ratios, isotope constraints, phenocryst cargo and regional tectonics instead of reading source directly from colour. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Change one assumption at a time when constructing an alternative model. This approach demonstrates whether the conclusion is stable, or whether a single unmeasured parameter controls the interpretation.

Why this inference can fail

An assumed dry mantle solidus ignores water, oxidation state and chemical variability. The scale of observation controls what the measured record can resolve. Two samples showing similar assemblages are not necessarily coeval; two mineral compositions agreeing within analytical precision are not necessarily in equilibrium. Evidence of replacement can survive after bulk chemistry has changed. Change one assumption at a time when constructing an alternative model. This approach demonstrates whether the conclusion is stable, or whether a single unmeasured parameter controls the interpretation. No degree of numerical polishing eliminates a missing source reservoir or undocumented sample context.

A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Use two samples at contrasting spatial scales where possible: a local thin section and a regional outcrop pattern. Agreement across scales is more informative than repeated estimates of a single proxy.

§1.7 Lesson 3: Flux melting in subduction systems

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Arc-front positions, water-rich volcanic products and slab geometry collectively bear on the mechanism. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

A hydrated arc magma is not proof that all its water came from a single slab mineral. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Use fluid-mobile versus immobile element patterns and petrological evidence for hydrous mineral breakdown. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

An independent mineralogical check can be more valuable than simply increasing precision of an already biased major-element measurement. Plan discrimination first, then precision.

§1.9 Lesson 5: Phase diagrams and the meaning of a solidus

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

A solidus marks the first appearance of melt for a specified composition and conditions; a liquidus marks complete melting. To reason quantitatively about phase diagrams and the meaning of a solidus, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Binary eutectic experiments and natural mineral assemblages provide complementary information. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

A two-component eutectic picture does not accurately capture every multicomponent magma. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Apply the lever rule to a controlled two-phase example, then identify why natural systems depart from its assumptions. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Do not infer time from spatial distance without independent constraints; a cooling front, reaction isograd and intrusive contact are different physical objects.

§1.10 Unit 1 summary and assessment

The five lessons in this unit share a logic of observation → explanation → discrimination → calculation → qualified conclusion. Submit the five worked calculations with their units, three alternative-hypothesis comparisons and an annotated conceptual diagram. The diagram should distinguish arrows that represent measured directions from arrows that indicate proposed process pathways. Your final statement should name the observation that would most efficiently falsify your preferred account. A final confidence label is required, but the label is not a substitute for the written justification.
