Unit 3 — Magma evolution and intrusive architecture

Use mass balance and physical constraints to reconstruct magma changes.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university petrology courses. Eight units with five solved questions per unit and 16 interactive simulations illustrating eutectic melting fractions, Rayleigh fractional crystallization, mineral modal abundance, geobarometric pressure gradients, and metamorphic reaction kinetics. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§3.1 Central question

Use mass balance and physical constraints to reconstruct magma changes.

§3.2 Unit outcomes and perspective

Track how crystals, liquids, fluids and wall rocks interact, and describe what observations distinguish processes. The central approach is to separate measurement, interpretation, and uncertainty before assigning any historical meaning to a rock. Petrography identifies minerals, textures and overprinting relationships; geochemistry expresses chemical patterns; field mapping constrains geometry and chronology; quantitative models test whether a proposed history is physically plausible. No one method can normally reconstruct the entire history of a complex terrain. The unit therefore uses deliberately simplified calculations alongside explicit alternatives and checks.

§3.3 Prior knowledge and the logic of the lesson sequence

Recall that composition is not the same as mineral assemblage: two rocks can have similar bulk chemistry yet different minerals after differing thermal or pressure histories. Revisit the distinction between an intensive variable such as temperature or pressure and an extensive quantity such as mass or volume. When sketching diagrams, annotate axis scales and units and include the assumptions that make a boundary meaningful. For any result, ask whether equilibrium, a closed reservoir or ideal geometry was used. These assumptions should be stated before applying equations to natural rocks.

§3.4 Evidence ladder for this unit

Begin with repeatable descriptions of a specimen or synthetic dataset. Next ask which process could account for those descriptions under plausible Earth conditions. Finally test a process model against a competing explanation. Even a good process model does not automatically identify a unique geographic origin. A strong field interpretation shows where each independent line of evidence enters, what could overturn it and which uncertainty is irreducible without new sampling. Use contextual evidence from the surrounding stratigraphy and structures to prevent a microscope-scale feature from being overinterpreted.

§3.5 Fractional and equilibrium crystallization

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Removing crystals as they form changes residual melt composition differently from keeping crystals in equilibrium with the liquid. To reason quantitatively about fractional and equilibrium crystallization, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Crystal cumulates, incompatible-element enrichment and mineral zoning can constrain separation histories. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

A trend in whole-rock silica alone does not uniquely establish fractional crystallization. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Use a simple Rayleigh trace-element model and test whether major-element balances are plausible. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

An independent mineralogical check can be more valuable than simply increasing precision of an already biased major-element measurement. Plan discrimination first, then precision.

§3.6 Magma mixing and mingling

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Compositionally different magmas can exchange heat and material; mingling preserves discrete globules whereas mixing homogenizes more thoroughly. To reason quantitatively about magma mixing and mingling, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Mafic enclaves, disequilibrium textures, resorbed crystals and mixed isotopic signatures are diagnostic in combinations. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

Dark enclaves may instead be restite or disaggregated wall rock. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Look for chilled enclave margins, acicular crystals and compositional trends that support an injection event. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Document negative evidence as carefully as positive evidence. A predicted reaction product that is absent from an adequately sampled rock deserves an explanation rather than being silently ignored.

§3.7 Assimilation and crustal contamination

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

To reason quantitatively about assimilation and crustal contamination, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Isotope shifts and inherited zircon populations can signal interaction with older crust. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

An isotopic signature may come from mantle-source heterogeneity rather than assimilation during ascent. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Compare wall-rock composition, zircon inheritance and thermal constraints with a candidate mixing line. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Do not infer time from spatial distance without independent constraints; a cooling front, reaction isograd and intrusive contact are different physical objects.

§3.8 Dikes, sills and plutons

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Magma emplacement depends on buoyancy, host-rock stress, available fractures, viscosity and local heat flow. To reason quantitatively about dikes, sills and plutons, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Cross-cutting dikes, concordant sills, chilled margins and contact aureoles reveal emplacement relationships. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

A sheet parallel to layering is not necessarily a sill if the host beds were folded later. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Map regional fabrics and contacts in three dimensions; use cross-cutting sequences where exposures allow. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Change one assumption at a time when constructing an alternative model. This approach demonstrates whether the conclusion is stable, or whether a single unmeasured parameter controls the interpretation.

§3.9 Cumulate processes and crystal settling

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Crystal accumulation can concentrate minerals through settling, flotation, compaction, replenishment and in-situ growth. To reason quantitatively about cumulate processes and crystal settling, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Layered mafic complexes show modal layering, graded beds and poikilitic textures. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

A mineral-rich layer can originate by changing crystallization conditions rather than gravitational sorting. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Compare shape fabrics, mineral chemistry and layer continuity with physical settling estimates. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Use two samples at contrasting spatial scales where possible: a local thin section and a regional outcrop pattern. Agreement across scales is more informative than repeated estimates of a single proxy.

§3.10 Unit 3 summary and assessment

The five lessons in this unit share a logic of observation → explanation → discrimination → calculation → qualified conclusion. Submit the five worked calculations with their units, three alternative-hypothesis comparisons and an annotated conceptual diagram. The diagram should distinguish arrows that represent measured directions from arrows that indicate proposed process pathways. Your final statement should name the observation that would most efficiently falsify your preferred account. A final confidence label is required, but the label is not a substitute for the written justification.
