Unit 2 — Igneous classification and textures

Describe what can be observed before interpreting rock origins.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university petrology courses. Eight units with five solved questions per unit and 16 interactive simulations illustrating eutectic melting fractions, Rayleigh fractional crystallization, mineral modal abundance, geobarometric pressure gradients, and metamorphic reaction kinetics. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§2.1 Central question

Describe what can be observed before interpreting rock origins.

§2.2 Unit outcomes and perspective

Create a defensible rock name from mineral mode, chemistry and texture; document cases where a formal scheme cannot be applied. The central approach is to separate measurement, interpretation, and uncertainty before assigning any historical meaning to a rock. Petrography identifies minerals, textures and overprinting relationships; geochemistry expresses chemical patterns; field mapping constrains geometry and chronology; quantitative models test whether a proposed history is physically plausible. No one method can normally reconstruct the entire history of a complex terrain. The unit therefore uses deliberately simplified calculations alongside explicit alternatives and checks.

§2.3 Prior knowledge and the logic of the lesson sequence

Recall that composition is not the same as mineral assemblage: two rocks can have similar bulk chemistry yet different minerals after differing thermal or pressure histories. Revisit the distinction between an intensive variable such as temperature or pressure and an extensive quantity such as mass or volume. When sketching diagrams, annotate axis scales and units and include the assumptions that make a boundary meaningful. For any result, ask whether equilibrium, a closed reservoir or ideal geometry was used. These assumptions should be stated before applying equations to natural rocks.

§2.4 Evidence ladder for this unit

Begin with repeatable descriptions of a specimen or synthetic dataset. Next ask which process could account for those descriptions under plausible Earth conditions. Finally test a process model against a competing explanation. Even a good process model does not automatically identify a unique geographic origin. A strong field interpretation shows where each independent line of evidence enters, what could overturn it and which uncertainty is irreducible without new sampling. Use contextual evidence from the surrounding stratigraphy and structures to prevent a microscope-scale feature from being overinterpreted.

§2.5 Lesson 1: Phaneritic, aphanitic, glassy and porphyritic textures

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Cooling and nucleation histories influence grain size, but grain size also reflects supersaturation, chemistry and residence time. To reason quantitatively about phaneritic, aphanitic, glassy and porphyritic textures, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

A coarse crystal population in fine groundmass documents at least two growth or residence regimes. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

A porphyritic texture can arise during storage, recharge or decompression rather than from a single two-stage cooling event. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Measure grain-size distributions and compare crystal zoning with the groundmass. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Use two samples at contrasting spatial scales where possible: a local thin section and a regional outcrop pattern. Agreement across scales is more informative than repeated estimates of a single proxy.

Why this inference can fail

Annealing, deformation and alteration may erase or modify the original magmatic fabric. The scale of observation controls what the measured record can resolve. Two samples showing similar assemblages are not necessarily coeval; two mineral compositions agreeing within analytical precision are not necessarily in equilibrium. Evidence of replacement can survive after bulk chemistry has changed. Use two samples at contrasting spatial scales where possible: a local thin section and a regional outcrop pattern. Agreement across scales is more informative than repeated estimates of a single proxy. No degree of numerical polishing eliminates a missing source reservoir or undocumented sample context.

§2.6 Lesson 2: QAPF modal rock naming

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Plutonic felsic rock names use relative proportions of quartz, alkali feldspar, plagioclase and feldspathoids in applicable IUGS fields. To reason quantitatively about qapf modal rock naming, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Point counts in a representative thin section can provide modal Q, A and P fractions. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

A visually pink coarse rock is not necessarily granite; colour and trade names are unreliable classifiers. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Normalize Q+A+P appropriately and compare the estimate with a defined classification diagram. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

An independent mineralogical check can be more valuable than simply increasing precision of an already biased major-element measurement. Plan discrimination first, then precision.

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

Secondary sodium or potassium mobility can shift a volcanic sample into an inappropriate TAS field. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Inspect loss on ignition, alteration petrography and immobile-element consistency before classification. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Document negative evidence as carefully as positive evidence. A predicted reaction product that is absent from an adequately sampled rock deserves an explanation rather than being silently ignored.

§2.8 Lesson 4: Vesicles, amygdales and pyroclastic fabrics

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Gas bubbles, later mineral-filled cavities and broken clasts record distinct physical histories. To reason quantitatively about vesicles, amygdales and pyroclastic fabrics, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Vesicle shape, flattened pumice fragments, welding textures and clast sorting provide process evidence. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

Rounded cavities may be primary vesicles, solution features or later voids. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Combine cross-cutting relationships with microscopy and field-scale bedding or flow structures. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Do not infer time from spatial distance without independent constraints; a cooling front, reaction isograd and intrusive contact are different physical objects.

§2.9 Lesson 5: Crystal size distributions and residence histories

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Population shapes in crystal-size plots reflect nucleation, growth, mixing and recycling rather than just cooling rate. To reason quantitatively about crystal size distributions and residence histories, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Counts by size class and zoning traverses test whether the population is single-stage. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

An apparent kink can arise from sampling bias or intersection effects in two-dimensional sections. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Compare replicated grain counts and uncertainty envelopes, then test alternative population models. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Change one assumption at a time when constructing an alternative model. This approach demonstrates whether the conclusion is stable, or whether a single unmeasured parameter controls the interpretation.

§2.10 Unit 2 summary and assessment

The five lessons in this unit share a logic of observation → explanation → discrimination → calculation → qualified conclusion. Submit the five worked calculations with their units, three alternative-hypothesis comparisons and an annotated conceptual diagram. The diagram should distinguish arrows that represent measured directions from arrows that indicate proposed process pathways. Your final statement should name the observation that would most efficiently falsify your preferred account. A final confidence label is required, but the label is not a substitute for the written justification.
