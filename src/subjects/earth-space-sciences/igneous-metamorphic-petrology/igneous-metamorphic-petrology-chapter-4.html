Unit 4 — Volcanic systems, fluids and geochemical tracers

Connect magma composition to surface behaviour without equating composition with hazard.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Igneous and Metamorphic Petrology study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§4.1 Central question

Connect magma composition to surface behaviour without equating composition with hazard.

§4.2 Unit outcomes and perspective

Compare competing physical models and explain why forecasts require monitored measurements and not textbook classifications alone. The central approach is to separate measurement, interpretation, and uncertainty before assigning any historical meaning to a rock. Petrography identifies minerals, textures and overprinting relationships; geochemistry expresses chemical patterns; field mapping constrains geometry and chronology; quantitative models test whether a proposed history is physically plausible. No one method can normally reconstruct the entire history of a complex terrain. The unit therefore uses deliberately simplified calculations alongside explicit alternatives and checks.

§4.3 Prior knowledge and the logic of the lesson sequence

Recall that composition is not the same as mineral assemblage: two rocks can have similar bulk chemistry yet different minerals after differing thermal or pressure histories. Revisit the distinction between an intensive variable such as temperature or pressure and an extensive quantity such as mass or volume. When sketching diagrams, annotate axis scales and units and include the assumptions that make a boundary meaningful. For any result, ask whether equilibrium, a closed reservoir or ideal geometry was used. These assumptions should be stated before applying equations to natural rocks.

§4.4 Evidence ladder for this unit

Begin with repeatable descriptions of a specimen or synthetic dataset. Next ask which process could account for those descriptions under plausible Earth conditions. Finally test a process model against a competing explanation. Even a good process model does not automatically identify a unique geographic origin. A strong field interpretation shows where each independent line of evidence enters, what could overturn it and which uncertainty is irreducible without new sampling. Use contextual evidence from the surrounding stratigraphy and structures to prevent a microscope-scale feature from being overinterpreted.

§4.5 Viscosity, temperature and volatile content

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Melt viscosity responds strongly to polymerization, dissolved water, temperature and suspended crystals. To reason quantitatively about viscosity, temperature and volatile content, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Flow fronts, erupted glass compositions, temperature estimates and crystal volume fractions constrain rheology. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

Two lavas with similar silica may flow differently because of temperature, bubbles or crystal load. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Contrast controlled-temperature viscosity curves and field observations while keeping strain-rate effects explicit. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Document negative evidence as carefully as positive evidence. A predicted reaction product that is absent from an adequately sampled rock deserves an explanation rather than being silently ignored.

Why this inference can fail

Newtonian assumptions often break down in crystal-rich or bubble-rich magma. The scale of observation controls what the measured record can resolve. Two samples showing similar assemblages are not necessarily coeval; two mineral compositions agreeing within analytical precision are not necessarily in equilibrium. Evidence of replacement can survive after bulk chemistry has changed. Document negative evidence as carefully as positive evidence. A predicted reaction product that is absent from an adequately sampled rock deserves an explanation rather than being silently ignored. No degree of numerical polishing eliminates a missing source reservoir or undocumented sample context.

§4.6 Degassing and vesicle growth

A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Vesicle number density, dissolved volatile measurements and gas emission records probe different process stages. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

A vesicular lava can reflect open-system escape rather than high initial dissolved water. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Compare glass inclusions, matrix glasses and bubble size distributions with ascent scenarios. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Do not infer time from spatial distance without independent constraints; a cooling front, reaction isograd and intrusive contact are different physical objects.

§4.7 Pyroclasts, fragmentation and welding

To reason quantitatively about pyroclasts, fragmentation and welding, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Glass shards, pumice, bombs, lapilli, fiamme and graded beds record transport and deposition. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

A fine ash layer need not result from the same mechanism as a nearby coarse pumice bed. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Measure grain-size distribution, componentry, bed geometry and deformation of hot clasts. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Change one assumption at a time when constructing an alternative model. This approach demonstrates whether the conclusion is stable, or whether a single unmeasured parameter controls the interpretation.

§4.8 Trace elements and rare-earth patterns

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Elements with different partitioning behaviour can constrain melting and differentiation when normalized and contextualized correctly. To reason quantitatively about trace elements and rare-earth patterns, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Incompatible-element ratios and rare-earth patterns may reveal residual garnet or variable melting degrees. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

A steep heavy rare-earth slope is not proof of one depth because source differences and alteration can confound it. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Test patterns against at least two mass-balance scenarios with transparent partition coefficients. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

Use two samples at contrasting spatial scales where possible: a local thin section and a regional outcrop pattern. Agreement across scales is more informative than repeated estimates of a single proxy.

§4.9 Radiogenic isotope fingerprints

Mechanism: what physical or chemical change is being proposed?

Long-lived parent–daughter systems constrain source characteristics and timing under specific closure and alteration assumptions. To reason quantitatively about radiogenic isotope fingerprints, first distinguish the part of the system that is open to transport from the part treated as a closed parcel. Track changes in the mineral or melt assemblage, not just the name assigned to the whole rock. A rock records its latest preserved state together with some relics, so the working question is not simply “What rock is this?” but “What sequence of processes could have produced these specific observations?”

Evidence: what would a geologist actually measure?

Sr, Nd, Pb and Hf isotopic signatures can be compared with mapped crust and mantle reservoirs. Organize the field notebook as a sequence of observation, measurement conditions and tentative interpretation. Geological evidence can come from hand specimens, thin sections, mapped contacts, electron-microprobe analyses, isotopes or dated mineral domains. The methods are complementary rather than interchangeable. Do not describe a map-scale contact as a microscopic mineral reaction, or a crystal core analysis as a whole-rock average.

Competing interpretations and a discriminating test

An isotope mixing curve can arise from source blending, contamination or inherited mineral phases. The alternative is worth considering because two very different histories can converge on similar final textures or compositions. Check mineral-scale domains, geochronology and isotope ratios in candidate endmembers. A test must be designed before the final preferred interpretation is chosen: otherwise the analyst risks using every possible feature as evidence for the same story. Identify the response variable, a comparison sample and the result each proposed process would predict.

An independent mineralogical check can be more valuable than simply increasing precision of an already biased major-element measurement. Plan discrimination first, then precision.

§4.10 Unit 4 summary and assessment

The five lessons in this unit share a logic of observation → explanation → discrimination → calculation → qualified conclusion. Submit the five worked calculations with their units, three alternative-hypothesis comparisons and an annotated conceptual diagram. The diagram should distinguish arrows that represent measured directions from arrows that indicate proposed process pathways. Your final statement should name the observation that would most efficiently falsify your preferred account. A final confidence label is required, but the label is not a substitute for the written justification.
