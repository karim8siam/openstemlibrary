About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university physical geology courses. Eight units with five solved questions per unit and 16 interactive simulations illustrating map distance scaling, density contrasts, isostatic equilibrium, seismic travel-time curves, and radioactive half-life decay. §2.1 Orientation: a geologist's problem

Which observations identify a material, and which reveal the conditions of its formation? This unit approaches the problem through testable claims: identify the material or process, distinguish directly observed features from reconstructed events, specify what could make the interpretation wrong, and explain the scale at which a result should be used. These are different intellectual tasks. Good observations do not automatically supply an explanation; a plausible explanation is not an adequate substitute for a measurement plan. Physical geology connects descriptive geology, quantitative reasoning, and the history of the Earth, but it depends on maintaining these distinctions.

Unit case: A teaching collection contains unknown grains, altered igneous hand samples, and fine sediments. Students must compare observable properties to avoid treating a single field test as decisive. Work with this as a fictional teaching scenario, not an actual measured field site. Write a preliminary hypothesis, an alternative hypothesis, a list of discriminating observations, and a decision about whether the existing information justifies a firm conclusion. A reader who begins with a favorite explanation should be able to describe the evidence that would lead them to abandon it.

Learning goals and scientific habits

A successful student will be able to define the terminology in context, describe a plausible chain of processes, examine evidence at multiple scales, calculate an interpretable quantity, state assumptions, and propose measurements that could reject an incorrect explanation. Students should not memorize a list of isolated labels and confuse them for field competence. Field notebooks should preserve sketches, orientation, dimensions, date, coordinate reference systems, and uncertainty where relevant. The analytical workflow should preserve formulas, dimensional checks, and whether numbers are hypothetical examples or observed data.

The unit includes five concept lessons, five solved calculations, a data investigation, and two interactive teaching models. The model sliders expose a specific assumption and recalculate a quantity; they are intentionally simple. They do not constitute validated scientific forecasts, hazard assessments, or mineral exploration services. A model can be internally mathematically correct while being inappropriate for the intended site, because representativeness and boundary conditions are separate requirements.

Unit-level synthesis map

Start with a measurable description and ask what conservation laws, boundary conditions, geometry, and timescales should connect it to a process. Compare the predicted trace of the process against observations. When a proposed cause is not unique, write down the alternative that best challenges it. Create a decision threshold tied to the intended use: conceptual understanding, preliminary screening, field campaign design, or certified professional work. A textbook exercise may be solved with the information given, whereas a real-world project may need more evidence before a number is safe to use. The most informative result is sometimes the identification of an unknown that changes the answer.

§2.2 Lesson 6: Atomic bonding and mineral definition

Geological concept

Minerals are naturally occurring crystalline solids characterized by ordered atomic arrangements and definable chemical compositions within permitted ranges. Distinguish an observed feature from an inferred history. Identify the material, physical scale, and duration at issue. A useful explanation should predict an additional observation that was not already used to construct the hypothesis.

Physical and chemical mechanisms

Ionic, covalent, and other bonding interactions influence hardness, cleavage, electrical behavior, and stability; lattice geometry can vary within compositional families. Sketch the necessary starting conditions, pathway, and geological record. Ask whether transport, phase change, reaction, or deformation is involved and whether later processes could modify the predicted evidence.

Evidence and analysis

Crystal form, cleavage planes, hardness tests, optical properties, and compositional analysis help identify mineral species. Record sample provenance, measurement scale, instrument or descriptive method, and uncertainty. Where an observation can support more than one process, look for independent evidence that can discriminate between alternatives.

Field notebook — a hypothetical example

Setting: A pale crystal from a hand specimen scratches glass and has irregular fracture.

Competing explanations: Quartz is a plausible identification, but some feldspars or weathered materials may pass a superficial hardness screen.

Discriminating investigation: Look for cleavage, check relative density, and obtain optical or diffraction evidence if mineral identity materially influences a petrologic interpretation.

Imagine presenting this evidence to a skeptical colleague. Specify three possible outcomes of the proposed test: supporting one explanation, favoring the alternative, or leaving the question unresolved. State any assumption about exposure, sample alteration, or instrument behavior.

Limits of the interpretation

A single hardness or color test is rarely diagnostic; solid solutions and impurities can produce overlap among otherwise distinct minerals. The observed pattern need not determine a unique history; spatial coverage and the timing of later alteration affect confidence. Name a competing history in the field report and make clear whether its rejection depends on evidence that is actually available.

Combine non-destructive screening with appropriate analytical methods while documenting how much material can be altered or destroyed. Distinguish a classroom inference from an applied decision with health, safety, environmental, or financial consequences. Professional site assessment requires appropriate standards and qualified personnel; the models here are learning aids.

§2.3 Lesson 7: Silicate structures and common rock formers

Geological concept

Silicon–oxygen tetrahedra form isolated units, chains, sheets, and frameworks; associated cations create mineral diversity. Distinguish an observed feature from an inferred history. Identify the material, physical scale, and duration at issue. A useful explanation should predict an additional observation that was not already used to construct the hypothesis.

Physical and chemical mechanisms

Connectivity governs bonding directions and helps explain cleavage behavior in olivine, pyroxene, amphibole, mica, feldspar, and quartz groups. Sketch the necessary starting conditions, pathway, and geological record. Ask whether transport, phase change, reaction, or deformation is involved and whether later processes could modify the predicted evidence.

Evidence and analysis

Hand-specimen textures, polarized-light microscopy, and X-ray diffraction reveal complementary aspects of mineral composition and structure. Record sample provenance, measurement scale, instrument or descriptive method, and uncertainty. Where an observation can support more than one process, look for independent evidence that can discriminate between alternatives.

Field notebook — a hypothetical example

Setting: A coarse mafic specimen contains dark prismatic crystals and pale feldspar.

Competing explanations: Pyroxene, amphibole, and altered ferromagnesian minerals may resemble one another at hand-sample scale.

Discriminating investigation: Measure cleavage angles on fresh grains, review thin-section relief and pleochroism, and use chemistry where the identification remains ambiguous.

Limits of the interpretation

The observed pattern need not determine a unique history; spatial coverage and the timing of later alteration affect confidence. Name a competing history in the field report and make clear whether its rejection depends on evidence that is actually available.

Use a mineral identification key with confidence ratings rather than assigning a species solely from appearance. §2.4 Lesson 8: Mineral properties and identification

Geological concept

Hardness, streak, luster, cleavage, fracture, density, and crystal habit are measurements or observations with distinct physical origins. Distinguish an observed feature from an inferred history. Identify the material, physical scale, and duration at issue. A useful explanation should predict an additional observation that was not already used to construct the hypothesis.

Physical and chemical mechanisms

Scratch tests compare relative resistance to abrasion, whereas cleavage indicates directions of weak bonds and density reflects mass per unit volume. Sketch the necessary starting conditions, pathway, and geological record. Ask whether transport, phase change, reaction, or deformation is involved and whether later processes could modify the predicted evidence.

Evidence and analysis

Hand-lens descriptions, streak plates, balance measurements, and controlled acid tests are standard screening observations when appropriate and safe. Record sample provenance, measurement scale, instrument or descriptive method, and uncertainty. Where an observation can support more than one process, look for independent evidence that can discriminate between alternatives.

Field notebook — a hypothetical example

Setting: Two metallic-looking specimens show different streak colors despite similar luster.

Competing explanations: The contrast may indicate different sulfides or oxides, but weathering coatings and mixed grains can distort both luster and streak.

Discriminating investigation: Collect fresh surfaces using appropriate safety procedures and pair streak with density, magnetic response, and compositional results rather than one definitive scratch.

Imagine presenting this evidence to a skeptical colleague. Specify three possible outcomes of the proposed test: supporting one explanation, favoring the alternative, or leaving the question unresolved. State any assumption about exposure, sample alteration, or instrument behavior.

Limits of the interpretation

Surface coatings, tiny grains, powder mixtures, and instrument calibration can distort classifications. The observed pattern need not determine a unique history; spatial coverage and the timing of later alteration affect confidence. Name a competing history in the field report and make clear whether its rejection depends on evidence that is actually available.

Prefer several independent properties and record test conditions, sample alteration, and any irrecoverable uncertainty. §2.5 Lesson 9: Mineral stability and reaction pathways

Geological concept

Equilibrium stability depends on temperature, pressure, fluid composition, and bulk chemistry, but natural rocks may preserve metastable minerals. Distinguish an observed feature from an inferred history. Identify the material, physical scale, and duration at issue. A useful explanation should predict an additional observation that was not already used to construct the hypothesis.

Physical and chemical mechanisms

Dissolution, precipitation, oxidation, hydration, and solid-state reactions proceed when transport and reaction kinetics permit. Sketch the necessary starting conditions, pathway, and geological record. Ask whether transport, phase change, reaction, or deformation is involved and whether later processes could modify the predicted evidence.

Evidence and analysis

Reaction rims, veins, replacement textures, weathering profiles, and mineral zoning record partial changes rather than instantaneous equilibrium. Record sample provenance, measurement scale, instrument or descriptive method, and uncertainty. Where an observation can support more than one process, look for independent evidence that can discriminate between alternatives.

Field notebook — a hypothetical example

Setting: A weathering rind surrounds a feldspar-rich clast with a sharp boundary.

Competing explanations: The rind could record hydrolysis and secondary mineral growth, yet an inherited coated grain or post-depositional pore-fluid event might also produce a boundary.

Discriminating investigation: Map rind thickness across clasts, observe replacement textures in thin section, and test for chemical changes from core to edge before interpreting reaction duration.

Imagine presenting this evidence to a skeptical colleague. Specify three possible outcomes of the proposed test: supporting one explanation, favoring the alternative, or leaving the question unresolved. State any assumption about exposure, sample alteration, or instrument behavior.

Limits of the interpretation

The absence of an expected phase may reflect slow reaction rates, depleted reactants, or later erosion rather than the wrong phase diagram. The observed pattern need not determine a unique history; spatial coverage and the timing of later alteration affect confidence. Name a competing history in the field report and make clear whether its rejection depends on evidence that is actually available.

Write a conditional interpretation distinguishing predicted equilibrium products from the observed extent of reaction. §2.6 Lesson 10: Rock classification and hand-sample reasoning

Geological concept

Igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic classifications emphasize origin and texture, not only chemical composition. Distinguish an observed feature from an inferred history. Identify the material, physical scale, and duration at issue. A useful explanation should predict an additional observation that was not already used to construct the hypothesis.

Physical and chemical mechanisms

Cooling produces interlocking igneous crystals, deposition can preserve grains and layering, and solid-state recrystallization can generate new metamorphic fabric. Sketch the necessary starting conditions, pathway, and geological record. Ask whether transport, phase change, reaction, or deformation is involved and whether later processes could modify the predicted evidence.

Evidence and analysis

Grain boundaries, fossils, foliation, vesicles, rounding, sorting, and contact relations help test hypotheses about origin. Record sample provenance, measurement scale, instrument or descriptive method, and uncertainty. Where an observation can support more than one process, look for independent evidence that can discriminate between alternatives.

Field notebook — a hypothetical example

Setting: A gray rock is fine grained, weakly laminated, and lacks visible fossils.

Competing explanations: It may be mudstone, a fine ash deposit, or low-grade metamorphosed shale; none is established simply by grain size.

Discriminating investigation: Examine shards or sedimentary grading, check cleavage relative to bedding, and compare the unit's contacts with neighboring rocks.

Imagine presenting this evidence to a skeptical colleague. Specify three possible outcomes of the proposed test: supporting one explanation, favoring the alternative, or leaving the question unresolved. State any assumption about exposure, sample alteration, or instrument behavior.

Limits of the interpretation

Weathered, tectonized, and poorly exposed materials can mix diagnostic features or eliminate them. The observed pattern need not determine a unique history; spatial coverage and the timing of later alteration affect confidence. Name a competing history in the field report and make clear whether its rejection depends on evidence that is actually available.

Identify what process each visible feature supports before assigning the final rock name; allow mixed or uncertain classifications. Distinguish a classroom inference from an applied decision with health, safety, environmental, or financial consequences. §2.7 Integrated investigation 2: Minerals, Crystals, and Rock Materials — constructing and testing an explanation

Scenario and provenance. A teaching collection contains unknown grains, altered igneous hand samples, and fine sediments. Students must compare observable properties to avoid treating a single field test as decisive. Every numerical value in the accompanying datasets/unit-02.csv is fictional. The table illustrates a possible data structure and does not represent real geological measurements, monitoring instruments, mapped hazards, mineral reserves, or any named community. The first task is to decide what the data can actually answer rather than to impose a preferred scientific story on the columns.

Research question and rival models

Formulate a research question anchored to the unit's central themes: Atomic bonding and mineral definition; Silicate structures and common rock formers; Mineral properties and identification; Mineral stability and reaction pathways; Rock classification and hand-sample reasoning. Write a primary hypothesis that would explain the relationship between the first two recorded variables. Write a rival hypothesis that predicts either a weaker relationship or a different response if a third variable is controlled. For each hypothesis, identify one observation that would strongly support it and another that would challenge it. Avoid conflating association with causation; both curves may be generated by a shared, unmeasured process. Use the correct vocabulary for the kind of age, rock property, or geologic event that the variable is intended to represent.

Sampling protocol and provenance

Imagine acquiring these data through a permitted classroom field investigation, laboratory session, or secondary dataset. Define a sampling frame with a spatial or stratigraphic coordinate, then identify how selection would occur. Would samples be clustered along accessible roads, selected at regular intervals, or chosen to represent the full range of rock types? Accessibility sampling may omit the very materials that would falsify a favored explanation. A reliable protocol would document instruments, calibrations, units, replicate measurements, missing values, detection limits, coordinate reference systems, and any preparation that changes the specimen. Here those details are intentionally not actual field records; the data are simulated and should be labeled at every export.

Quantitative exploration

Read the CSV header and determine which variables can be compared dimensionally. Calculate a mean and a range for at least two numeric columns, and create a scatterplot that retains the original sample identifiers. Check whether the response changes monotonically; do not assume a straight line from a small table. Fit a simple slope only if the units and sample selection make it interpretable. For every fitted coefficient, explain what a one-unit input change would mean physically. Do not report a formal confidence interval unless the sampling assumptions and statistical method justify it. The exercise assesses the quality of inference, not the sophistication of a software package.

Perturbations and missing evidence

Repeat the analysis after removing the single point with the largest response. Then repeat after treating that point as an authentic observation whose cause is not yet known. Compare how the story changes. Consider a hidden spatial gradient, instrument drift, and an unmeasured material property as alternative explanations for a pattern. If two samples appear contradictory, inspect the provenance first. A geological contradiction may be scientifically valuable: perhaps the rock units are not equivalent, the chronology differs, or a younger event overprinted only part of the area. Construct a follow-up plan that could discriminate these possibilities without inventing field facts.

Communicating a bounded conclusion

Produce a one-page briefing with a short question, the exact assumptions, a plot, a table of key statistics, and an interpretation expressed at the scale of the data. Include three explicit limitations, such as synthetic provenance, missing spatial coverage, uncertain material properties, or simplified boundary conditions. Recommend exactly one high-value additional observation, and explain why it would be more discriminating than merely collecting many more similar samples. For a safety-related application, state that no design, permit, drilling, construction, or public risk communication should rely on a classroom simulation alone.

Synthesis and oral defense

Defend your interpretation to a colleague who did not see the raw data. First, explain the mechanism without using the apparent correlation as its sole evidence. Second, show the figure and state what is measured and what is simulated. Third, present the strongest plausible alternative. Fourth, identify whether the proposed model would extrapolate beyond observed conditions, and say why that extrapolation might fail. Finally, explain what observation or model discrepancy would make you revise your conclusion. A complete scientific answer includes both a positive claim and its boundary of applicability.

Suggested rubric

Allocate 20% to valid definitions and evidence provenance, 20% to dimensional and statistical checks, 20% to competing explanations, 20% to meaningful uncertainty and sensitivity analysis, and 20% to clarity of scientific communication. A high-scoring report need not select the most dramatic explanation. It must display the reasoning necessary for another investigator to reproduce the numerical steps and understand precisely why some questions remain unresolved. This rubric is a proposed instructional aid and can be adjusted for an institution's curriculum.

§2.8 Unit 2 synthesis, transfer, and examination workshop

The five lessons in this unit—Atomic bonding and mineral definition, Silicate structures and common rock formers, Mineral properties and identification, Mineral stability and reaction pathways, Rock classification and hand-sample reasoning—should be understood as connected components of one scientific argument rather than isolated vocabulary. The first connects observations to an initial classification; the second or third develops a physical mechanism; and the later examples test whether that mechanism has explanatory and predictive power. Construct a one-page concept diagram showing these links. Each connecting arrow must be labeled with a physical reason, a measurement, or a conditional hypothesis. Do not connect two claims simply because they are both common geology terms.

Transfer problem. Imagine a new fictional site in which only half of the observations described in the unit are available and the remaining measurements would require costly fieldwork. State one conservative interpretation that the evidence supports, and one attractive interpretation that is still unwarranted. Rank three additional data-collection activities by expected usefulness, not by apparent technical sophistication. Define how an unfavorable result would change the interpretation. The point is to practice the economics of information: expensive observations are valuable only when they can alter a real decision.

Examination problem. Choose two solved numerical examples from the unit, change one input in each, and recompute the result with clearly displayed units. Describe the limiting behavior as the input approaches zero or becomes large, and assess whether the original formula remains physically appropriate there. Connect the calculation to a field observation that could be made with commonly available instructional equipment or public datasets. Distinguish an exact mathematical solution from a geologically validated model; mathematical consistency is necessary for a model but cannot establish its empirical applicability.

Oral defense. A reviewer challenges your interpretation using a contradictory field observation. Your response should not be to dismiss the observation or to invent undocumented special circumstances. Explain how you would confirm its provenance, whether it violates a necessary model prediction, and what other evidence could settle the disagreement. State one inference that would remain valid even if your preferred mechanism turned out to be wrong. That is the kind of robust knowledge that can survive revision when new evidence is collected.
