Unit 1 — Sedimentary archives and field description

From fragments and chemical precipitates to layered records.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university sedimentology and stratigraphy courses. Eight units with five solved questions per unit and 16 interactive simulations illustrating grain settling velocities, cross-bedding paleoflow vectors, porosity loss compaction curves, accommodation-to-sediment supply ratios, and subsidence curves. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§1.1 Learning outcomes

Describe a sedimentary body using observations before assigning environment or geologic age.

§1.2 Extended unit perspective

A sedimentary section is a selective archive. Source rocks can weather for millions of years before their grains reach a depositional sink; after arrival, the same particles may be repeatedly eroded and redeposited. The geologist therefore distinguishes the time when a grain crystallized from the time when a bed accumulated. A fossil shell enclosed in mud, a detrital zircon in sandstone and a volcanic ash bed play different roles in dating. The rock also has more than one composition: framework grains, mud matrix, early marine cement and late burial cement may all record distinct sources of matter. An accurate log begins by separating these components. Students should practise sketching fabric, measuring bed thickness perpendicular to bedding, and documenting the uncertainty introduced by vegetation, talus or covered sections. A single grain-size curve can be useful, but a curve without stratigraphic position, sample mass and field context is a weak geological explanation.

§1.3 Lesson 1.1 — The sedimentary source-to-sink system

What is being explained?

Weathering liberates grains or solutes, transport sorts them, and accommodation permits preservation; sediment flux can change dramatically before the material reaches a basin. Explain which visible feature would distinguish this process from later sedimentary reworking or burial modification.

The observational record

Provenance indicators, thickness patterns, modern sediment budgets and downstream grain-size trends each sample different portions of the system. Record the scale and instrument behind each observation; do not convert a field description into a claim about ancient environment without additional evidence.

Competing interpretations

A coarse deposit may record high transport capacity, but it could instead reflect erosion of a nearby coarse source with little downstream reworking. Describe at least one predicted feature that differs between the rival and leading model, and identify a realistic observation that would distinguish them.

A discriminating investigation

Construct a sediment budget with input, temporary storage, bypass and output; compare adjacent reaches to find an unmeasured term. Specify the comparison material and observable decision rule so that a negative test outcome could change your preferred interpretation.

Field notebook and facies recognition. Write a two-column field record headed observation and inference. Enter grains, laminae, colour, contact shape and any measurable geometry in the observation column; reserve terms describing ancient rivers, shorelines or deep water for the inference column. Choose an outcrop reference scale suitable for the problem: centimetres for a ripple foreset, metres for a channel body, and kilometres for a basin map. Mark beds that cannot be observed because of cover. The same feature may be meaningful at one scale and misleading at another. Draw the simplest facies association consistent with the visible relationships and list one expected lateral equivalent. In your report do not call an exposure representative merely because it is accessible; accessibility itself may produce sampling bias.

Worked quantitative exercise 1: Net annual sediment export

Hypothetical inputs: 560 thousand tonnes supplied, 110 in temporary channel storage and 70 on floodplain; no unmeasured input. The transparent classroom expression is 560-110-70. Evaluating this expression produces 380 thousand tonnes per year. Identify the numerator and denominator when the calculation produces a percentage, and write each conversion between metres, kilometres, days, years or millions of years explicitly. If the result is a transport rate, do not describe it as an observed sedimentation rate unless storage and bypass are accounted for; if it is a thickness, identify whether erosion and compaction are represented.

Evidence limitations and a qualified conclusion

Sediment is often stored and remobilized over periods much longer than an individual event, so present-day flux is not ancient accumulation rate. Categorize the dominant uncertainty as sampling, analytical, chronological, mechanistic or preservation-related, and name the measurement that would reduce it.

Interpret preservation as filtered transport history rather than as a continuous record of everything supplied.

Practice and evidence checks

  1. Describe the sedimentary source-to-sink system using three observations that do not yet assume a depositional setting.
  2. State the physical reason the proposed mechanism would produce one of those observations, then identify a contrary observation that would count against it.
  3. Recalculate Exercise 1 after modifying one stated parameter, document units and explain sensitivity.
  4. Design an independent test using the protocol above; state a clear predicted outcome for the leading model and for its rival.
  5. Write an 80-word report that explains what is known, what remains uncertain and why the distinction matters when interpreting preserved strata.

§1.4 Lesson 1.2 — Lithologic logging and bounding surfaces

What is being explained?

An outcrop log ties bed thickness, grain size, sorting, composition, structures and contact types to a measured vertical scale. Explain which visible feature would distinguish this process from later sedimentary reworking or burial modification.

The observational record

Documented erosional contacts, sharp but conformable contacts, gradational transitions and diagenetic boundaries carry distinct implications. Record the scale and instrument behind each observation; do not convert a field description into a claim about ancient environment without additional evidence.

Competing interpretations

A colour break or cemented ledge can resemble a depositional boundary although it may arise from weathering or selective diagenesis. Describe at least one predicted feature that differs between the rival and leading model, and identify a realistic observation that would distinguish them.

A discriminating investigation

Log the same interval twice using independent observers; test interobserver variation in bed boundaries and facies assignments. Specify the comparison material and observable decision rule so that a negative test outcome could change your preferred interpretation.

Measurement and dimensional reasoning. For the numerical component, write the governing relation and then translate every quantity into a unit consistent with that relation. State whether thickness is true stratigraphic thickness or apparent width, whether grain size is a mass-weighted or number-weighted distribution, and whether a flux represents sediment delivered or rock preserved. A point estimate may hide a large physical uncertainty. Recalculate after changing one assumed input by ten percent, and distinguish the mathematical sensitivity from the real variability of that input. Present units in the plotted axis titles rather than only in figure captions. A sensible arithmetic result is not sufficient evidence that the equation is valid at a particular outcrop. Discuss whether equilibrium, uniform flow, conserved grain mass or constant deposition has been assumed.

Prefer a repeatable log with uncertainty annotations over an elaborate environmental story without primary descriptions.

§1.5 Lesson 1.3 — Clastic textures and grain-size statistics

What is being explained?

Hydraulic sorting, abrasion, fragmentation and selective preservation influence the distribution of particle sizes and shapes. Explain which visible feature would distinguish this process from later sedimentary reworking or burial modification.

The observational record

Sieve curves, laser-diffraction distributions, petrographic counts and grain-shape measurements reveal related but nonidentical sediment properties. Record the scale and instrument behind each observation; do not convert a field description into a claim about ancient environment without additional evidence.

Competing interpretations

A well-sorted sample may have experienced sustained hydraulic selection, aeolian recycling or repeated shoreline reworking. Describe at least one predicted feature that differs between the rival and leading model, and identify a realistic observation that would distinguish them.

A discriminating investigation

Compare median, percentiles, skewness and counting uncertainty from replicate subsamples rather than relying on one visual adjective. Specify the comparison material and observable decision rule so that a negative test outcome could change your preferred interpretation.

Hypothesis competition and counterexample. Begin with a pair of incompatible explanations that both seem plausible from the first observation. Instead of collecting more features that merely illustrate the preferred story, identify the measurement on which the predictions disagree. Consider that one hypothesis may involve sediment transport while the other involves later erosion or cementation; these are not mutually exclusive unless the timing is explicitly constrained. Record the expected observation under each explanation before seeing any additional dataset. Evidence that fails to discriminate is still descriptive evidence, but it does not rank the competing models. Where a third process could imitate both, give that possibility a separate row in the reasoning table. The final verdict may be that present evidence supports only a family of environments or ages.

Sampling and measurement techniques differ in shape assumptions, fines recovery and mass versus volume weighting. Categorize the dominant uncertainty as sampling, analytical, chronological, mechanistic or preservation-related, and name the measurement that would reduce it.

Treat texture as a partial process indicator requiring sedimentary structures and context.

§1.6 Lesson 1.4 — Primary sedimentary structures

What is being explained?

Bedforms migrate under moving fluids; ripples, cross-strata, graded beds and mud cracks encode flow or exposure processes at different scales. Explain which visible feature would distinguish this process from later sedimentary reworking or burial modification.

The observational record

Cross-bed foreset angles, ripple asymmetry, graded layers and desiccation polygons provide independently describable geometric features. Record the scale and instrument behind each observation; do not convert a field description into a claim about ancient environment without additional evidence.

Competing interpretations

Tectonic shear bands or soft-sediment deformation may mimic inclined laminae and obscure the primary way-up direction. Describe at least one predicted feature that differs between the rival and leading model, and identify a realistic observation that would distinguish them.

A discriminating investigation

Measure palaeocurrent azimuths on multiple undisturbed beds and compare them with channel axes or shoreline indicators. Specify the comparison material and observable decision rule so that a negative test outcome could change your preferred interpretation.

Sampling design and data integrity. Prepare an explicit sampling protocol: what interval is the population of interest, how will representative sites be selected, which fraction of the sediment is included, and what precision is possible? Replicate critical counts because sedimentary deposits are patchy in both space and time. A sieve sample, thin section and borehole log observe different volumes and may not sample equivalent properties. Record locations and orientation without inventing precision that was not measured. For destructive sampling, respect collection permits and institution rules. Store the original readings separately from any normalized percentages so that the transformation can be inspected later. Treat missing data as missing, not as zeros, and write down every exclusion criterion before producing an attractive final plot.

If the result is a transport rate, do not describe it as an observed sedimentation rate unless storage and bypass are accounted for; if it is a thickness, identify whether erosion and compaction are represented.

Evidence limitations and a qualified conclusion

Limited bedding-plane exposure and compaction alter angles; individual structures seldom indicate a unique flow regime. Categorize the dominant uncertainty as sampling, analytical, chronological, mechanistic or preservation-related, and name the measurement that would reduce it.

Report structure geometry before inferring current direction, transport medium or setting.

§1.7 Lesson 1.5 — Sediment composition and provenance

What is being explained?

Framework grains, matrix, cement and heavy minerals reflect source composition plus weathering, transport and diagenetic filtering. Explain which visible feature would distinguish this process from later sedimentary reworking or burial modification.

The observational record

Detrital zircon populations, QFL counts, rock fragments and resistant heavy mineral suites can illuminate sediment supply. Record the scale and instrument behind each observation; do not convert a field description into a claim about ancient environment without additional evidence.

Competing interpretations

Similar quartz-rich sandstone can be generated by multiple source terrains after prolonged recycling and selective dissolution. Describe at least one predicted feature that differs between the rival and leading model, and identify a realistic observation that would distinguish them.

A discriminating investigation

Combine framework point counts with stratigraphic changes and one independent geochemical or geochronological constraint. Specify the comparison material and observable decision rule so that a negative test outcome could change your preferred interpretation.

Comparative synthesis and communication. Move from a single log to a lateral comparison. Propose which properties ought to remain similar between neighbouring sections and which may change as environments migrate. A marker bed is only a reliable time line when evidence establishes its correlation; similar sandstone colour is not such evidence. Choose a visual representation that distinguishes observed boundaries from extrapolated ones, perhaps using solid versus dashed lines and a brief uncertainty legend. Present at least one alternative explanation in the figure caption rather than burying disagreement in the final paragraph. A coherent story should preserve contradictions instead of quietly removing inconvenient beds. In the conclusion identify the smallest claim that would remain valid if your preferred process model were overturned.

Evidence limitations and a qualified conclusion

Hydraulic concentration and chemical instability change mineral abundance independently of source proportions. Categorize the dominant uncertainty as sampling, analytical, chronological, mechanistic or preservation-related, and name the measurement that would reduce it.

Separate original source signal from the transport and burial filters that changed it.

§1.8 Integrated research studio: Provenance versus reworking

Prepare a measured log of a synthetic 120 m section in which quartz content increases upward while coarse beds become less common. Develop two histories: increasing recycling versus changing source terrain. Decide where you would sample heavy minerals, where a detrital-age sample is most useful, and how bed geometry could challenge either account. Supply a grain-size histogram, a framework grain-count table and a diagram showing the inferred sediment pathway.

Practical workflow

A teaching dataset is a deliberately simplified set of observations with enough metadata to support a reproducible calculation. It is not evidence from a named real basin. Before graphing the numbers, decide which independent variable you think orders the processes and which observations might instead reflect changes in sampling position. Draw uncertainty markers or ranges where possible, even when the figures are hypothetical. A single good-fitting curve should be treated as a question: Which physical assumptions created it, and what independent data would disprove it? The submission must contain both a physical interpretation and a statement of one competing explanation. If a student uses a generative model or plotting program, they should preserve the original measurements and equations so the result can be independently reproduced.

The first deliverable is a clean measured-section or experimental-data table with a metadata line defining measurement units. The second is a graph or annotated cross-section that does not confuse elevation and stratigraphic thickness. The third is a two-model comparison in which each model must make at least one concrete additional prediction. The fourth is a brief calculation checked by an independent method and accompanied by a description of uncertainty. The fifth is a research note that states what cannot be resolved without additional evidence. Assign confidence to individual inferences, not to the diagram as a whole.

Data critique and transfer

A reproducible student submission must preserve a copy of the raw values, retain any derived normalized quantities separately and state which values were inferred rather than measured. Consider the possibility that the most accessible outcrop or easiest-to-analyse core was systematically unrepresentative. Plan an independent observation that would most sharply reduce uncertainty. If the best test is not feasible, present the honest limit of the inference rather than substituting a plausible-looking numerical value. This is especially important when educational methods are adapted to real land-use or subsurface questions.

§1.9 Unit 1 synthesis and assessment

Across these five topics, the central scientific skill is to distinguish a physically possible depositional interpretation from one that is specifically supported by evidence. Submit all five numerical exercises with units, the integrated research studio, and a correlation or process diagram that uses line styles to separate observed boundaries from inferred ones. Cite the source of external classification terminology when appropriate. A brief oral defence should explain which sedimentary observation would best falsify the preferred interpretation, how a missing interval affects the reconstruction, and whether the strongest evidence is geometric, compositional, chronological or process-based.

Self-assessment questions

  • Which observations in this unit are primary depositional properties, and which could be modified by burial or structural deformation?
  • Which quantities are measured directly and which are model-dependent estimates?
  • Where would another measured section most effectively distinguish competing hypotheses?
  • Does the proposed correlation connect similar rocks, synchronous time markers or a hypothesized environmental boundary?
  • What remains valid if the preferred interpretation is wrong?
