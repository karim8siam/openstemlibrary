§6.1 Central question: How do bulk conductivity, dielectric properties and induced polarization constrain fluids and structures?

Fictional unit investigation: A hypothetical valley aquifer is imaged by resistivity electrodes, frequency-domain induction and radar lines. Clay, salinity and moisture have overlapping responses; students must avoid declaring contamination from conductivity alone.

§6.2 Geophysical foundation and the measurement-to-model chain

Electrical resistivity is a bulk property influenced by interconnected water, ions, temperature, pore geometry, clay surface conduction and mineral phases. Four-electrode apparent resistivity is not the resistivity at a single sampled depth. Electromagnetic surveys sense conductivity through inductive coupling, and radar travel time depends on effective electromagnetic velocity and attenuation. Different array configurations sample different sensitivity regions, so apparent contrasts require geometry-aware interpretation. Saltwater and clay can both yield high conductivity; dry rock, air voids and clean sand may give resistive signatures. Instrument coupling, buried utilities, cultural noise and inversion regularization add further uncertainty.

§6.3 Five lessons: hypotheses, observables and field checks

For each lesson the forward physics and observation type are explicit. The lesson is complete only when the student can name a competing explanation, check units in the worked calculation and explain a measurement that could reject the preferred interpretation. Every stated numerical value is invented for this textbook; none is a field station or an operational parameter.

§6.4 Resistivity principles and apparent resistivity

The physical mechanism and its measurable consequence

The measured potential difference generated by injected current is converted using an electrode geometry factor to apparent resistivity. The result is an effective measure of a sensitivity volume, not a local rock sample. Contact resistance and near-surface heterogeneity affect data quality.

Acquisition protocol and observable variables

Record electrode coordinates, current, voltage, polarity reversal, stacking error, contact resistance and reciprocal measurements.

The inverse ambiguity: alternative explanations

Clay conduction, saline water or metallic mineralization can each lower apparent resistivity; high values may indicate dry soils or lithology changes.

Worked calculation: synthetic classroom data

Question — Wenner apparent resistivity. Wenner spacing 5 m, V=0.12 V, I=0.01 A; compute 2πa V/I.

Explicit computation: 2math.pi5*0.12/0.01 = 376.9911 ohm m. §6.5 Electrical resistivity tomography and inversion

The physical mechanism and its measurable consequence

ERT estimates a resistivity distribution from many electrode combinations using numerical forward models and regularization. Depth of investigation is gradual and sensitivity decreases, not a sharp maximum depth. Smooth and blocky inverse priors can produce different geometries from the same data.

Acquisition protocol and observable variables

Compare observed and predicted apparent resistivity, inversion residual maps, reciprocal error and model smoothness. Mask poorly resolved cells.

The inverse ambiguity: alternative explanations

An appealing low-resistivity plume may be an inversion artefact, clay layer or unmodelled topography; it is not proof of contamination.

§6.6 Electromagnetic induction and skin depth

The physical mechanism and its measurable consequence

Alternating fields induce electric currents whose secondary response depends on conductivity, magnetic permeability, frequency, geometry and coupling. Simple skin-depth approximations assume a uniform half-space and become misleading in strongly layered or anisotropic media.

Acquisition protocol and observable variables

Record frequency, transmitter–receiver separation, coil orientation, phase, height and calibration standard. Check nearby metallic objects.

The inverse ambiguity: alternative explanations

A conducting utility can overshadow a regional aquifer signal. A shallow conductive layer may shield deeper information.

§6.7 Ground-penetrating radar and dielectric velocity

The physical mechanism and its measurable consequence

Radar reflections arise mainly from contrasts in dielectric properties, and subsurface travel time must be converted to depth using velocity. Penetration varies dramatically with conductivity, moisture, frequency and antenna coupling; conductive clay strongly limits it.

Acquisition protocol and observable variables

Collect common-offset profiles with time-zero calibration, trace spacing and antenna frequency; estimate velocity from hyperbolae or a measured reflector depth.

The inverse ambiguity: alternative explanations

A hyperbola may come from a pipe, stone or diffraction rather than a continuous stratigraphic horizon. Depth can be severely wrong when velocity varies.

§6.8 Polarization, fluids and discrimination

The physical mechanism and its measurable consequence

Induced polarization records delayed electrical response that can be influenced by pore geometry, metallic particles, clays and fluid chemistry. Self-potential and streaming potentials can accompany subsurface water movement but have multiple sources. Multi-physics interpretation can be helpful without being decisive.

Acquisition protocol and observable variables

Measure time windows or phase spectra, background drift, salinity, temperature and field control data. Compare against direct borehole water samples where appropriate.

The inverse ambiguity: alternative explanations

A strong chargeability anomaly is not automatically an ore body, and low resistivity is not an unambiguous salinity estimate.

§6.9 Research clinic — resistivity is not a water-quality laboratory

Imagine a valley where one electrode line finds a conductive zone. One hypothesis is saline groundwater, another is clay-rich sediment, and a third is a cultural conductor such as buried metal. All can lower apparent resistivity, and an inverse model might display a blue lens regardless of which interpretation is correct. A resistivity map is not a chemical analysis of water and cannot establish contamination by itself.

The measured voltage/current ratio depends on electrode geometry; the familiar Wenner formula converts it to apparent resistivity for an ideal array. Because the sensitivity kernel occupies a broad volume, changing electrode spacing changes depth sensitivity but not in the manner of a perfectly focused probe. Inversion imposes smoothness or other constraints to produce a model whose predicted apparent resistivities resemble data. A different regularization can yield a different-looking structure with similar residuals. Check reciprocal measurements, electrode contact and topographic treatment before trusting the inverse image.

Electromagnetic data provide another sensitivity, but field conductivity still depends on clay, water chemistry, connected porosity, frequency and geometry. A simple skin-depth equation is a useful scaling relation under a homogeneous nonmagnetic half-space. It is not a guaranteed exploration depth in a layered conductive valley. High-frequency ground-penetrating radar may be attenuated so rapidly in wet clay that a blank section is evidence of measurement limitation, not absence of an interface.

Design an integrated survey to distinguish the three hypotheses with minimal disturbance. One shallow borehole may reveal clay type, while fluid sampling directly tests dissolved ions. Independently map buried infrastructure and repeat measurements in a second season if moisture may change. The final interpretation should map where conductivity is inferred, where fluid salinity is actually measured, and where model resolution is inadequate. Conflating those layers creates an actionable-sounding but unsupported environmental claim.

§6.10 Unit investigation: reproducible synthetic dataset and experimental design

Research aim. The invented scenario is: A hypothetical valley aquifer is imaged by resistivity electrodes, frequency-domain induction and radar lines. Clay, salinity and moisture have overlapping responses; students must avoid declaring contamination from conductivity alone. Treat datasets/unit-06.csv as forty fictional rows deliberately designed to exercise quality control and model criticism. Read the file header before drawing any map. Plot the two relevant measured fields from unit 6; display the quality_flag column rather than deleting all flagged stations silently. The synthetic table contains no geographic coordinate reference system or real site identifiers and cannot be used to identify a place.

Part A — raw measurements and dimensions. Read each column as a quantity, not as a decorative label. For electrical, electromagnetic and ground-penetrating methods, check whether the stated units are compatible with its equation and record which columns represent observed signals, instrument corrections, independent controls or model-derived quantities. Identify missing information such as date, site geology, true coordinate system, instrument orientation or calibration. If those omissions prevent a physical inversion, state the limitation and limit the task to transparent exploratory analysis. This is a stronger result than assigning fictitious precision.

Part B — repeat and quality flags. Count the rows marked review. Compare summary statistics, plotted gradients and model parameters with all forty records against the subset that passes the flag. Document every change; flags indicate inspection priority, not a universal mandate to delete. Check whether questionable values cluster along position or signal amplitude, because exclusions can introduce bias. If the noise appears correlated, do not assume a root-sum-square independent-error formula without a justification.

Part C — model competition. Describe two geophysical models that explain the synthetic observations in electrical, electromagnetic and ground-penetrating methods; they must differ in physical property, geometry or forcing rather than just parameter precision. For each hypothesis make an observable prediction about station response, uncertainty pattern, external control or independent method. Select one new measurement that would best discriminate them, and explain its predicted signal under both hypotheses. A new dense sample from the same short profile may add less independent information than a crossing line or sample.

Part D — numerical sensitivity. Recompute one of the unit worked cases with the principal input increased by 10% and decreased by 10%. Check if the response is linear, inverse, logarithmic, quadratic or subject to another nonlinearity. Show a table with input, computed output, unit and physical assumption. Do not claim a sensitivity of the geological interpretation itself if only the toy forward equation was changed; the inverse solution may have a much broader family of possibilities.

Part E — out-of-sample prediction and reporting. Split the synthetic observations by physical position, retaining a contiguous group as a blind holdout. Fit a deliberately simple forward trend to remaining rows and predict the held-out quantity without retuning. Compare predictive error with a baseline that uses a constant mean. State why geographic separation can be a stronger test than random point withholding. Report all transformations, quality flags, fitted parameters, residual plots and failure modes in a compact methods appendix.

Map the chain of evidence from sensor to interpretation; highlight an uncertainty not reducible by extra decimal places. In your conclusion, indicate which measurement you would acquire next if the synthetic experiment were replaced with licensed real-world data. Keep any real data sourced from the references separate from these forty invented rows.

Suggested report. Submit a transparent CSV-reading script or spreadsheet calculation, one raw-data figure, a sensitivity plot, two candidate geological explanations, a withheld-test result and a one-page uncertainty ledger. Grade on conceptual correctness (25%), numerical units and reproducibility (25%), alternate hypotheses (20%), validation logic (20%) and scientific communication (10%). Never reward a map for appearing more certain than its underlying observations.

§6.11 End-of-unit reflection and problems

  1. Identify which part of electrical, electromagnetic and ground-penetrating methods would remain ambiguous even if measurement noise were reduced to zero. Explain how the survey geometry or parameter relationship creates that ambiguity.
  1. Find a case where a large change in the observed signal would not imply a proportionally large change in target size. Write the forward relation, name fixed parameters, and state why inversion differs from prediction.
  1. Propose a new survey that would risk rejecting the preferred geological interpretation. Its value should be based on discriminating predictions rather than its technological sophistication.
  1. Draw a data lineage chart from raw instrument data through correction, inversion and geological claim. Underline any operation that assumes a material property not measured in the original dataset.
  1. Explain why the synthetic data and interactive sliders are educational examples and cannot support emergency warnings, ground-investigation certification, resource appraisal or environmental enforcement.
