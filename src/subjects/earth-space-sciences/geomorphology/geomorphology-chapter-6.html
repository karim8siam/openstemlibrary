Unit 6 — Coasts, deltas and sea-level change

Why do shorelines retreat, advance or change shape?

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university geomorphology courses. Eight units with five solved questions per unit and 16 interactive simulations illustrating hillslope slope-flux relations, critical shear velocity, glacial ice discharge, coastal wave power, and steady-state landscape relief. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§6.1 Guiding scientific question

Why do shorelines retreat, advance or change shape?

§6.2 Unit perspective

A coastline is a moving interface among waves, tides, rivers, coastal sediment, land elevation and sea level. A beach can narrow because of reduced sediment delivery even if mean sea level remains steady. Relative sea-level rise, by contrast, combines changes in ocean level with local uplift or subsidence. Delta evolution depends on river sediment supply, offshore dispersal, wave and tidal reworking and accommodation space. A single shoreline photograph is insufficient to estimate a long-term recession trend: tide stage, storm timing and seasonal beach cycles must be considered. A sediment budget identifies inputs, outputs and storage, but boundary choices and unmeasured offshore losses can dominate the residual. Shoreline hardening may protect a particular structure while redistributing erosion or eliminating space for wetland migration. Educational calculators can demonstrate sensitivities, but cannot replace survey-grade wave, tide, sediment, storm and engineering information.

Learning outcomes and scale discipline

By the end of this unit, students should be able to describe at least five independent landform or sediment observations, demonstrate one dimensionally consistent quantitative calculation, design a comparative measurement, and distinguish an interpretation from the observations used to support it. Practice viewing the same landform in a map, a section and a material description. Keep an explicit column for phenomena that are absent, unobserved or only assumed. At the finest scale, a few grain contacts may govern transport; at larger scales, inherited valley geometry and event histories control the response. Switching scale is legitimate only after specifying which variables remain meaningful. At the start of the field exercise, make a drawing of repeat profile, mapped shoreline and bounded littoral sediment budget; list which parts would be measured directly and which reconstructed from other evidence.

The unit's measurement choices should include wave direction, tidal datum, survey dates and sediment sources; the strongest report also checks registered surveys under comparable shoreline definitions. Even a carefully derived rate does not establish its formative mechanism unless the sedimentary or stratigraphic evidence agrees. Interpret changes in the context of the process and observation interval, and use at least two sources of independent evidence. Most useful scientific uncertainties are not just numerical instrument errors: researchers must also consider incorrect boundaries, missing events, biased sample sites, mistaken ages and incomplete constitutive laws.

§6.3 Five topic lessons and worked investigations

§6.4 Wave action, swash and beach profiles

Process mechanism and scale

Wave shoaling, breaking and swash move sediment across the nearshore; profile changes depend on wave climate, grain size and sediment exchange. Draw a control volume showing forcing, material transfers and storage. Name the scale and timescale of the proposed process; landform shape alone does not reveal either.

Observable evidence

Beach transects, wave buoy records, storm berms and grain-size maps constrain seasonal variability. Record wave direction, tidal datum, survey dates and sediment sources. In the diagram of repeat profile, mapped shoreline and bounded littoral sediment budget, label measured features separately from inferred histories. Provide survey dates, scale and a detection threshold.

Alternatives and field tests

A beach profile following a winter storm may look erosional even if the annual sediment budget is stable. Repeat transects at matching datum and tidal conditions while logging wave timing. Consider beach seasonal exchange mistaken for permanent coastal erosion. Identify one independently measurable outcome that would distinguish the two interpretations, and one negative result that would undermine the leading explanation.

Worked quantitative example — Wave-energy height sensitivity

Given. Compare idealized heights of 2.0 m and 1.0 m at fixed period and water density. Calculation: (2 / 1)². Result: 4 ratio. Work through the units before using the output to discuss Earth-surface history. The energy per unit surface area of deep-water gravity waves scales with squared height. Wave power and runup depend on period, direction and bathymetry. A correct result from an incorrect conceptual model is not evidence that the process operated. If the answer is a rate, indicate the averaging interval; if it is a geometric ratio, identify the scale and measurement direction; if it is a budget, give the boundary and the sign convention.

Research procedure and critical interpretation

Document datum consistency; independently check sediment cell boundaries. Assess sensitivity to tidal timing and verify land-level motion. Record each raw observation, units, uncertainty and assumptions before model fitting.

One beach slope does not uniquely establish longshore drift direction. Report whether the measured shoreline is high-water line, vegetation limit or mapped contour. A high-resolution shape map without ages and a dated deposit without spatial coverage offer different constraints. Explain how they could be combined for wave action, swash and beach profiles without overclaiming a process history. Identify one further test.

§6.6 Relative sea level and coastal land motion

Process mechanism and scale

Relative sea level reflects ocean-level change plus local ground subsidence or uplift; tides and storms add short-period water-level variability. Observable evidence

§6.7 Delta growth, avulsion and sediment trapping

Process mechanism and scale

Deltas build where river sediment enters a receiving basin, but waves, tides, subsidence and upstream dams alter storage and lobe development. Draw a control volume showing forcing, material transfers and storage. Name the scale and timescale of the proposed process; landform shape alone does not reveal either.

Observable evidence

Distributary patterns, mouth bars, dated delta-front strata and satellite imagery constrain sediment routing. Record wave direction, tidal datum, survey dates and sediment sources. Alternatives and field tests

A retreating delta edge may occur despite local channel deposition if offshore losses exceed supply. §6.8 Cliff erosion, coastal hazards and adaptation

Process mechanism and scale

Wave attack, weathering, rainfall-driven failures and geology combine in cliff retreat, often producing episodic rather than uniform change. Draw a control volume showing forcing, material transfers and storage. Name the scale and timescale of the proposed process; landform shape alone does not reveal either.

Observable evidence

Historic cliff-top positions, debris aprons, discontinuities and groundwater seepage constrain drivers. Record wave direction, tidal datum, survey dates and sediment sources. In the diagram of repeat profile, mapped shoreline and bounded littoral sediment budget, label measured features separately from inferred histories. Provide survey dates, scale and a detection threshold.

Alternatives and field tests

An apparent cliff retreat line may be a mapping artifact caused by changing image angle and vegetation. Register multiple maps to stable control points and compare rates across distinct observation intervals. Consider beach seasonal exchange mistaken for permanent coastal erosion. §6.9 Integrated field-and-model capstone — Shoreline budget with conflicting indicators

Given aerial shoreline positions, small tide-gauge trends and a simplified coastal sediment ledger, calculate mean shoreline displacement and net littoral storage. Explain how a beach can accrete locally in a sediment-deficit cell, and how subsidence modifies relative sea level. Propose a monitoring plan before recommending a shore protection intervention.

Protocol and evidence allocation

Write a project proposal that names the study question, a defined sampling region, hypotheses H1 and H2 and three observations that could discriminate them. Build an input table with at least twelve synthetic measurements; record a unit, identifier, uncertainty class and data source for every row. Design one transect, one temporal repeat survey and one test using a different kind of evidence. State how the field team would avoid confirmation bias: preregister predicted outcomes, assign a second mapper to identify landforms independently and blind the initial data processing to the favored model. Any unmeasured quantity must be marked as an assumption. Base the arithmetic on the numerical cases in the unit, but do not present the invented data as actual measurements.

In the analysis, start with a mass or elevation balance rather than a purely visual explanation. If the balance does not close, label the residual and list processes that may lie outside the boundary, including sediment stored in a channel, transport at an unmonitored exit and error in estimated density or surface area. Separate interpretation of rate from reconstruction of age and chronology. An apparently consistent average rate can conceal long inactive periods punctuated by large storms, landslides or channel switching. Explore a parameter range wide enough to expose this sensitivity. Explain what observation would tell you whether the residual is an actual physical loss or a measurement artifact.

Reporting template and peer review

The final report should contain: a title that describes an inquiry rather than a conclusion; data source and acquisition notes; a map or section with scale; a table of raw observations; a short derivation with units; alternative interpretations; uncertainty and detection limits; and an honest conclusion identifying what the exercise cannot show. A peer reviewer should trace one plotted value back to an original input without assistance. Report uncertainty in both physical data and causal attribution. Describe how sampling locations could bias the result, and identify one independent validation datum that was not used when choosing the model. Treat a good fit as a starting point for further testing, not proof of a unique historical trajectory.

Assessment prompts and rubric

Interpretation. Provide two competing process histories consistent with the invented evidence, predict one differentiating field measurement, and identify which uncertainties could reverse the preferred interpretation. Quantitative reasoning. Recompute the selected unit case in SI units, vary two inputs independently, and interpret signs, magnitudes and nonlinearities. Evidence ethics. Identify all fabricated datasets and explain why they must never be described as real hazard, glacial or coastal monitoring records. Review standard. Full credit requires correct units, reproducible methods, a mass or geometry balance, explicit chronology limits and an alternative interpretation; numerical correctness alone is insufficient.

§6.10 Unit synthesis

Return to the guiding question: Why do shorelines retreat, advance or change shape? Make a process diagram contrasting an observable pattern with a process that could produce it. Now add a second process capable of leaving a similar pattern. Underline measurements that would distinguish the two. Ask whether the lesson's calculations predict the type of change measured by those instruments or only provide a scale for discussing it. The emphasis throughout geomorphology is not on naming spectacular landforms but on reconstructing defensible histories, with uncertainties, from repeatable measurements and physical consistency.
