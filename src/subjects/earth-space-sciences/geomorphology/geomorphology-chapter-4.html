Unit 4 — Glacial and periglacial landscapes

Which landforms and sediment records diagnose cold-region processes?

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university geomorphology courses. Eight units with five solved questions per unit and 16 interactive simulations illustrating hillslope slope-flux relations, critical shear velocity, glacial ice discharge, coastal wave power, and steady-state landscape relief. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§4.1 Guiding scientific question

Which landforms and sediment records diagnose cold-region processes?

§4.2 Unit perspective

Glaciers deform and slide under their own weight while moving water and sediment through ice, on its surface and at its bed. The position of the terminus cannot be equated with ice-flow direction or used as a single-year mass-balance measurement. Retreat may occur while every point of the glacier continues to move downhill. Valley shape, striated bedrock, till, outwash and moraines collectively support glacial interpretation; individual features may be ambiguous, reworked or modified by later rivers. Ice-marginal lakes and sediment dams can produce hazards, but maps alone do not predict failure timing. In periglacial settings, repeated freezing and thawing, thermal contraction, thaw settlement and soil frost alter the ground even where no glacier exists. Rates depend on climate, ice content, sediment textures and ecological cover. The same valley may preserve several phases of cold-climate activity and modern processes. Students should practice disentangling a sequence of events using crosscutting relationships and multiple independent dates.

Learning outcomes and scale discipline

By the end of this unit, students should be able to describe at least five independent landform or sediment observations, demonstrate one dimensionally consistent quantitative calculation, design a comparative measurement, and distinguish an interpretation from the observations used to support it. Practice viewing the same landform in a map, a section and a material description. Keep an explicit column for phenomena that are absent, unobserved or only assumed. At the finest scale, a few grain contacts may govern transport; at larger scales, inherited valley geometry and event histories control the response. Switching scale is legitimate only after specifying which variables remain meaningful. At the start of the field exercise, make a drawing of mapped moraine ridge, till section, outwash terrace and dated surface; list which parts would be measured directly and which reconstructed from other evidence.

The unit's measurement choices should include surface stake location, snow density and sedimentary facies; the strongest report also checks ice-contact facies and independent chronology. Even a carefully derived rate does not establish its formative mechanism unless the sedimentary or stratigraphic evidence agrees. Interpret changes in the context of the process and observation interval, and use at least two sources of independent evidence. Most useful scientific uncertainties are not just numerical instrument errors: researchers must also consider incorrect boundaries, missing events, biased sample sites, mistaken ages and incomplete constitutive laws.

§4.3 Five topic lessons and worked investigations

§4.4 Lesson 4.1 — Glacier mass balance and terminus response

Process mechanism and scale

Accumulation adds snow and ice; ablation removes mass by melt, sublimation and calving where applicable. Ice transfer creates a lag between mass balance and terminus position. Draw a control volume showing forcing, material transfers and storage. Name the scale and timescale of the proposed process; landform shape alone does not reveal either.

Observable evidence

Stake readings, snow pits, firn densities and remote sensing of surface elevation track different parts of the glacier system. Record surface stake location, snow density and sedimentary facies. In the diagram of mapped moraine ridge, till section, outwash terrace and dated surface, label measured features separately from inferred histories. Provide survey dates, scale and a detection threshold.

Alternatives and field tests

A glacier may retreat while ice continues to flow downhill, so retreat is not reversal of motion. Calculate annual mass balance in water equivalent for an invented set of stakes, then compare with mapped snout movement. Consider a landslide ridge misidentified as a moraine. Identify one independently measurable outcome that would distinguish the two interpretations, and one negative result that would undermine the leading explanation.

Worked quantitative example — Annual ice surface mass balance

Given. Annual accumulation .95 m water equivalent and annual ablation 1.20 m water equivalent. Calculation: 0.95 − 1.20. Result: -0.25 m w.e./yr. Work through the units before using the output to discuss Earth-surface history. The negative sign means net surface ice-equivalent loss over the chosen balance year. Terminus movement is an integrated dynamic response, not this balance. A correct result from an incorrect conceptual model is not evidence that the process operated. State which measured variable would need independent verification. If the answer is a rate, indicate the averaging interval; if it is a geometric ratio, identify the scale and measurement direction; if it is a budget, give the boundary and the sign convention.

Research procedure and critical interpretation

Document balance-year boundaries; independently check sediment facies. Assess sensitivity to surface versus basal movement and verify reworked material. Record each raw observation, units, uncertainty and assumptions before model fitting.

Sparse stakes may miss wind redistribution, avalanches and elevation-dependent gradients. Report balance period and density conversion; avoid diagnosing mass change from terminus position alone. A high-resolution shape map without ages and a dated deposit without spatial coverage offer different constraints. Explain how they could be combined for glacier mass balance and terminus response without overclaiming a process history. Identify one further test.

§4.5 Lesson 4.2 — Ice motion, deformation and basal sliding

Process mechanism and scale

Ice moves by internal deformation and, where conditions permit, sliding and sediment deformation at the bed; velocity varies across and down glacier. Draw a control volume showing forcing, material transfers and storage. Name the scale and timescale of the proposed process; landform shape alone does not reveal either.

Observable evidence

Repeated survey stakes, surface crevasses and radar bed imagery constrain deformation and geometry. Record surface stake location, snow density and sedimentary facies. In the diagram of mapped moraine ridge, till section, outwash terrace and dated surface, label measured features separately from inferred histories. Provide survey dates, scale and a detection threshold.

Alternatives and field tests

Fast surface motion may partly reflect seasonal water-pressure changes rather than a permanently higher driving stress. Compare displacement at three stakes with distance from glacier edge and measurement dates. Consider a landslide ridge misidentified as a moraine. Identify one independently measurable outcome that would distinguish the two interpretations, and one negative result that would undermine the leading explanation.

§4.6 Lesson 4.3 — Glacial erosion and sediment transport

Process mechanism and scale

Abrasion polishes and striates rock while quarrying removes blocks; sediment can move within, under and atop ice. Draw a control volume showing forcing, material transfers and storage. Name the scale and timescale of the proposed process; landform shape alone does not reveal either.

Observable evidence

Striations, roche moutonnée asymmetry, erratic lithology and till fabric provide complementary evidence. Record surface stake location, snow density and sedimentary facies. Provide survey dates, scale and a detection threshold.

Alternatives and field tests

A polished bedrock surface may have been modified by later streams or human abrasion. Measure two aligned bedrock striation populations and compare with independently mapped erratic source regions. §4.7 Lesson 4.4 — Moraines and glaciofluvial sequences

Process mechanism and scale

Ice margins can concentrate unsorted sediment in moraines, while meltwater sorts sand and gravel into outwash and terraces. Draw a control volume showing forcing, material transfers and storage. Name the scale and timescale of the proposed process; landform shape alone does not reveal either.

Observable evidence

Stratigraphic contacts, clast roundness, bedding and moraine geometry constrain ice-contact and river deposition. Record surface stake location, snow density and sedimentary facies. In the diagram of mapped moraine ridge, till section, outwash terrace and dated surface, label measured features separately from inferred histories. Provide survey dates, scale and a detection threshold.

Alternatives and field tests

A ridge along a valley wall may be a landslide deposit rather than a lateral moraine. Log a measured section for till matrix, bedded sand and deformation structures; map ridge continuity. Consider a landslide ridge misidentified as a moraine. Identify one independently measurable outcome that would distinguish the two interpretations, and one negative result that would undermine the leading explanation.

§4.8 Lesson 4.5 — Permafrost and frost-related landforms

Process mechanism and scale

Ground ice, thermal contraction and seasonal freezing alter volume and strength, promoting frost heave, thaw settlement and patterned ground. Draw a control volume showing forcing, material transfers and storage. Name the scale and timescale of the proposed process; landform shape alone does not reveal either.

Observable evidence

Ice-wedge casts, sorted circles, solifluction lobes and temperature profiles preserve related but distinct processes. Record surface stake location, snow density and sedimentary facies. In the diagram of mapped moraine ridge, till section, outwash terrace and dated surface, label measured features separately from inferred histories. Provide survey dates, scale and a detection threshold.

Alternatives and field tests

Polygonal cracks in drying lake mud can resemble thermal-contraction polygons. Compare polygon scale and subsurface structure with thermal and moisture records. Consider a landslide ridge misidentified as a moraine. Identify one independently measurable outcome that would distinguish the two interpretations, and one negative result that would undermine the leading explanation.

§4.9 Integrated field-and-model capstone — Cold landscape history without a single-date shortcut

Reconstruct glacial position from three moraines and associated facies, then test whether one ridge could be a rock avalanche. Compute stake velocity separately from annual mass balance and state why one does not imply the other. Analyze the stratigraphic order of till, outwash and younger river incision; mark dating ties that are not supported by evidence.

Protocol and evidence allocation

Write a project proposal that names the study question, a defined sampling region, hypotheses H1 and H2 and three observations that could discriminate them. Build an input table with at least twelve synthetic measurements; record a unit, identifier, uncertainty class and data source for every row. Design one transect, one temporal repeat survey and one test using a different kind of evidence. State how the field team would avoid confirmation bias: preregister predicted outcomes, assign a second mapper to identify landforms independently and blind the initial data processing to the favored model. Any unmeasured quantity must be marked as an assumption. Base the arithmetic on the numerical cases in the unit, but do not present the invented data as actual measurements.

In the analysis, start with a mass or elevation balance rather than a purely visual explanation. If the balance does not close, label the residual and list processes that may lie outside the boundary, including sediment stored in a channel, transport at an unmonitored exit and error in estimated density or surface area. Separate interpretation of rate from reconstruction of age and chronology. An apparently consistent average rate can conceal long inactive periods punctuated by large storms, landslides or channel switching. Explore a parameter range wide enough to expose this sensitivity. Explain what observation would tell you whether the residual is an actual physical loss or a measurement artifact.

Reporting template and peer review

The final report should contain: a title that describes an inquiry rather than a conclusion; data source and acquisition notes; a map or section with scale; a table of raw observations; a short derivation with units; alternative interpretations; uncertainty and detection limits; and an honest conclusion identifying what the exercise cannot show. A peer reviewer should trace one plotted value back to an original input without assistance. Report uncertainty in both physical data and causal attribution. Describe how sampling locations could bias the result, and identify one independent validation datum that was not used when choosing the model. Treat a good fit as a starting point for further testing, not proof of a unique historical trajectory.

Assessment prompts and rubric

Interpretation. Provide two competing process histories consistent with the invented evidence, predict one differentiating field measurement, and identify which uncertainties could reverse the preferred interpretation. Quantitative reasoning. Recompute the selected unit case in SI units, vary two inputs independently, and interpret signs, magnitudes and nonlinearities. Evidence ethics. Identify all fabricated datasets and explain why they must never be described as real hazard, glacial or coastal monitoring records. Review standard. Full credit requires correct units, reproducible methods, a mass or geometry balance, explicit chronology limits and an alternative interpretation; numerical correctness alone is insufficient.

§4.10 Unit synthesis

Return to the guiding question: Which landforms and sediment records diagnose cold-region processes? Make a process diagram contrasting an observable pattern with a process that could produce it. Now add a second process capable of leaving a similar pattern. Underline measurements that would distinguish the two. Ask whether the lesson's calculations predict the type of change measured by those instruments or only provide a scale for discussing it. The emphasis throughout geomorphology is not on naming spectacular landforms but on reconstructing defensible histories, with uncertainties, from repeatable measurements and physical consistency.
