§7.1 Transfer matrices and channel interaction

Lesson 7.1 — Transfer matrices and channel interaction

Unit: Multivariable systems, coupling and decoupling. Prerequisites: Book 11 feedback and state-space fundamentals; preceding lessons in this unit. Learning objectives: explain the model, reproduce the worked case, identify an important failure condition, and independently solve the problems.

Concept and mechanism

A multivariable plant has several inputs and outputs. Its transfer matrix G(s) describes how each input affects each output; off-diagonal terms represent coupling. Independent single-loop tuning may work when those terms are weak at relevant frequencies, but steady-state decoupling alone may fail near dynamic resonance. Signal scaling must be fixed before comparing numerical gains.

Governing relation: y(s)=G(s)u(s), G=[g11 g12;g21 g22]

To interpret this expression, mark each symbol as a state, command, observation, uncertainty, noise statistic or design weight. Determine whether the result is continuous-time, sampled-time, a static mapping, or a bound. The statement may require nonsingular matrices, bounded variation, a linear model or known timing. List the specific hypotheses before drawing an engineering conclusion.

Worked example

Take static G=[[2,1],[0.5,1.5]]. Applying u=[1,0] produces y=[2,0.5], so the first actuator moves both measured outputs. Applying u=[0,1] produces y=[1,1.5]. For target y=[1,1], solve det(G)=3−0.5=2.5, yielding inverse (1/2.5)[[1.5,−1],[−0.5,2]], and u=[0.2,0.6].

Independent check: Write the same example as an equation whose two sides must agree after substitution. Check the sign and limiting cases; then change only one number and predict whether the outcome increases, decreases, or requires a full recalculation. Do not mistake numerical agreement under synthetic data for physical validation. A result describing a covariance is conditional on the noise model; one describing stability is conditional on the model and its defined perturbations; one describing an optimum is conditional on both the objective and the feasible set.

Interpretation and decision

The inverse makes sense only for a nonsingular static gain matrix and actuator commands within limits. If one output represents a temperature and another a voltage, the numerical matrix depends on the measurement units.

The design choice should identify the goal being improved—tracking, robustness, estimation, control effort or timing—and a quantity that may become worse. Distinguish a sufficient theorem condition from a necessary condition. For optimization explicitly report actuator limits; for estimation report which measurements support the inference. A calculated number is useful only when its assumptions and units are available to the reader.

When the argument fails

A dynamic G(s) can have phase lag and nonminimum-phase zeros that a static inverse ignores. Attempting to cancel those dynamics can produce unstable or noncausal control laws.

Indicate which measured variable or computed check would reveal the discrepancy. Repeating the original calculation at the same nominal point cannot test a modeling assumption that never appeared in the equation.

§7.2 Singular values and poor conditioning

Lesson 7.2 — Singular values and poor conditioning

Unit: Multivariable systems, coupling and decoupling. Prerequisites: Book 11 feedback and state-space fundamentals; preceding lessons in this unit. Learning objectives: explain the model, reproduce the worked case, identify an important failure condition, and independently solve the problems.

Concept and mechanism

Singular values describe how strongly a matrix maps input directions into output directions after scaling. A small minimum singular value means some desired output direction requires large input, making control sensitive to noise and mismatch. Condition number is the ratio of maximum to minimum singular value, provided the minimum is nonzero. A full-rank matrix can still be practically difficult to invert.

Governing relation: kappa_2(G)=sigma_max(G)/sigma_min(G)

To interpret this expression, mark each symbol as a state, command, observation, uncertainty, noise statistic or design weight. Determine whether the result is continuous-time, sampled-time, a static mapping, or a bound. The statement may require nonsingular matrices, bounded variation, a linear model or known timing. List the specific hypotheses before drawing an engineering conclusion.

Worked example

For diagonal G=diag(10,0.1), singular values are 10 and 0.1, so condition number is 100. To obtain y=[1,1] requires u=[0.1,10], with a huge second input relative to the first. Rescaling the second output units can change the raw condition number, so an engineering interpretation must begin with normalized variables.

Interpretation and decision

A low singular value need not imply instability; it implies low authority in an input-output direction. Controller optimization can penalize that costly direction instead of demanding exact inversion.

Measuring frequency-dependent singular values can reveal interaction peaks that are invisible in a DC matrix. A matrix well-conditioned at zero frequency may be poorly conditioned near a fast actuator resonance.

§7.3 Relative gain array and input-output pairing

Lesson 7.3 — Relative gain array and input-output pairing

Unit: Multivariable systems, coupling and decoupling. Prerequisites: Book 11 feedback and state-space fundamentals; preceding lessons in this unit. Learning objectives: explain the model, reproduce the worked case, identify an important failure condition, and independently solve the problems.

Concept and mechanism

The relative gain array compares the open-loop effect of an actuator with the effect remaining when other channels are controlled. For a nonsingular steady-state gain matrix G, RGA=G elementwise-multiplied by (G^{-1})^T. Values close to one on proposed pairings can support decentralized pairing under stated assumptions; negative or very large elements warn of interactions. RGA is not a complete dynamic stability analysis.

Governing relation: Lambda=G .* (G^(-1))^T, elementwise product

To interpret this expression, mark each symbol as a state, command, observation, uncertainty, noise statistic or design weight. Determine whether the result is continuous-time, sampled-time, a static mapping, or a bound. The statement may require nonsingular matrices, bounded variation, a linear model or known timing. List the specific hypotheses before drawing an engineering conclusion.

Worked example

For G=[[2,1],[0.5,1.5]], the inverse is [[0.6,−0.4],[−0.2,0.8]]. RGA=[[2×0.6,1×(−0.2)],[0.5×(−0.4),1.5×0.8]]=[[1.2,−0.2],[−0.2,1.2]]. Row and column sums each equal one. Diagonal pairings appear preferable to off-diagonal ones for this static model.

Interpretation and decision

This analysis assumes the matrix represents the relevant process gain near the operating point. Transport delay, changing process direction and actuator saturation can reverse an apparently satisfactory pairing decision.

§7.4 State feedback and decentralized versus centralized design

Lesson 7.4 — State feedback and decentralized versus centralized design

Unit: Multivariable systems, coupling and decoupling. Prerequisites: Book 11 feedback and state-space fundamentals; preceding lessons in this unit. Learning objectives: explain the model, reproduce the worked case, identify an important failure condition, and independently solve the problems.

Concept and mechanism

In state-space form, multiple inputs can collectively control modes that a single actuator cannot influence. The controllability matrix tests mathematical reachability, while centralized feedback can coordinate channels to avoid conflicting commands. Decentralized control is simpler to deploy but may ignore strong cross-coupling. Physical authority, communication and available measurement determine whether a centralized policy is practical.

Governing relation: x_dot=A x+B u; controllability matrix C=[B AB ... A^(n-1)B]

To interpret this expression, mark each symbol as a state, command, observation, uncertainty, noise statistic or design weight. Determine whether the result is continuous-time, sampled-time, a static mapping, or a bound. The statement may require nonsingular matrices, bounded variation, a linear model or known timing. List the specific hypotheses before drawing an engineering conclusion.

Worked example

Let A=diag(−1,−2), B=I2. Both states can be driven independently and the pair is controllable because B itself has rank two. If instead B=[[1],[0]] with only one scalar input, the second state is uncontrollable; it remains stable in this example, but its trajectory cannot be assigned arbitrarily. Controllability alone does not say whether the commanded controls are within rated voltage or force.

Independent check: Write the same example as an equation whose two sides must agree after substitution. Check the sign and limiting cases; then change only one number and predict whether the outcome increases, decreases, or requires a full recalculation. Do not mistake numerical agreement under synthetic data for physical validation. A result describing a covariance is conditional on the noise model; one describing stability is conditional on the model and its defined perturbations; one describing an optimum is conditional on both the objective and the feasible set.

Interpretation and decision

A centralized state-feedback law can take advantage of cross-sensor information, but it may depend on data that arrive late. A decentralized law may keep essential local loops operating when a network fails, at the cost of weaker coordination.

The design choice should identify the goal being improved—tracking, robustness, estimation, control effort or timing—and a quantity that may become worse. §7.5 Engineering Design Project: Discrete-Time State-Space Systems, Z-Transform State Equations, and Deadbeat Control

Unit 7 applied project — Two-input two-output process pairing case

Engineering question

Use steady gain matrix G=[[2,1],[0.5,1.5]] and desired outputs [1,1]. Compute inverse input, RGA and condition number. Perturb g12 by +20% and recalculate required inputs. Introduce a ±0.5 actuator ceiling and explain the resulting compromise when exact outputs cannot be achieved. Compare diagonal pairing with an attempted cross-pairing.

This is an offline design and evidence exercise. Its output is a reproducible mathematical study and a bounded conclusion, not permission to commission a physical controller. Write the study question in a sentence that could be falsified by a measured trajectory or a computed counterexample. Explain why the chosen input variation contains more information than an arbitrary random curve.

Setup, model and data protocol

Specify each variable, unit and initial state. The baseline model must be documented independently of the controller. List any linearization point, noise covariance, uncertainty interval, constraints or bounded disturbance. If the project contains a numerical experiment, provide either a short script or a CSV data table, and log the equation and numerical integration interval that produced it. Avoid a test in which the same output is used both to choose every model parameter and to certify accuracy. If this project cannot be evaluated without a missing sensor or unsupported assumption, make the gap an explicit result.

Required analytical work

  1. Derive the governing state equation, transfer equation, cost, covariance update or stability inequality appropriate to this unit. Where possible, independently derive a special case without a library solver.
  2. Calculate a baseline numerical value and check it using a distinct method: direct substitution, a matrix identity, a simulated recurrence or an analytically known limit. Include the expected dimensions.
  3. Declare a testable range for one key parameter. Sample several values and describe what is and is not proven by that sample. For a mathematical worst-case guarantee, state why the chosen analytic bound applies to the full nominated uncertainty set.
  4. Check performance and feasibility separately. A low cost does not imply constraints are met; a stable trajectory does not prove that an actuator command is allowed. Where the optimizer or model predicts an impossible demand, report infeasibility plainly.
  5. Record at least one deliberately adverse scenario: sensor bias, delayed packet, perturbation outside the fit range, insufficient excitation, a saturated actuator, or a change in the model’s region of validity.

Measurement and critical interpretation

Actuator-direction audit: Examine the input direction that produces the smallest measured change. Ask whether that direction is affordable in actuator effort. Report how a small gain perturbation changes an inverse-based command and whether the proposed pairing remains sensible.

A technically useful report separates mathematical truths under explicit assumptions, simulations of those assumptions, and empirical claims that require physical evidence. Explain which additional variable would most improve confidence. A compelling graph should show labeled axes and a model-versus-reference comparison, not just a beautiful output trace. If a proposed controller improves response time but worsens effort, the tradeoff belongs in the conclusion. Do not discard a failed scenario merely because it disrupts the nominal result.

Required report package

  • Model card: signals and dimensions, operating range, nominal and uncertain parameters, initial state and constraints.
  • One derivation checked by a second independent calculation, with all rounding disclosed.
  • A baseline numeric table and an adverse-case numeric table, each with reproducible values.
  • At least two plots with captions explaining what the curves demonstrate and what they do not.
  • A decision paragraph about the next cheapest test that could reject an important assumption.
  • A separate limitations note identifying implementation, sensor, timing, actuator and safety evidence not supplied by this exercise.

Assessment rubric

25% analytical correctness means the mathematical object and its conditions are identified precisely. 20% numerical traceability means the results can be recomputed from the stated data. 20% comparison requires a meaningful alternative, sensitivity or failure scenario. 20% engineering interpretation requires an explicit distinction between a passed model test and an unverified deployment assumption. 15% communication covers units, graph labels, repeatability and clear identification of the limits of conclusions. Strong work may conclude that a proposed design is not justified by the evidence; that is not a failure of the assignment.
