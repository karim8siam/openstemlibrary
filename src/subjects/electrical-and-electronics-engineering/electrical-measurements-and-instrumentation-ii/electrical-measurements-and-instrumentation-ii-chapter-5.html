§5.1 Foundational Principles & Curriculum Scope: Digital Storage Oscilloscopes (DSO): Sampling Modes, Bandwidth, and Triggering

Unit 5 — Precision low-level and selective measurements

Conceptual thread: Resolve weak signals amid noise and drift. Application scenario: A low-amplitude sensor signal recovered through modeled synchronous detection.

Why this topic matters

Averaging is powerful only when the signal and noise satisfy the assumptions. Drift, flicker noise, offsets, mains interference and unwanted modulation invalidate simplistic square-root-of-time predictions. The instrument must be matched to the signal spectrum and the engineering question. The engineering goal is a measurement whose physical interpretation can survive an independent check. The activity is intentionally model-based: any numbers in assignments, datasets or simulator plots are invented for educational purposes. In actual equipment, frequency-dependent calibration, laboratory safety and environmental ratings would require external evidence.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, you should be able to construct the principal measurement relation and explain its limits; reproduce two independent numerical results; identify at least one coherent alternative explanation for an apparent trend; select the data and metadata needed for a reproducible report; and design a measurement assurance check that does not assume the result it intends to confirm. The last outcome is central: a collection of accurate-looking digital values is not itself a valid measurement.

Suggested sequence

Read the first lesson as a model-building exercise, the second as an instrument or method comparison, the third as an error-analysis problem, and the fourth as a verification and decision exercise. Keep an equation notebook with a units check, an uncertainty table and a record of any assumptions you changed. Then solve the [unit assessment](../assessments/unit-05.html), complete the [integrated project](../projects/unit-05.html) and explore the [interactive model](../simulations/model-05.html).

Model interpretation

The simulator for this unit is Lock-in detector I/Q components. Ideal coherent detector after perfect low pass; no physical noise source or filter transient included. It varies only the mathematical quantities visible in its controls; a plotted result does not constitute an equipment qualification or measured sample. For a meaningful parameter study, save the default values, change one input at a time, reproduce a point independently and then look for a counterexample outside the ideal model. The derivative of the curve, not merely its appearance, is often the best clue about sensitivity and where resolution becomes inadequate.

§5.2 Johnson noise, bandwidth and impedance

5.1 — Johnson noise, bandwidth and impedance

Unit: Precision low-level and selective measurements Prerequisites: introductory electronics, calculus, signals and probability. Type of evidence: mathematical and synthetic; no physical hardware was tested. Estimated study time: 75–110 minutes including the calculation and verification challenge.

Engineering question and underlying phenomenon

Thermal noise emerges from a resistor at finite temperature without an external driving signal. In the classical low-frequency approximation, mean-square noise voltage is proportional to resistance, temperature and equivalent noise bandwidth. Noise density and integrated RMS noise must be distinguished. Amplifier input current noise can make increasing source resistance especially costly.

Within this unit, the example application is a low-amplitude sensor signal recovered through modeled synchronous detection. A useful outcome is not merely a number: the reader must be able to decide what electrical or physical quantity is inferred, how the inference depends on a reference, and which alternative mechanisms might reproduce the observation. A calibrated indication is still conditional on the quantity being measured, reference range, environmental state and acquisition protocol. Consider whether the experiment will distinguish the input phenomenon from the measurement chain itself. If not, a second observable or controlled intervention is required.

Governing model

Central relation: Vn_rms=sqrt(4 kB T R B), with kB=1.380649×10^-23 J/K.

Interpret every mathematical symbol before substitution. Express frequencies in hertz, durations in seconds, temperatures in the convention required by the equation, and angles in degrees or radians as specified. When an inverse operation is needed, verify that it is unique over the operational range. For ratio measurements, verify that the denominator cannot approach zero; for logarithmic levels, identify the reference quantity, detection bandwidth and impedance convention. More reported digits cannot compensate for an undefined physical variable.

Model development

Classify the relation as a direct physical law, an approximation derived under controlled assumptions, or an empirical calibration curve. Write the independent and dependent variables and check the direction of the physical mechanism. In particular, look for a relevant limiting case: no input, very large impedance, long observation, or an ideal reference.

Specific context: To compare noise calculations, convert every spectral density to consistent amplitude or power units and state whether it is one-sided or two-sided. Noise suppression does not justify hiding signal bandwidth or group delay. Even when the equation is correct, it is valid only under its stated physical model. A good laboratory report differentiates assumptions adopted to make a model solvable, experimentally checked operating limits and parameters that remain completely unknown. The first two can be defended; the third must be stated as an unresolved source of uncertainty rather than assigned an attractive but unjustified small value.

Detailed worked calculation

For T=300 K, R=10 kΩ and B=1 kHz, Vn≈sqrt(4×1.380649e−23×300×10,000×1,000)=0.407 µV RMS. Doubling bandwidth raises the RMS by sqrt(2), not two.

Interpretation. This calculation is a model prediction under the stated conditions, not a newly observed experimental result. To audit it, write the substituted numbers next to their units, calculate without premature rounding, and estimate the order of magnitude before accepting the final digits. Distinguish a predicted change caused by a controlled parameter from an apparent change caused by sensor gain, instrument timing, filtering or normalization. If the expected sign of a correction is reversed, review the signal flow and physical reference before changing the equation to match the desired answer.

Checks that should accompany the numerical result

First, change one input while keeping the remaining assumptions fixed and confirm the output moves in the physically expected direction. Second, calculate the result through a different arrangement of the same governing law or a limiting-case approximation. Third, state the span over which the model has been calibrated or independently verified. Fourth, document whether the result is meant to represent an instantaneous value, an RMS estimate, an average over a defined interval or a statistical bound. These distinctions affect what can legitimately be compared with a manufacturer's specification.

Important departures from the ideal model

An actual amplifier adds voltage and current noise, 1/f noise and finite bandwidth-shape effects. The nominal −3 dB cutoff is not always equal to noise-equivalent bandwidth.

It is useful to classify the departures by where they enter. Errors before conversion can distort the physical input; errors in amplification or digitization change the measured indication; timebase and software errors corrupt event ordering; calibration errors convert an otherwise faithful electrical reading to an incorrect physical quantity. The appropriate check differs across these cases. Repeated acquisition on the same unmodified chain can measure repeatability but cannot expose every persistent bias. In practice one should prioritize a small number of physically plausible error sources over a long, unweighted list of hypothetical effects.

Practical verification strategy

Create a noise budget by referring every contributor to the same circuit input and stating bandwidth and spectral density conventions. Use a second method or reference only when its systematic errors are sufficiently independent. Record conditions that make the check reproducible: model revision, input data, ranges, reference conditions, clock and sample rate, software version, arithmetic precision and the date of the hypothetical experiment. If equipment were involved, the methods would also need actual safety ratings and personnel authorization. The offline simulations in this book test formulas, not equipment compliance.

Show the dimensional conversion, leading intermediate values and units. Explain the scope of the result in one sentence, and identify one missing uncertainty term. For full credit, the interpretation must not confuse uncertainty with an operating tolerance or a variation of the physical input.

Exercise B — discriminate between explanations (8 marks). Compute the resistor noise at 100 kHz and explain whether a 100-fold bandwidth increase permits a 100-fold signal resolution improvement. Prepare a two-column table comparing the ideal physical prediction with a competing measurement-chain explanation. Name the variable you would change, the response you would record and the expected observation that distinguishes the hypotheses. A plan that changes two variables at once without controlling confounders does not earn full marks.

Exercise C — construct a defensible measurement plan (10 marks). Design a software-only analysis or a supervised low-energy verification compatible with this topic. Identify one reference quantity, two controlled operating points and the raw evidence required for another person to reproduce the result. State at least two ways your inference might fail. Specify how you would report an inconclusive result instead of force-fitting a conformance label.

§5.3 Lock-in detection and reference coherence

5.2 — Lock-in detection and reference coherence

Unit: Precision low-level and selective measurements Prerequisites: introductory electronics, calculus, signals and probability. Type of evidence: mathematical and synthetic; no physical hardware was tested. Estimated study time: 75–110 minutes including the calculation and verification challenge.

Engineering question and underlying phenomenon

A lock-in detector multiplies an input by a reference and low-pass filters the product. The desired narrowband component becomes approximately constant when signal and reference are coherent; off-frequency interference is moved to higher beat frequencies and often suppressed. The filter time constant controls a tradeoff between noise rejection and response speed. Phase errors redistribute signal between in-phase and quadrature channels.

Within this unit, the example application is a low-amplitude sensor signal recovered through modeled synchronous detection. A useful outcome is not merely a number: the reader must be able to decide what electrical or physical quantity is inferred, how the inference depends on a reference, and which alternative mechanisms might reproduce the observation. §5.4 Chopping, autozero and offset drift

5.3 — Chopping, autozero and offset drift

Unit: Precision low-level and selective measurements Prerequisites: introductory electronics, calculus, signals and probability. Type of evidence: mathematical and synthetic; no physical hardware was tested. Estimated study time: 75–110 minutes including the calculation and verification challenge.

Engineering question and underlying phenomenon

A weak DC signal cannot be resolved reliably when amplifier offset and low-frequency drift are of comparable magnitude. Chopper stabilization modulates the signal to a higher frequency where flicker noise is smaller, then demodulates it. Autozero techniques sample an offset estimate and subtract it. Both methods create practical artifacts, including ripple, switching charge injection, aliasing or noise folding.

Within this unit, the example application is a low-amplitude sensor signal recovered through modeled synchronous detection. A useful outcome is not merely a number: the reader must be able to decide what electrical or physical quantity is inferred, how the inference depends on a reference, and which alternative mechanisms might reproduce the observation. Correlated calibration terms must not be treated as independent contributions simply because they appear in different datasheets.

§5.5 Averaging, autocorrelation and detection limits

5.4 — Averaging, autocorrelation and detection limits

Unit: Precision low-level and selective measurements Prerequisites: introductory electronics, calculus, signals and probability. Type of evidence: mathematical and synthetic; no physical hardware was tested. Estimated study time: 75–110 minutes including the calculation and verification challenge.

Engineering question and underlying phenomenon

For independent identically distributed noise, averaging N samples reduces standard error as 1/sqrt(N). Correlated low-frequency fluctuations reduce the effective number of independent observations. A long smooth trace can therefore create false confidence if its sample-to-sample correlation is high. Detection limits must also consider false positives, multiple testing and uncertainty in background level.

Within this unit, the example application is a low-amplitude sensor signal recovered through modeled synchronous detection. A useful outcome is not merely a number: the reader must be able to decide what electrical or physical quantity is inferred, how the inference depends on a reference, and which alternative mechanisms might reproduce the observation. Central relation: For an approximate AR(1) process with lag correlation ρ, Neff≈N(1−ρ)/(1+ρ), away from finite-sample limits.

A protocol that can only reproduce the expected result has insufficient diagnostic power.
