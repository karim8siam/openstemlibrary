§6.1 Foundational Principles & Curriculum Scope: Spectrum Analyzers, FFT Signal Analyzers, and Harmonic Distortion Measurement

Unit 6 — Spectral, harmonic and RF measurement fundamentals

Conceptual thread: Read spectra without confusing instruments with signals. Application scenario: Synthetic spectrum measurements from a low-energy signal generator model.

Why this topic matters

Sampling, record length, detector bandwidth, windows and calibration all change what a plotted trace means. A narrow spectral spike can be a real tone, leakage, an alias or an overload product. This unit focuses on correctly interpreting data rather than building or transmitting RF hardware. The engineering goal is a measurement whose physical interpretation can survive an independent check. The activity is intentionally model-based: any numbers in assignments, datasets or simulator plots are invented for educational purposes. In actual equipment, frequency-dependent calibration, laboratory safety and environmental ratings would require external evidence.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, you should be able to construct the principal measurement relation and explain its limits; reproduce two independent numerical results; identify at least one coherent alternative explanation for an apparent trend; select the data and metadata needed for a reproducible report; and design a measurement assurance check that does not assume the result it intends to confirm. The last outcome is central: a collection of accurate-looking digital values is not itself a valid measurement.

Suggested sequence

Read the first lesson as a model-building exercise, the second as an instrument or method comparison, the third as an error-analysis problem, and the fourth as a verification and decision exercise. Keep an equation notebook with a units check, an uncertainty table and a record of any assumptions you changed. Then solve the [unit assessment](../assessments/unit-06.html), complete the [integrated project](../projects/unit-06.html) and explore the [interactive model](../simulations/model-06.html).

Model interpretation

The simulator for this unit is DFT spectral leakage and window effects. Calculated discrete Fourier magnitude for an ideal sampled cosine; Hann-window scaling corrected for coherent gain. It varies only the mathematical quantities visible in its controls; a plotted result does not constitute an equipment qualification or measured sample. For a meaningful parameter study, save the default values, change one input at a time, reproduce a point independently and then look for a counterexample outside the ideal model. The derivative of the curve, not merely its appearance, is often the best clue about sensitivity and where resolution becomes inadequate.

§6.2 FFT bins, coherent sampling and leakage

6.1 — FFT bins, coherent sampling and leakage

Unit: Spectral, harmonic and RF measurement fundamentals Prerequisites: introductory electronics, calculus, signals and probability. Type of evidence: mathematical and synthetic; no physical hardware was tested. Estimated study time: 75–110 minutes including the calculation and verification challenge.

Engineering question and underlying phenomenon

A discrete Fourier transform analyzes a finite observation multiplied by a time window. When a tone does not complete an integer number of cycles, its energy spreads across multiple spectral bins, even if the source is spectrally pure. Coherent sampling reduces leakage for a stable single tone, while window functions trade narrow main lobes for reduced sidelobes. The displayed peak bin is not automatically the true tone amplitude.

Within this unit, the example application is synthetic spectrum measurements from a low-energy signal generator model. A useful outcome is not merely a number: the reader must be able to decide what electrical or physical quantity is inferred, how the inference depends on a reference, and which alternative mechanisms might reproduce the observation. A calibrated indication is still conditional on the quantity being measured, reference range, environmental state and acquisition protocol. Consider whether the experiment will distinguish the input phenomenon from the measurement chain itself. If not, a second observable or controlled intervention is required.

Governing model

Central relation: FFT bin spacing Δf=fs/N and observation duration T=N/fs.

Interpret every mathematical symbol before substitution. Express frequencies in hertz, durations in seconds, temperatures in the convention required by the equation, and angles in degrees or radians as specified. When an inverse operation is needed, verify that it is unique over the operational range. For ratio measurements, verify that the denominator cannot approach zero; for logarithmic levels, identify the reference quantity, detection bandwidth and impedance convention. More reported digits cannot compensate for an undefined physical variable.

Model development

Classify the relation as a direct physical law, an approximation derived under controlled assumptions, or an empirical calibration curve. Write the independent and dependent variables and check the direction of the physical mechanism. In particular, look for a relevant limiting case: no input, very large impedance, long observation, or an ideal reference.

Specific context: Frequency plots require a detector definition, sampling and window conventions, and the gain of the complete signal path. Peak heights cannot be compared between unlike windows without appropriate scaling. Even when the equation is correct, it is valid only under its stated physical model. A good laboratory report differentiates assumptions adopted to make a model solvable, experimentally checked operating limits and parameters that remain completely unknown. The first two can be defended; the third must be stated as an unresolved source of uncertainty rather than assigned an attractive but unjustified small value.

Detailed worked calculation

At fs=20 kS/s with N=2,000 points, the frequency-bin spacing is 10 Hz and record duration is 0.1 s. A 1,005 Hz tone lies between nominal 1,000 and 1,010 Hz bins and can leak into many bins with a rectangular window.

Interpretation. This calculation is a model prediction under the stated conditions, not a newly observed experimental result. To audit it, write the substituted numbers next to their units, calculate without premature rounding, and estimate the order of magnitude before accepting the final digits. Distinguish a predicted change caused by a controlled parameter from an apparent change caused by sensor gain, instrument timing, filtering or normalization. If the expected sign of a correction is reversed, review the signal flow and physical reference before changing the equation to match the desired answer.

Checks that should accompany the numerical result

First, change one input while keeping the remaining assumptions fixed and confirm the output moves in the physically expected direction. Second, calculate the result through a different arrangement of the same governing law or a limiting-case approximation. Third, state the span over which the model has been calibrated or independently verified. Fourth, document whether the result is meant to represent an instantaneous value, an RMS estimate, an average over a defined interval or a statistical bound. These distinctions affect what can legitimately be compared with a manufacturer's specification.

Important departures from the ideal model

The FFT assumes a stationary finite record; transients and multiple interfering tones complicate interpretation. Window coherent gain and equivalent noise bandwidth must be used when reporting tone amplitude or noise density.

It is useful to classify the departures by where they enter. Errors before conversion can distort the physical input; errors in amplification or digitization change the measured indication; timebase and software errors corrupt event ordering; calibration errors convert an otherwise faithful electrical reading to an incorrect physical quantity. The appropriate check differs across these cases. Repeated acquisition on the same unmodified chain can measure repeatability but cannot expose every persistent bias. In practice one should prioritize a small number of physically plausible error sources over a long, unweighted list of hypothetical effects.

Practical verification strategy

Compare spectra under rectangular and Hann windows with equal raw samples, and report correction factors before comparing displayed peaks. Use a second method or reference only when its systematic errors are sufficiently independent. Record conditions that make the check reproducible: model revision, input data, ranges, reference conditions, clock and sample rate, software version, arithmetic precision and the date of the hypothetical experiment. If equipment were involved, the methods would also need actual safety ratings and personnel authorization. The offline simulations in this book test formulas, not equipment compliance.

For full credit, the interpretation must not confuse uncertainty with an operating tolerance or a variation of the physical input.

Exercise B — discriminate between explanations (8 marks). Double N at the same fs and predict new bin spacing and observation duration. Does this necessarily increase physical sensor bandwidth? Prepare a two-column table comparing the ideal physical prediction with a competing measurement-chain explanation. Name the variable you would change, the response you would record and the expected observation that distinguishes the hypotheses. A plan that changes two variables at once without controlling confounders does not earn full marks.

§6.3 THD and harmonic power interpretation

6.2 — THD and harmonic power interpretation

Unit: Spectral, harmonic and RF measurement fundamentals Prerequisites: introductory electronics, calculus, signals and probability. Type of evidence: mathematical and synthetic; no physical hardware was tested. Estimated study time: 75–110 minutes including the calculation and verification challenge.

Engineering question and underlying phenomenon

Total harmonic distortion describes harmonic RMS content relative to the fundamental for an agreed harmonic set. It does not include all nonharmonic noise or interharmonics unless a broader metric is defined. A low THD number can coexist with a large DC offset, poor transient behavior or interference outside the analyzed band. Instrument front-end linearity can itself create harmonics that appear to belong to the device under test.

Within this unit, the example application is synthetic spectrum measurements from a low-energy signal generator model. §6.4 Spectrum analyzers, resolution bandwidth and noise floor

6.3 — Spectrum analyzers, resolution bandwidth and noise floor

Unit: Spectral, harmonic and RF measurement fundamentals Prerequisites: introductory electronics, calculus, signals and probability. Type of evidence: mathematical and synthetic; no physical hardware was tested. Estimated study time: 75–110 minutes including the calculation and verification challenge.

Engineering question and underlying phenomenon

An analyzer uses filtering and detection to estimate power within finite resolution bandwidth. Narrowing RBW can lower the displayed noise power per bin, while also increasing sweep time or changing transient capture. Video bandwidth and detector mode further modify presentation. A display level is meaningful only when input attenuation, reference level, impedance and bandwidth conventions are known.

Within this unit, the example application is synthetic spectrum measurements from a low-energy signal generator model. §6.5 Transmission-line return loss and reflection

6.4 — Transmission-line return loss and reflection

Unit: Spectral, harmonic and RF measurement fundamentals Prerequisites: introductory electronics, calculus, signals and probability. Type of evidence: mathematical and synthetic; no physical hardware was tested. Estimated study time: 75–110 minutes including the calculation and verification challenge.

Engineering question and underlying phenomenon

When physical length is significant relative to wavelength, the voltage-current relationship depends on distributed propagation rather than a lumped resistor. Reflection coefficient quantifies mismatch against a declared reference impedance. Return loss is a logarithmic magnitude expression, not direct proof of cable fault location or absolute insertion loss. Network analyzers require a calibration or reference-plane correction appropriate to the fixture.
