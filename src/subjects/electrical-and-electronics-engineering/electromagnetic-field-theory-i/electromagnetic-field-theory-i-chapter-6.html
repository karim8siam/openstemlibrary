Learning outcomes

Unit investigation

Compute the field at a set of points near two parallel opposite currents. Use Biot–Savart superposition in the infinite-wire approximation to draw B vectors, but mark the assumptions that the wires are straight, very long and sufficiently separated from terminations. Use Ampère’s integral law along a loop enclosing only one conductor and then a loop enclosing both. The second has zero net enclosed current, yet local B on its perimeter is not generally zero. This is an accessible example of the danger in interpreting an integral law as a pointwise zero-field prediction.

Turn the analysis into an EMC routing choice. A paired current and close return typically gives a smaller loop area than two widely separated conductors; compare field magnitude at specified observation points. §6.2 Magnetic flux density and the Biot–Savart law

Unit 6, lesson 1 — Magnetic flux density and the Biot–Savart law

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

A stationary electric charge produces an electrostatic field, while a steady current produces a magnetostatic field. Biot–Savart calculates B by adding contributions from current elements, with orientation determined by a cross product. The result is not generally radial: around a long straight wire, B wraps circumferentially. Where a conductor has finite thickness, the singular filament formula is not valid inside the metal; use the appropriate current distribution.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

For a current element I dl, dB=μ0/(4π) I (dl×Rhat)/R². For an infinitely long thin straight wire, B_phi=μ0 I/(2πrho). For a circular loop of radius a, B_z(0)=μ0 I/(2a).

Worked numerical illustration

A long straight wire carrying 5 A produces B≈(4π×10^-7 ×5)/(2π×0.02)=50 μT at 2 cm. The right-hand rule determines phi direction; reversing current reverses B.

Independent numerical variant

A long 8 A wire at 4 cm generates B=40 μT in vacuum, assuming the observation lies outside its thin cross section.

Engineering context: Wire-return geometry

Plot the B field around one long straight current and then introduce a parallel return conductor carrying equal opposite current. Far away, the return path cancels much of the pair’s magnetic field; near one wire, the local field remains significant. This illustrates why a measured magnetic field cannot be predicted from a supply wire alone. In compact electronics routing, loop area rather than wire current alone often controls magnetic coupling and emission.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

Doubling straight-wire current doubles B; doubling observation radius halves B outside a thin long wire.

Why the model can fail

A finite conductor needs an end-angle correction, and neighboring return conductors can cancel or enhance the field. Avoid comparing line-current near-field calculations directly with occupational exposure limits without correct averaging and geometrical context.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. What change of B occurs when current direction reverses in a thin wire?

Reasoned answer. The circumferential B direction reverses while magnitude stays unchanged for the same

Question B. Why can a distant paired return conductor decrease magnetic field?

Reasoned answer. Its opposite current produces an opposing far-field contribution; full vector superposition accounts for cancellation.

§6.3 Ampère’s law and symmetry

Unit 6, lesson 2 — Ampère’s law and symmetry

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

Ampère’s circuital law is to steady magnetic current what Gauss’s law is to charge: an exact relationship whose convenience depends on symmetry. For a long uniform-current cylinder, a circular Amperian loop gives B proportional to radius inside and inversely proportional outside. For a long solenoid, an axial field approximation is strongest well away from its ends. For a finite transformer core or near a coil terminal, simple one-dimensional magnetic circuits omit leakage and fringing.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

Magnetostatic integral law: ∮C H·dl = Ifree,enclosed. In free space B=μ0H. Inside a cylinder radius a with uniformly distributed I, Hphi=I rho/(2πa²) for rho

Worked numerical illustration

A solid conductor radius 4 mm carries 10 A uniformly. At rho=2 mm, Hphi=10×0.002/[2π(0.004)²]≈199 A/m; at rho=8 mm Hphi=199 A/m again. Equal numerical values here occur at two special radii, not at every radius.

Independent numerical variant

Inside a uniform solid wire radius 3 mm carrying 6 A, at rho=1.5 mm B≈0.2 mT; at rho=6 mm B also ≈0.2 mT.

Engineering context: Inside versus outside a solid conductor

The B field of a solid uniformly conducting cylinder increases linearly with radius inside and falls as 1/rho outside. Build one continuous piecewise graph and verify that the two branches meet at the conductor surface. The derivative changes there because the source-current distribution changes. If current distribution becomes skin-effect dominated at high frequency, the uniform-current assumption is invalid and the plotted inside branch must be replaced.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

Inside a uniformly conducting solid wire, B grows linearly with radial position; outside it decreases inversely with radius.

Why the model can fail

The surface used to count enclosed current must agree with contour orientation. If material magnetization matters, using B=μ0H everywhere is invalid: account for B=μ0(H+M).

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. Where is B maximum for a uniformly current-filled long cylindrical wire?

Reasoned answer. At the surface radius a, where the increasing inside branch meets the decreasing outside branch.

Question B. Can Ampère’s law around a contour enclosing zero net current imply B=0 at every point?

Reasoned answer. No. Only the signed circulation integral is constrained; local B may be substantial.

§6.4 Magnetic materials and field boundaries

Unit 6, lesson 3 — Magnetic materials and field boundaries

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

Magnetic permeability is a constitutive property that relates B to H under specified conditions. Real ferromagnetic cores exhibit nonlinearity, hysteresis and saturation, so a constant relative permeability applies only within a limited range. At an interface, normal B is continuous because magnetic monopoles are absent; tangential H jumps by any free surface current. These relations matter at air gaps, magnetic shields, sensor cores and transformer laminations.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

∇·B=0; n·(B2-B1)=0. The boundary relation n×(H2-H1)=Kfree (depending on the normal orientation convention). For an ideal linear isotropic material B=μH; magnetic susceptibility gives M=χm H in the linear regime.

Worked numerical illustration

If H=400 A/m and a hypothetical linear core has μr=500, B=μ0 μr H≈0.251 T. This arithmetic cannot predict whether the actual core saturates; the manufacturer’s B–H curve is needed.

Independent numerical variant

For μr=100 and H=1000 A/m, the linear estimate gives B=0.126 T. A nonlinear B–H curve may shift this value substantially.

Engineering context: Air-gap uncertainty

Compute a nominal air-gap B using a constant-μ magnetic circuit. Compare it with a sensitivity range where μr varies by a factor of ten and where the effective gap changes due to mechanical tolerance. If the air gap dominates reluctance, changes in core μ may matter less than gap length. The exercise illustrates why “magnetic material with μr=1000” is not a complete component specification.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

At fixed H, B scales with an assumed linear μ; saturation prevents this extrapolation indefinitely in ferromagnets.

Why the model can fail

Treat hysteresis loops as path-dependent: B at a given H can differ after opposite excitation histories. A high μr number without a frequency and bias specification is inadequate for precision design.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. Which magnetic component cannot jump across an ordinary interface?

Reasoned answer. Normal B remains continuous because div B=0. Tangential H may jump due to surface free current.

Question B. Is a single μr enough to specify hysteresis and saturation?

Reasoned answer. No. Ferromagnetic response depends on bias, frequency, path history and the B–H relation.

§6.5 Vector potential and magnetic flux conservation

Unit 6, lesson 4 — Vector potential and magnetic flux conservation

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

Because div B=0, it is convenient to introduce a magnetic vector potential A such that B=curl A. The vector potential is not unique: a gradient can be added without changing B. This gauge freedom is useful for computational field solvers, but it means A itself should not be interpreted as a directly measurable unique field. In simple two-dimensional magnetostatics, a single out-of-plane component of A can describe in-plane flux contours.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

B=∇×A and A_new=A+grad chi produce identical B. In Coulomb gauge div A=0, ∇²A=-μJ for constant μ and suitable steady conditions. Magnetic flux through S is ΦB=∫S B·dA=∮boundary A·dl.

Worked numerical illustration

For a uniform B=0.05 T normal to a 0.01 m² planar loop, Φ=5e-4 Wb. If the loop is rotated so its normal makes 60° to B, the flux becomes 2.5e-4 Wb, highlighting the signed dot product.

Independent numerical variant

A uniform 0.1 T field threading a planar loop of area 20 cm² carries flux .0002 Wb; reversing the normal flips its signed flux.

Engineering context: Vector potential gauge test

Choose a simple A field that produces a uniform magnetic flux density and add the gradient of a smooth scalar χ. Numerically differentiate both A expressions and verify that curl A is unchanged within discretization precision. Then compare their pointwise A values; the difference can be large. This prevents assigning independent physical meaning to an arbitrary vector potential contour while preserving its computational utility for field-solvers.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

Changing A by a pure gradient leaves B unchanged, no matter the scale of that added gradient.

Why the model can fail

Flux through an open surface can be nonzero; flux through a closed surface is always zero in the classical Maxwell theory. A magnetic vector-potential plot is gauge-dependent unless the gauge is specified.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. Does A+grad χ change measurable B?

Reasoned answer. No, because curl of a gradient is zero. This is gauge freedom, provided required regularity holds.

Question B. What must be true of magnetic flux through a closed surface?

Reasoned answer. It is zero in classical electromagnetism: field lines do not begin or end at isolated magnetic charges.
