§7.2 Self-inductance and flux linkage

Unit 7, lesson 1 — Self-inductance and flux linkage

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

An inductor stores energy in its magnetic field. Flux linkage λ counts magnetic flux threaded by turns, and inductance connects λ to current under linear conditions. In a long ideal solenoid, increasing turns substantially increases inductance because current both creates the field and the resulting field links the turns. Real coils have winding resistance, parasitic capacitance, leakage, fringing and frequency-dependent cores; a single L is a low-frequency approximation, not an all-frequency device model.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

For a linear inductor λ=LI and v=dλ/dt=L di/dt if L is constant. Ideal long solenoid L≈μ N² A/ell. Magnetic energy Wm=0.5 L I² in the linear regime.

Worked numerical illustration

For an air-core solenoid with N=200, A=1 cm² and ell=0.10 m, L≈μ0×200²×1e-4/0.10≈50.3 μH. At 1 A the stored energy is about 25.1 μJ.

Independent numerical variant

An ideal solenoid N=100, A=2 cm², length .2 m, air core has L≈12.6 μH and W≈6.28 μJ at 1 A.

Engineering context: Coil dimension sensitivity

For the ideal long air-core solenoid L scales with turns squared, cross-sectional area and inverse length. Vary turns by ±10% and observe that inductance changes approximately by +21% or -19%, not ±10%. This sensitivity can dominate geometry tolerances in a prototype. At high frequency, measure winding capacitance and resistance separately; relying on the ideal inductance relation beyond its range creates false resonance and loss predictions.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

For an ideal long solenoid at fixed geometry, doubling N quadruples L and quadruples energy at fixed I.

Why the model can fail

The inductance formula neglects end fringing, and using a constant high μ for a saturating core can overstate stored energy. A coil with an air gap may store much of its energy in the gap, even though the iron carries the flux.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. If turns N double at fixed ideal geometry, by what factor does L change?

Reasoned answer. Inductance scales as N², so it becomes four times larger in the ideal linear solenoid model.

Question B. Why can a core with an air gap store most energy in its gap?

Reasoned answer. Gap H can be large for a given B because μ is small; field energy density B²/(2μ) is correspondingly high.

§7.3 Mutual inductance and coupled circuits

Unit 7, lesson 2 — Mutual inductance and coupled circuits

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

Two coils can share magnetic flux so that changing current in one produces voltage in the other. Mutual inductance describes this linkage and reciprocity makes M12=M21 under ordinary linear passive conditions. The sign of induced voltages depends on dot convention and winding orientation, not simply whether a displayed current is positive. Coupling coefficient k expresses the fraction of perfect coupling and has magnitude at most one for passive linear two-coil models.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

λ1=L1 i1+M i2 and λ2=M i1+L2 i2 under aligned dot references; M=k sqrt(L1 L2), 0≤|k|≤1. Induced terms v1=L1 di1/dt+M di2/dt and v2=M di1/dt+L2 di2/dt are signed by chosen terminal conventions.

Worked numerical illustration

With L1=4 mH, L2=9 mH and k=0.5, M=0.5 sqrt(36) mH=3 mH. A primary current slope of +200 A/s creates magnitude 0.6 V in the secondary, with polarity decided by dot convention.

Independent numerical variant

For L1=1 mH, L2=16 mH and k=.25, M=1 mH. A current ramp of 1000 A/s induces 1 V magnitude in the second winding.

Engineering context: Polarity verification

Mark the terminals of two coupled coils using a dot convention. Increase current entering the dotted primary terminal and determine which secondary terminal becomes positive under the chosen voltage reference. Check M ≤ sqrt(L1 L2) to protect the energy model from an unphysical coupling claim. In a real instrument, phase delay and winding capacitance can complicate measurements; the textbook ideal is intentionally low-frequency.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

Doubling coupling k doubles M when L1 and L2 are fixed; physical |k| cannot exceed one in the passive model.

Why the model can fail

A coupling coefficient from one operating point may vary with mechanical position or saturating material. Do not ignore core loss and leakage when interpreting an actual transformer at high frequency.

§7.4 Magnetic energy and electromechanical force

Unit 7, lesson 3 — Magnetic energy and electromechanical force

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

In magnetic actuators the field energy depends on current and mechanical geometry. The force that moves an armature can be obtained from energy or co-energy derivatives, but the choice of held-fixed electrical variables matters. For a linear coil with position-dependent inductance L(x), magnetic co-energy at constant current is 0.5L(x)I²; the force along x is its derivative. This provides a bridge between field calculations and practical relays, speakers and solenoids.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

For an ideal linear actuator, F_x|I = 0.5 I² dL/dx. Magnetic field energy density is u_m=0.5 B·H for a linear nondispersive material. Across a simple air gap the field pressure scale is p≈B²/(2μ0).

Worked numerical illustration

An actuator has dL/dx=0.8 mH/m and constant current I=2 A. The ideal force is 0.5×4×0.0008=0.0016 N along increasing inductance. This small magnitude is a reminder that geometry sensitivity matters as much as current.

Independent numerical variant

For a linear actuator dL/dx=.002 H/m and I=3 A, the ideal constant-current force is .009 N in the increasing-L direction.

Engineering context: Force derivative model

For a movable coil with L(x) estimated at three equally spaced positions, approximate dL/dx using a central difference. Insert current into F=0.5 I² dL/dx to estimate force direction. Repeat with current doubled and confirm a factor-of-four scaling in the linear model. If a measured force departs from that prediction, inspect saturation, mechanical friction, end effects and current-source constraints before blaming the numerical derivative.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

Doubling I at fixed geometry multiplies magnetic force by four if the linear co-energy model remains valid.

Why the model can fail

The energy method gives net generalized force, not necessarily local stress concentration. Friction, springs, saturation and eddy currents can dominate in real devices. Always state what “held constant” means.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. If I doubles at fixed L(x), how does ideal magnetic force change?

Reasoned answer. Force at constant current is proportional to I², so it becomes four times as large.

Question B. What must be held fixed in the co-energy force derivative?

Reasoned answer. Current, rather than flux linkage, for F=(∂Wco/∂x)

§7.5 Reluctance circuits and design limits

Unit 7, lesson 4 — Reluctance circuits and design limits

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

A magnetic circuit approximates flux as current through a reluctance network. Magnetomotive force NI drives flux; reluctance grows with length and shrinks with permeability and cross-sectional area. The analogy to resistive networks is useful but imperfect: flux leakage, fringing and saturation make permeability nonconstant and routes nonunique. Air gaps often dominate total reluctance, allowing intentionally controlled inductance but also introducing strong local fields and mechanical force.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

Reluctance Rm=ell/(μ A), magnetic flux Φ≈NI/Rm and B≈Φ/A. For series sections with a common flux, reluctances add. In an air-gap-dominated core of gap g and cross section A, Rgap≈g/(μ0 A) ignoring fringe.

Worked numerical illustration

Take a gap 1 mm long, area 4 cm², N=500 turns and I=0.2 A. Approximate Bgap≈μ0 NI/g=0.126 T when iron reluctance is negligible. Estimated gap pressure is B²/(2μ0)≈6.3 kPa.

Independent numerical variant

A 0.5 mm air gap, area 2 cm², N=200, I=.1 A gives ideal B≈50.3 mT neglecting iron reluctance and gap fringing.

Engineering context: Reluctance and fringing

Model a core gap as g/(μ0A) and then compare two approximations for the effective gap area: geometric A and a slightly enlarged effective area accounting for fringing. The second gives lower reluctance and somewhat larger flux. No fixed fringing correction is universal; geometry sets the correction. A useful student report shows both cases and identifies which physical data would be needed to justify either approximation.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

Doubling air-gap length doubles dominant reluctance and roughly halves flux at fixed NI.

Why the model can fail

A magnetic network is not a substitute for a field solver when air gaps are wide or dimensions change sharply. A nominally one-dimensional gap includes fringing, and gap B at saturation can differ drastically.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. If an ideal dominant gap doubles, what happens to its reluctance?

Reasoned answer. Reluctance doubles at fixed effective area. The predicted flux halves at fixed NI if the gap truly dominates.

Question B. Why is a large-fringing gap hard to model with one area?

Reasoned answer. The flux spreads in space, making effective area and local B nonuniform. A field solver or measured correction is needed.
