Unit 4: Dielectrics, capacitance and stored energy

Four lessons: Polarization and electric displacement, Dielectric interfaces and field refraction, Parallel-plate, coaxial and spherical capacitance, Electrostatic energy, force and breakdown limits.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, explain the physical field variables and their SI units; solve a useful analytical reference geometry; reproduce a field-level numerical calculation; identify boundary assumptions; and explain an engineering decision that would be unjustified by the ideal model alone.

Unit investigation

A fixed charge and a fixed voltage are different experiments. For a parallel-plate ideal capacitor, analyze what happens to E, Q, C and stored field energy when a dielectric slab is inserted completely while a battery remains attached. Then repeat after charging and disconnecting the battery. At fixed V, C increases and Q must enter through the circuit; at fixed Q, the voltage falls. The change in field energy cannot by itself describe mechanical work until the source’s energy exchange is considered. Explain why both models can predict dielectric attraction without a contradiction.

For a coaxial capacitor calculate C per unit length from a voltage integral of E(r). Record surface field at the inner conductor and stored energy for the same geometry. Compare increasing εr to decreasing inner radius, using one electrical constraint at a time. An engineer designing for maximum charge storage, low voltage stress and low cable mass faces competing objectives. The student report should be a Pareto comparison, not an unsupported winner based on capacitance alone.

§4.2 Polarization and electric displacement

Unit 4, lesson 1 — Polarization and electric displacement

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

A dielectric does not permit freely mobile charges to neutralize an imposed static field like a metal. Instead it responds through microscopic polarization: tiny dipoles align or are induced, producing bound charge. The displacement field D is defined to separate free and bound charge bookkeeping. In a linear isotropic medium D=epsilon E; that relation is a constitutive assumption, not a universal field law. Anisotropic crystals require a tensor epsilon, and strong fields may invalidate linear response.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

D=epsilon0 E+P and div D=rho_free. For a linear isotropic nondispersive model, P=epsilon0(εr-1)E and epsilon=epsilon0 εr. Bound charge density is rho_bound=-div P and surface bound charge sigma_bound=P·n.

Worked numerical illustration

For εr=4 and E=1000 V/m, D=4epsilon0·1000=3.542e-8 C/m² and P=3epsilon0·1000=2.656e-8 C/m². This is a macroscopic average, not the polarization of a specific molecule.

Independent numerical variant

For εr=5 at E=500 V/m, D=2.214e-8 C/m² and P=(5-1)epsilon0×500≈1.771e-8 C/m².

Engineering context: Dielectric response ledger

Measure capacitance with the same electrode geometry filled first with air and then with an insulating sample. In an ideal uniform-field situation their ratio estimates relative permittivity. However the measured ratio also includes fringe fields and parasitic leads. Repeat at several frequencies and temperatures; the apparent εr may change because polarization mechanisms are dispersive. Avoid inserting one tabulated permittivity into both a static high-voltage model and a high-frequency RF model without checking its validity range.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

At fixed E, D scales linearly with εr under the linear model; at fixed D, E varies inversely with εr.

Why the model can fail

Some texts call D “electric flux density”; it does not mean electric field lines are material wires. Frequency-dependent complex permittivity in a lossy dielectric must be handled separately from static εr.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. What is D in a linear dielectric of εr=3 at fixed E compared with vacuum?

Reasoned answer. It is three times the vacuum displacement value. That statement presumes the same E and a linear isotropic model.

Question B. Is all polarization charge free charge?

Reasoned answer. No. Bound charge arises from divergence of P and appears in microscopic polarization; Gauss’s law for D uses free charge.

§4.3 Dielectric interfaces and field refraction

Unit 4, lesson 2 — Dielectric interfaces and field refraction

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

Electrostatic boundary conditions are as important as bulk equations. The tangential component of E remains continuous at a static interface, while the normal component of D changes by any free surface charge. At an interface without free surface charge, D normal is continuous, so E normal generally differs on the two sides when the permittivities differ. The resulting direction change is sometimes called field refraction; it follows conservation and constitutive relations rather than optical Snell’s law.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

For interface normal n pointing from medium 1 to 2, n·(D2-D1)=sigma_free and n×(E2-E1)=0 in electrostatics. When sigma_free=0, epsilon1 E1n=epsilon2 E2n. Et is the same on both sides.

Worked numerical illustration

Two planar dielectrics with εr1=2 and εr2=5 carry Dn=20 nC/m² with no free interfacial charge. E1n=20e-9/(2epsilon0)=1129 V/m while E2n=452 V/m. Assigning the same normal E to both would create a fictitious interface charge.

Independent numerical variant

For normal D=12 nC/m², εr=3 yields E≈451.8 V/m; εr=6 yields E≈225.9 V/m. Neither result requires a free interface sheet charge.

Engineering context: Two-layer insulation

Connect two dielectric sheets in series across a common electrode area. With no free interfacial charge, the normal displacement is the same, while electric-field magnitudes differ inversely with permittivity. The voltage fraction carried by a layer is its thickness times its field, not just its thickness fraction. A thinner lower-permittivity layer can experience the larger field. Record the electric stress in each layer before deciding which layer limits withstand capability.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

At zero free interface charge, doubling ε2 halves E2n for the same Dn, while Et remains equal.

Why the model can fail

At a real rough interface, local normals vary and ideal planar results approximate only the locally smooth region. Interfaces can carry trapped charge, making the zero-free-charge simplification invalid.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. At a charge-free interface, which normal quantity stays continuous?

Reasoned answer. Normal D stays continuous; normal E changes inversely with epsilon. Tangential E stays continuous in electrostatics.

Question B. Why does the lower-ε layer often experience higher normal electric stress?

Reasoned answer. At fixed normal D, E=D/epsilon, so smaller epsilon produces larger E even while the normal displacement matches.

§4.4 Parallel-plate, coaxial and spherical capacitance

Unit 4, lesson 3 — Parallel-plate, coaxial and spherical capacitance

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

Capacitance measures charge per potential difference for a specified geometry and dielectric configuration. Increasing electrode overlap or permittivity increases C, while increasing plate separation generally decreases it. Geometry matters: the field of a coaxial cable varies as 1/rho; treating it as a constant parallel-plate field gives a systematically wrong voltage. In a two-conductor static problem, the capacitance is positive and depends on the full conductor arrangement, not just the material label.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

C=Q/ΔV. Ideal plates: C≈epsilon A/d when fringing is negligible. Coaxial line length L, inner radius a, outer b: C=2πepsilon L/ln(b/a). Concentric spheres radii a

Worked numerical illustration

For 1 m of coax with εr=2.25, a=1 mm and b=4 mm, C=2π(2.25epsilon0)/ln 4≈90.3 pF. Doubling length doubles C; doubling both radii leaves the ratio b/a unchanged and does not change capacitance per unit length in this ideal model.

Independent numerical variant

For 1 m coax with a=1 mm, b=5 mm, εr=2, C≈69.1 pF/m. Increasing b to 10 mm reduces C to about 48.3 pF/m.

Engineering context: Cable geometry experiment

Hold a coaxial cable’s outer radius fixed and vary its inner radius. Capacitance per metre increases as ln(b/a) decreases, but the maximum E at the inner conductor may also increase for certain constrained voltage designs. Choose what is held constant—V, Q or field limit—before comparing candidates. A trustworthy design table contains geometry, εr, C per metre, peak surface E and stored energy. No one scalar metric establishes a universally superior geometry.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

At fixed b, increasing inner radius a decreases ln(b/a), increasing capacitance per length while changing peak field.

Why the model can fail

For a cable with several dielectric layers, an effective permittivity cannot generally be obtained by arithmetic averaging. Integrate each radial electric-field segment and add the resulting voltage drops at fixed charge.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. For ideal coax, does doubling both radii change C per metre?

Reasoned answer. No. ln(b/a) stays fixed, and per-length C for a homogeneous medium is unchanged. However operating electric field at fixed voltage changes.

Question B. Why is C=εA/d unsafe for a coaxial cable?

Reasoned answer. Its radial field is nonuniform and cylindrical geometry requires integrating voltage from a to b; the logarithm is essential.

§4.5 Electrostatic energy, force and breakdown limits

Unit 4, lesson 4 — Electrostatic energy, force and breakdown limits

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

Charging a capacitor transfers energy from the source into its field. For linear nondispersive materials, electrostatic energy density is one half of E·D. An attractive force between biased electrodes can be obtained by an appropriate energy or co-energy derivative, but the electrical constraint must be explicit: fixed voltage and fixed charge lead to different mathematical derivatives. The ideal field model cannot determine safe working voltage without material breakdown data, defects, temperature and field concentration.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

W_e = (1/2)CV²=Q²/(2C) for a linear capacitor; u_e=(1/2)E·D. For an ideal parallel plate at fixed V, magnitude of attractive force is approximately epsilon A V²/(2d²) if fringing is negligible.

Worked numerical illustration

An ideal C=100 pF capacitor charged to 500 V stores 0.5×100e-12×500²=12.5 μJ. Increasing voltage by a factor of two quadruples stored energy, an important result for design of test circuits and discharge protections.

Independent numerical variant

A 50 pF ideal capacitor at 200 V stores 1.0 μJ. At 400 V it stores 4.0 μJ, which verifies the quadratic voltage dependence.

Engineering context: Stored charge and safe discharge

An energy calculation is a starting point for analyzing a capacitor, not a complete safety assessment. Even modest average energy can accompany dangerous high voltages or rapid currents. Model a resistive discharge with V(t)=V0 exp[-t/(RC)] and identify the period during which a measurement instrument should not be connected. Real capacitors can have dielectric absorption and residual voltage. Student simulations should use synthetic signals and should never advise building a live high-voltage test.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

At fixed C, doubling voltage multiplies energy by four; at fixed voltage, doubling C doubles energy.

Why the model can fail

Do not use vacuum electrostatic stress blindly inside nonlinear ferroelectrics. Breakdown field depends on time scale and geometry; a simulation reporting voltage alone is not an insulation qualification.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. If capacitor voltage triples at fixed C, what happens to stored energy?

Reasoned answer. It increases by a factor of nine because W=0.5CV², but only while C stays effectively constant.

Question B. Can an energy calculation alone specify dielectric safety?

Reasoned answer. No. Peak field, defects, duration, geometry, leakage and material dielectric strength all matter.
