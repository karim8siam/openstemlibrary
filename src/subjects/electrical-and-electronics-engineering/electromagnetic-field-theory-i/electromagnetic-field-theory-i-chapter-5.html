§5.1 Theoretical Framework and Overview: Conductors, Dielectrics, Boundary Conditions, and Capacitance

Unit 5: Current flow and conduction fields

Four lessons: Conduction current density and Ohm’s law, Charge continuity and relaxation, Resistance fields and the conduction–electrostatic analogy, Field-based thermal loss and measurement.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, explain the physical field variables and their SI units; solve a useful analytical reference geometry; reproduce a field-level numerical calculation; identify boundary assumptions; and explain an engineering decision that would be unjustified by the ideal model alone.

Unit investigation

Treat a uniform conductive bar as a resistor at first. Calculate R, I, J, E and dissipated power using terminal V and known σ. Verify the local power integral matches VI to rounding precision. Now divide the same bar into two serial segments of equal length but different cross-sectional areas. Because steady current must be equal in both, the narrower segment has greater J and E. Integrate voltage drop along the length, rather than attempting to assign one uniform gradient to the full device. This introduces field-based resistance as a physical consequence of boundary and conservation laws.

Extend the discussion to a rounded constriction in a two-dimensional conductive sheet. There is no simple exact one-dimensional current-density profile near the taper; show the limitations of a series-slab estimate. Explain why contacts contribute spreading resistance and how a four-terminal measurement can isolate the sensing region. The project is complete only when it states the ambient temperature used to select σ and identifies whether electrode contact resistance is included.

§5.2 Conduction current density and Ohm’s law

Unit 5, lesson 1 — Conduction current density and Ohm’s law

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

In a conductor a macroscopic current is the flow of charge across a surface per unit time. The vector density J describes flow per unit area; total current is its surface integral. The pointwise Ohm law J=σE assumes a local linear isotropic material, sufficiently slow variations and a known temperature. In a resistive rod the same total current flows through each cross section at steady state, but J can be very nonuniform around bends, constrictions or heterogeneous conductors.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

I=∫S J·dA; in homogeneous isotropic material J=σE and rho_resistivity=1/σ. For a uniform bar of length L, area A, R=L/(σA). Joule power per volume p=J·E=σ|E|².

Worked numerical illustration

A 2 m copper-like conductor with σ=5.8e7 S/m and cross-sectional area 1 mm² has R=2/(5.8e7×1e-6)=0.0345 Ω. At 2 A its ideal steady loss is I²R≈0.138 W, excluding contacts and temperature rise.

Independent numerical variant

A 1 m copper-like rod with σ=5.8e7 S/m and A=2 mm² has R≈8.62 mΩ; at 3 A its ideal loss is about 0.0776 W.

Engineering context: Current density mapping

Imagine two copper traces carrying the same total current but having different widths. A uniform cross-section estimate predicts larger average J in the narrower trace. Now add a sharp inside corner: current lines crowd toward the corner, so local J can exceed the average. To assess heating, compute an integral of J·E over the conductor rather than using peak J alone. Device-level measurements require calibrated current and temperature observations to compare with the model.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

Doubling L doubles resistance; doubling A halves resistance, for constant σ and negligible contacts.

Why the model can fail

Electrical conductivity changes with temperature and microstructure. A simulation using constant σ must not claim to predict thermal runaway; current crowding can produce local hot spots even when average J appears acceptable.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. If a conductor length doubles but area and conductivity stay fixed, how does R change?

Reasoned answer. It doubles under uniform-bar approximation, and at fixed current the Joule loss doubles. At fixed voltage the loss falls by half.

Question B. Why do sharp trace corners threaten a uniform-J assumption?

Reasoned answer. Current trajectories crowd near some edges, raising local J and J·E above average values.

§5.3 Charge continuity and relaxation

Unit 5, lesson 2 — Charge continuity and relaxation

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

Charge cannot vanish when a wire branches. The continuity equation states that net outward conduction-current flux equals the negative rate of decrease of charge inside a region. At steady state, div J=0 even though E and J may be nonzero. The equation also reveals why conduction and capacitance phenomena are linked: during transients, conduction current may be balanced by changing electric displacement, and regions can temporarily accumulate charge.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

Local continuity: ∂rho_free/∂t+div J=0. Integral form: dQfree/dt=-∮S J·dA. With J=σE, div E=rho/epsilon and homogeneous constant σ, charge density decays as rho(t)=rho(0)e^(-t/τ), τ=epsilon/σ.

Worked numerical illustration

For εr=3 and σ=1e-9 S/m, τ=3epsilon0/1e-9≈0.0266 s. After three relaxation times the idealized excess bulk charge has dropped to e^-3≈5.0% of its initial amount.

Independent numerical variant

If ε=4epsilon0 and σ=2e-9 S/m, τ≈17.7 ms, and after 2τ only e^-2≈13.5% of initial homogeneous bulk excess charge remains.

Engineering context: Transient charge decay

Take an initially charged bulk dielectric with known ε and small finite σ. Under a simplistic homogeneous relaxation model its charge density falls exponentially. Plot rho(t)/rho(0) over five relaxation times and verify 1/e at t=τ. Then discuss the missing mechanisms: surface conduction, traps, injection, humidity, electrode polarization. These omissions explain why a real dielectric can display much slower tails than an ideal single-exponential model.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

Doubling σ halves the homogeneous relaxation time ε/σ, when ε is held fixed.

Why the model can fail

Relaxation time depends strongly on humidity and impurity. A material called an insulator may still have measurable leakage, especially on contaminated surfaces. Do not equate div J=0 to zero current.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. After one relaxation time, what fraction of excess bulk charge remains in the homogeneous model?

Reasoned answer. The exponential factor is e^-1≈0.368, not zero. This is a model prediction for a specified ε/σ.

Question B. How can steady current exist with div J=0?

Reasoned answer. The divergence measures local net source or sink, not the current magnitude; equal inflow and outflow are allowed.

§5.4 Resistance fields and the conduction–electrostatic analogy

Unit 5, lesson 3 — Resistance fields and the conduction–electrostatic analogy

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

Many steady conductive structures can be analyzed with the same mathematics as electrostatic potential problems. If σ is uniform and there are no internal sources, V satisfies Laplace’s equation because J=-σgrad V and div J=0. Current flow lines are parallel to E in an isotropic medium and orthogonal to equipotentials. The analogy is powerful for field plotting, but boundary conditions distinguish conducting electrodes, insulated surfaces and material interfaces.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

div(σgrad V)=0 in steady source-free conduction. For constant σ this becomes ∇²V=0. Across an interface, potential is continuous under ideal contact and the normal component of J is continuous if no charge accumulates. An insulated wall satisfies J·n=0.

Worked numerical illustration

A uniform slab of thickness 2 mm and electrode area 10 cm² has σ=1e-3 S/m. Its resistance is d/(σA)=0.002/(1e-3×0.001)=2000 Ω. With 10 V the current is 5 mA and the uniform current density is 5 A/m².

Independent numerical variant

A uniform 1 mm thick slab with area 20 cm² and σ=1e-4 S/m has R=5 kΩ. At 10 V it carries 2 mA.

Engineering context: Distributed current solver

A resistor slab clamped between large planar electrodes can be solved analytically, while tiny surface contacts create spreading-current patterns. Treat the planar case as the solver benchmark and then replace one electrode with a small circular pad. Check that integrated current across every cut plane is the same at steady state. Document how the mesh resolves electrode edges; otherwise computed peak current density may be dominated by a geometric singularity.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

Doubling conductivity at the same fixed field doubles J and local loss σE²; at fixed terminal current it reduces loss instead.

Why the model can fail

Potential-continuity at a contact is not guaranteed if appreciable contact resistance exists. A finite-element conductivity model should include electrode spreading resistance when electrode area is much smaller than the sample cross section.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. When does the conduction potential obey Laplace’s equation?

Reasoned answer. For steady, source-free current in a region of constant isotropic conductivity. Variable conductivity requires div(σgrad V)=0.

Question B. At a dielectric-insulated wall in a steady conduction solver, what condition applies?

Reasoned answer. The normal conduction current J·n is zero. Prescribing arbitrary fixed voltage there may create an unintended current sink.

§5.5 Field-based thermal loss and measurement

Unit 5, lesson 4 — Field-based thermal loss and measurement

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

Power dissipation is a spatial integral, not merely a circuit-level I²R value. A field solver can predict where heat generation is concentrated using p=J·E; coupled thermal simulation is needed to translate those losses into temperatures. Conductivity measurements at low voltages should verify contact condition and instrument loading. Four-terminal sensing separates voltage leads from current-carrying contact drops when high precision is needed.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

P=∫V J·E dV. For a uniform resistive bar, this reduces to VI=I²R=V²/R. The integral is nonnegative for passive ohmic materials σ>0. The appropriate convection and conduction heat paths determine temperature rise.

Worked numerical illustration

For a 10 Ω resistive film at 12 V, P=14.4 W. If that heat is deposited uniformly in volume 2 cm³, the average source density is 7.2e6 W/m³. The result gives a thermal source, not a finished junction-temperature estimate.

Independent numerical variant

A 25 Ω film powered from 10 V dissipates 4 W. If its volume is 1 cm³, average p is 4e6 W/m³, with no temperature predicted.

Engineering context: Heat-source consistency

Compare three independent routes to resistive power in a uniform conductor: VI, I²R and ∫σE² dV. Agreement indicates consistency of fields, boundary voltages and cross-sectional geometry. If one route differs, first check unit conversion, contact resistance or whether heat is generated outside the modeled volume. In a real heating study, couple field-derived volumetric power to thermal conduction, convection and radiation equations before predicting steady temperature.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

At fixed resistance, doubling terminal voltage multiplies Joule power by four.

Why the model can fail

A thermal camera measures surface radiance and requires emissivity interpretation. Resistive loss plots are often most sensitive to assumed conductivity; always report assumed temperature and resistance values with model results.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. Which is the correct local heat source: J·E or

Reasoned answer. J

Question B. Would a 14.4 W heating value determine the film temperature?

Reasoned answer. Not without geometry, thermal conductivities, ambient temperature and boundary heat-transfer mechanisms.
