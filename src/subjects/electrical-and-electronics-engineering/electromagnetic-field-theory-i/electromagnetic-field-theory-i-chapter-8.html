§8.1 Theoretical Framework and Overview: Magnetostatics: Biot-Savart Law, Ampère Law, and Vector Potential

Unit 8: Time-varying fields and the wave bridge

Four lessons: Faraday induction and motional emf, Displacement current and charge conservation, Maxwell equations and material relations, Plane waves, impedance and energy transport.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, explain the physical field variables and their SI units; solve a useful analytical reference geometry; reproduce a field-level numerical calculation; identify boundary assumptions; and explain an engineering decision that would be unjustified by the ideal model alone.

Unit investigation

Make three links between circuit quantities and fields. First, charge an ideal parallel-plate capacitor with a constant terminal current. At fixed C, V(t) rises linearly, and the displacement flux derivative matches the wire conduction current for a spanning surface through its gap. Second, consider an isolated conducting loop whose magnetic flux varies linearly; calculate signed induced emf from the declared loop normal and contour orientation. Third, construct a uniform plane wave with E along x, H along y and propagation along +z. From the vacuum intrinsic impedance, compute H for a chosen E0 and then evaluate average Poynting power density.

The final synthesis should state what is, and is not, shared between the three systems. A capacitor near field can be approximately quasi-static; a wave is a distributed propagating solution. The induction loop can transfer energy between mechanical, magnetic and electrical systems, while the wave transports power through space. Maxwell equations unify the description but do not make these three geometries identical.

§8.2 Faraday induction and motional emf

Unit 8, lesson 1 — Faraday induction and motional emf

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

An electric field can circulate even without an electrostatic charge source when magnetic flux changes in time. Faraday’s law states that the signed induced emf around a closed stationary contour is the negative time derivative of magnetic flux through it. In moving conductors, the Lorentz force on charge carriers adds a motional contribution. These mechanisms must not be indiscriminately combined as if one were always the other; use the circuit geometry and moving-path formulation.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

For a stationary loop, ∮C E·dl = -dΦB/dt. For an N-turn ideal coil e=-N dΦ/dt. A straight conductor of length ell moving at speed v perpendicular to a uniform B has motional emf magnitude Bell v.

Worked numerical illustration

A 100-turn coil experiences magnetic flux per turn changing from 1 mWb to 3 mWb in 0.02 s. Its average emf magnitude is 100×0.002/0.02=10 V. Lenz’s law fixes polarity so the induced current would oppose the imposed flux change.

Independent numerical variant

If N=50 and flux changes by 0.004 Wb over .1 s, the induced average emf magnitude is 2 V, with polarity set by chosen orientation.

Engineering context: Flux change signs

Use a loop normal along +z and a magnetic flux increasing in +z. The induced circulation is negative with respect to the positive right-hand contour, opposing the increase. Reverse the loop normal and contour together; the physical current direction should remain the same despite different algebraic signs. This small orientation test prevents many silent errors in motor and generator modeling.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

Doubling the rate of change of magnetic flux doubles induced emf magnitude for fixed turns.

Why the model can fail

The sign of a reported emf requires a contour orientation and loop normal. Mechanical work, electrical loss and stored-field energy must balance; the induced emf is not a source of free energy.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. What defines the sign of Faraday emf?

Reasoned answer. A chosen contour orientation and its consistent positive surface normal; induced emf opposes signed flux change.

Question B. Can motional emf be obtained solely from a stationary-contour changing-flux formula in every geometry?

Reasoned answer. No. For moving conductive paths, the velocity-cross-B Lorentz contribution needs a suitable moving-contour formulation.

§8.3 Displacement current and charge conservation

Unit 8, lesson 2 — Displacement current and charge conservation

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

A charging capacitor creates an apparent puzzle for a simple conduction-current-only Ampère law: a surface cutting its wire carries current, while a bulging surface passing between the plates cuts no wire. Maxwell’s additional displacement-current term makes the field circulation independent of which spanning surface is chosen. The term is the time derivative of electric displacement flux, not a flow of identical mobile electrons through the vacuum gap.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

Ampère–Maxwell: ∮C H·dl = Icond,through S + d/dt ∫S D·dA. Differential form: curl H=Jfree+∂D/∂t. Taking divergence produces div Jfree+∂rho_free/∂t=0 because div(curl H)=0 and div D=rho_free.

Worked numerical illustration

For ideal plate capacitor C=100 pF with voltage ramp dV/dt=1e6 V/s, displacement current is Id=C dV/dt=100 μA. Any surrounding loop that links its feed wire must give a consistent H circulation regardless of spanning-surface choice.

Independent numerical variant

For C=200 pF and voltage slope 0.5 MV/s, displacement current magnitude is 100 μA.

Engineering context: Capacitor surface paradox

Draw one closed Ampèrian loop around a capacitor feed and two different spanning surfaces, one cutting the conducting wire and one bulging through the plate gap. The former encloses conduction current, and the latter encloses changing displacement flux. Under the ideal charging model those rates match. Record both results in amperes and plot the equality as voltage slope changes. This is a much stronger understanding check than memorizing a formula for displacement current.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

Doubling dV/dt doubles displacement current in an ideal constant-C capacitor.

Why the model can fail

At high frequencies, fringing makes uniform parallel-plate fields approximate. In a dispersive medium, constitutive D(t) can depend on earlier E history; replacing it by a constant epsilon E may be invalid.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. If dV/dt doubles for a constant ideal C, what happens to displacement current?

Reasoned answer. It doubles: Id=C dV/dt. The plate-to-plate gap carries changing displacement flux without conduction electrons crossing it.

Question B. Why is Maxwell’s correction required for two spanning surfaces of one contour?

Reasoned answer. The predicted H circulation must be surface-independent; the displacement term balances missing wire conduction current through the gap.

§8.4 Maxwell equations and material relations

Unit 8, lesson 3 — Maxwell equations and material relations

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

The four Maxwell equations impose charge conservation, magnetic flux continuity, induction and the sources of magnetic circulation. They do not predict a particular material response without constitutive laws connecting D to E, B to H and J to E. An engineering field solution combines all four equations, constitutive data, boundary conditions and excitation. A static electrostatic result is a limiting case; mixing it with a rapidly switched drive can omit important magnetic and radiative effects.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

Differential SI form: div D=rho_free; div B=0; curl E=-∂B/∂t; curl H=Jfree+∂D/∂t. For linear isotropic homogeneous media, D=epsilon E, B=μH and J=σE. These are separate assumptions.

Worked numerical illustration

In a homogeneous source-free lossless medium with constant epsilon and mu, applying curl to Faraday and substituting Ampère–Maxwell yields ∇²E=με ∂²E/∂t². The predicted propagation speed is v=1/sqrt(με).

Independent numerical variant

For lossless material with εr=4 and μr=1, propagation speed is c/2≈149.9 Mm/s; this assumes frequency-independent parameters.

Engineering context: Static and dynamic limits

Use Maxwell’s equations as a model-selection checklist. If geometry size is tiny compared with the shortest wavelength and all source changes are slow, a quasi-static approximation may suffice. If propagation delay, distributed resonance or radiation matters, a static capacitance or inductance alone cannot capture behavior. The size criterion is not an absolute law; strong resonances and high-Q structures can invalidate simplistic rules even when nominal dimensions are small.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

Doubling ε and μ simultaneously reduces ideal lossless wave speed to half, since v=1/sqrt(με).

Why the model can fail

The wave-equation derivation uses div E=0 only in the charge-free homogeneous case. The same mathematical steps cannot be carried unchanged through layered materials or time-varying constitutive coefficients.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. Can one solve material fields with Maxwell equations but no D–E relation?

Reasoned answer. Not generally. Maxwell laws constrain sources and circulation while constitutive relations provide the medium’s response.

Question B. When does the simple wave speed 1/sqrt(με) apply?

Reasoned answer. In a homogeneous, linear, isotropic, lossless medium with appropriate constant ε and μ, not in an arbitrary dispersive or layered medium.

§8.5 Plane waves, impedance and energy transport

Unit 8, lesson 4 — Plane waves, impedance and energy transport

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

A simple uniform plane wave has mutually perpendicular electric field, magnetic field and propagation direction. The ratio of E to H magnitudes is the intrinsic impedance in a lossless medium. The Poynting vector E×H describes instantaneous power flux density, and its time average is the appropriate quantity for sinusoidal power transport. Reflecting boundaries create standing-wave patterns, so a local E amplitude alone does not specify net transmitted power.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

For lossless homogeneous material v=1/sqrt(με), η=sqrt(μ/epsilon), and for a +z wave E=E0 cos(ωt-βz) ax, H=(E0/η)cos(ωt-βz) ay. Average S=E0²/(2η) az for peak amplitude E0.

Worked numerical illustration

In free space, η0≈376.7 Ω. A plane wave with peak E0=10 V/m has peak H0=0.0265 A/m and average power flux ≈10²/(2×376.7)=0.1327 W/m². The result assumes an ideal far-field plane wave, not a nearby circuit conductor.

Independent numerical variant

For a vacuum forward plane wave with peak E=20 V/m, peak H≈53.1 mA/m and average flux ≈0.531 W/m².

Engineering context: Plane wave energy consistency

For a sinusoidal lossless plane wave, E and H rise and fall in phase and their cross product points along propagation. The instantaneous flux is proportional to cos², never negative for one purely forward wave. Its time average is half its peak. If a reflected wave is added, the local E and H superpose with different relative signs and instantaneous power may reverse. The basic interactive model intentionally displays only a forward traveling wave.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

Doubling E0 doubles H0 and quadruples time-average power flux in an ideal plane wave.

Why the model can fail

“EMF” in a coil and “E-field amplitude” in a wave are different units and concepts. The book’s wave model omits attenuation, polarization rotation and boundary reflections; those belong to the advanced continuation.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. Does a lossless forward wave have negative instantaneous Poynting flux at any point?

Reasoned answer. Not for the single uniform traveling plane wave in the stated orientation: E×H∝cos² points forward.

Question B. What effect does reflection have on determining net energy flow from E alone?

Reasoned answer. Incident and reflected waves superpose; E amplitude can form standing maxima while net average transported power depends on both E and H.
