§2.1 Theoretical Framework and Overview: Electrostatic Fields, Coulomb Force, and Field Intensity

Unit 2: Electric fields and Gauss’s law

Four lessons: Coulomb’s law and electric superposition, Flux, Gaussian surfaces and symmetry, Conductors, shielding and surface charge, Continuous charge distributions and field integration.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, explain the physical field variables and their SI units; solve a useful analytical reference geometry; reproduce a field-level numerical calculation; identify boundary assumptions; and explain an engineering decision that would be unjustified by the ideal model alone.

Unit investigation

Use a nonuniform spherical charge density as a test of the difference between enclosed charge and total charge. For an ideal distribution rho_v(r)=rho0(r/R) inside radius R, integrate 4πr²rho_v(r) dr to obtain Qenclosed(r)=πrho0 r⁴/R. The radial field then scales as r² inside. Compare this result with the linear-in-r field for uniform volume charge. Outside the charged sphere both fields collapse to the same inverse-square expression when the total charges are matched. This is a more convincing use of Gauss’s law than memorizing a few geometries.

Add a conductor-shell scenario. With the field inside metal zero in electrostatic equilibrium, determine what induced surface charges are needed if the cavity contains an isolated point charge. Use conservation of charge on the shell to find charge on its outer surface when the shell began neutral. Finally discuss why an insulating shell does not support the same redistribution and why a finite opening changes perfect shielding. Keep “free charge,” “enclosed charge” and “net total charge” as separate labels throughout.

§2.2 Coulomb’s law and electric superposition

Unit 2, lesson 1 — Coulomb’s law and electric superposition

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

The electrostatic point-charge law has inverse-square distance dependence because flux spreads over spherical area. Superposition adds the vector contributions of each source; it does not sum magnitudes unless every contribution shares the same direction. For a continuous charge density, an integral replaces the discrete sum. A point charge creates a mathematical singularity at its exact location, so numerical field maps must mask a small neighborhood rather than pretend the plotted value there is finite.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

For point charges qi at ri, E(r) = (1/(4πepsilon0)) Σ qi (r-ri)/|r-ri|³. A charge qtest experiences F=qtest E. A line charge density lambda has C/m, surface density sigma has C/m², and volume density rho_v has C/m³.

Worked numerical illustration

A +2 nC charge at the origin yields E= kq/r²=179.75 V/m at r=0.10 m, radially outward. Doubling distance lowers E to one quarter; doubling source charge doubles E. This is a useful diagnostic for any particle-field calculator.

Independent numerical variant

For one +1 nC charge at 0.20 m in vacuum, |E|≈224.7 V/m. At 0.40 m its field is 56.2 V/m; the 1/r² scaling is visible.

Engineering context: Two-source cancellation

Place equal positive charges at x=±a and calculate E at the origin: their x components cancel. Then change the left source sign and interpret why the fields add instead. Trace a test-charge path along y=0.1 m and calculate the vector contribution before taking magnitude. In a visualization, never plot the singular field exactly at a charge marker. Practical electrode arrays require iterating source strengths and positions because small charge asymmetries can shift nominally zero-field points.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

Doubling point-source separation at a corresponding scaled observation position reduces field magnitude to one quarter.

Why the model can fail

Electrostatic superposition presumes linear vacuum response. Near real electrodes a prescribed distribution may move because mobile charges redistribute. Distinguish the field calculated for specified sources from a self-consistent conductor equilibrium.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. At what locations can fields from two equal positive charges cancel?

Reasoned answer. At their midpoint the vectors are equal and opposite. Elsewhere equal magnitudes alone do not ensure opposite directions.

Question B. Does doubling all charge strengths double the electrostatic field in the specified-source vacuum model?

Reasoned answer. Yes, the electric field is linear in each qi through superposition; position and geometry are held fixed.

§2.3 Flux, Gaussian surfaces and symmetry

Unit 2, lesson 2 — Flux, Gaussian surfaces and symmetry

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

Gauss’s law is always true for electric flux through a closed surface, but its use to solve for an unknown field requires symmetry. For a uniformly charged spherical shell, radial field is constant over a concentric sphere. For a very long uniform line, cylindrical side flux survives while flux through end caps vanishes by direction. For a finite line near its end, the infinite-line approximation breaks and a Gaussian cylinder will not have uniform field magnitude on its side.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

∮S D·dA = Qfree,enclosed; in vacuum D=epsilon0 E. An infinite line gives E=rho_hat lambda/(2πepsilon0 rho); a point charge gives E=r_hat Q/(4πepsilon0 r²). Inside a uniformly charged solid sphere of radius R, E(r)=Q r/(4πepsilon0 R³) for r

Worked numerical illustration

A long wire has lambda=1.0 nC/m. At rho=0.05 m, E=lambda/(2πepsilon0 rho)=359.5 V/m. On a coaxial cylinder of length 0.20 m, the flux of D equals lambda L=0.20 nC, independent of radius.

Independent numerical variant

For lambda=2 nC/m at rho=.1 m, E≈359.5 V/m. A Gaussian cylinder of length .3 m encloses 0.6 nC regardless of the chosen positive radius.

Engineering context: Symmetry audit

Start a Gaussian-flux computation by asking what rotation or translation leaves the entire source distribution unchanged. For a charged sphere, any rotation about the center leaves it unchanged; spherical magnitude can depend only on radius. For a finite rod, translation along its length is not exact symmetry. Construct a flux-balance plot for concentric surfaces and verify that enclosed free charge remains fixed. The curve E(r) may change strongly even when total flux stays constant, because area increases as r squared.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

Doubling distance from an infinite line halves E; doubling radial distance from a point charge reduces E to one quarter.

Why the model can fail

A zero enclosed net charge does not guarantee E=0 everywhere on the surface. Opposite charges may produce strong fields whose signed flux cancels. Gaussian symmetry must be argued, not guessed from the shape of a sketch.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. Can a Gaussian surface surrounding a neutral dipole have nonzero field everywhere?

Reasoned answer. Yes. The signed total D flux is zero, while local normal flux may be positive in some regions and negative in others.

Question B. Why does the long-line field scale as 1/rho rather than 1/rho squared?

Reasoned answer. For a cylinder of fixed length, the area carrying radial flux grows linearly with rho rather than quadratically as spherical area does.

§2.4 Conductors, shielding and surface charge

Unit 2, lesson 3 — Conductors, shielding and surface charge

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

In electrostatic equilibrium, the bulk electric field inside an ideal conductor is zero and its whole connected surface is an equipotential. A nonzero tangential external E would move free charge; therefore the tangential surface component vanishes. Normal displacement has a discontinuity equal to the free surface charge density. A cavity inside a conductor is shielded from static external fields if it has no internal charges and its enclosure is complete; openings and time-dependent fields demand separate analysis.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

At a conductor–vacuum boundary Dn,out = sigma_free when Ein=0. Therefore En,out = sigma_free/epsilon0. Electrostatic force density can be expressed via the Maxwell stress tensor; for an ideal planar surface the electric pressure is p = epsilon0 E²/2.

Worked numerical illustration

For sigma=1 μC/m² on a smooth ideal conducting surface, immediate exterior En=1e-6/epsilon0≈112,941 V/m. Ideal planar electric pressure is about 0.0565 Pa. These quantities are local idealizations, not direct insulation-safety limits.

Independent numerical variant

For sigma=.5 μC/m², the external vacuum En≈56.5 kV/m on an ideal conductor surface, with direction controlled by the sign of surface charge.

Engineering context: Shield aperture test

Compare an enclosed conductor with a box containing a narrow slot. Static shielding arguments apply cleanly to the complete ideal enclosure but the slot complicates boundary conditions. Draw equipotentials near the opening and discuss why the local field may be enhanced. Use a sensor of finite size to measure a region-averaged field and record distance from the slot. A practical shielding specification should mention frequency range, grounding, seam contact and field orientation instead of a single “perfect shield” claim.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

Doubling free surface charge doubles normal exterior E in the ideal vacuum conductor model.

Why the model can fail

Sharp corners concentrate field; the field of a mathematically perfectly sharp ideal conductor can diverge. A commercial shield must be evaluated for seams, mesh openings, contacts and frequency, not simply labeled a Faraday cage.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. Is the electrostatic field inside an ideal conductor allowed to persist permanently?

Reasoned answer. No. In equilibrium mobile charges redistribute until the interior E vanishes. This does not describe all high-frequency transient or imperfect-conductor cases.

Question B. How does a nonzero free surface charge appear in the boundary condition?

Reasoned answer. The normal component of D jumps by sigma_free. For an ideal conductor in vacuum, the exterior normal field is sigma_free/epsilon0.

§2.5 Continuous charge distributions and field integration

Unit 2, lesson 4 — Continuous charge distributions and field integration

Book 21 — Electromagnetic Field Theory I · Prerequisites: multivariable calculus, introductory circuits and SI units. Quality note: Original educational draft; physical equations and instructional solutions require independent review before publication.

Physical idea and meaning

Charge on rods, rings, disks and volumes can be treated as many infinitesimal sources. Symmetry often cancels perpendicular components and saves work, but the integration must retain the vector direction until cancellation is justified. On the axis of a uniformly charged ring, transverse components sum to zero while axial components add. Such results are useful checks for approximate numerical quadrature and for estimating electrode fringe fields.

Governing relationships and derivation pathway

For a ring of radius a and total charge Q, axial E_z(z)= (Q/(4πepsilon0)) z/(z²+a²)^(3/2). It vanishes at the center, grows for small positive z and eventually behaves as Q/(4πepsilon0 z²). A finite line is integrated by choosing dq=lambda dl.

Worked numerical illustration

Take Q=5 nC, a=0.10 m, z=0.10 m. E_z = k(5e-9)(0.10)/(0.02)^(3/2)≈1,589 V/m, directed along +z for positive charge. A point-charge replacement at z=a would be a poor near-field approximation.

Independent numerical variant

For Q=2 nC, a=.1 m, z=.2 m, the axial ring field is about 322 V/m from kQz/(a²+z²)^(3/2); check the units of the denominator carefully.

Engineering context: Ring model validation

Numerically approximate the ring with 12, 48 and 192 equal charge segments and compute the on-axis field. Compare to the continuous analytical expression. At z=0 the field cancels; at very large z it approaches the point-charge limit. Distinguish quadrature error from model error: adding segments cannot fix an approximation that treats a thick nonuniform torus as a perfectly thin uniform ring. Plot the relative error as a function of both segment count and observation distance.

Sensitivity and design interpretation

On a ring axis, far-field E falls as z^-2, but close to the center the axial E grows approximately proportionally to z.

Why the model can fail

Do not replace distributed charge by total charge at its center unless distance is large compared with its extent and approximation error is acceptable. Check limiting cases z→0 and z≫a before trusting any symbolic integration.

Two resolved conceptual checkpoints

Question A. What is the far-axis limit of the ring field?

Reasoned answer. For z much larger than a, E_z approaches kQ/z² because z/(z²+a²)^(3/2) approaches 1/z².

Question B. Why is the ring-axis field zero at its center even when charge is nonzero?

Reasoned answer. Each charge element has an opposing transverse contribution. The axial factor z is zero at the center.
