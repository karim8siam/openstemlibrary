§7.1 Generator terminal characteristic

7.1 Generator terminal characteristic

Physical model and mathematical relationship

A separately excited generator obeys V_t=E_g-I_a R_a minus relevant brush voltage. Increasing load may reduce voltage through copper drop and armature reaction. The core procedure is to select a reference polarity and sign convention, distinguish an ideal relation from an experimentally calibrated one, and account for quantities whose units differ. Electromagnetic quantities must be checked against the associated conservation law: electric current and flux linkage describe excitation; voltages and torques describe observable exchange; and losses reduce usable output. In rotating or time-varying equipment, steady-state expressions must not be extrapolated to startup without tracking stored energy.

Fully worked numerical example

At induced emf 250 V, armature current 30 A and resistance 0.3 ohm, predicted terminal voltage is 241 V ignoring brush drop. Write the given quantities in SI units before substitution, then compute the predicted response and its physical meaning. If an ideal result conflicts with a device rating, the correct engineering action is to question the simplifying assumptions rather than force the physical device to satisfy the equation. For a measured system, use its nameplate, empirical characteristic curves and operating limits to refine the estimate.

Failure modes, units, and boundary conditions

Field saturation and load-dependent flux shift may make measured V-I curves nonlinear. A useful independent verification is dimensional analysis, followed by one limiting case: for example, zero excitation, zero frequency, or no mechanical load. In magnetic systems, material saturation invalidates proportional field laws; in electric machines, parasitic resistance and friction introduce heat; and in measurements, uncertainty prevents an exact empirical conclusion.

Comprehensive Theoretical Derivations & Analytical Modeling

In electrical machinery analysis, quantitative formulation begins from Maxwell's integral laws applied to ferromagnetic geometries. For 7.1 Generator terminal characteristic, we define the magnetic circuit relationship:

$$\mathcal{F} = N I = \oint \vec{H} \cdot d\vec{l} = \Phi \left( \mathcal{R}_{\text{core}} + \mathcal{R}_{\text{gap}} \right)$$

where the reluctance $\mathcal{R}$ is governed by path length $l$, cross-sectional area $A$, and effective permeability $\mu = \mu_0 \mu_r$:

$$\mathcal{R} = \frac{l}{\mu A}$$

When analyzing electromechanical energy conversion in Unit 7, the differential mechanical force developed along coordinate $x$ is derived rigorously from the magnetic coenergy $W_f'(i, x)$:

$$F_e = \left. \frac{\partial W_f'(i, x)}{\partial x} \right|_{i = \text{const}} = \frac{1}{2} i^2 \frac{dL(x)}{dx}$$

For rotational devices, the developed electromagnetic torque becomes:

$$T_e = \left. \frac{\partial W_f'(i, \theta)}{\partial \theta} \right|_{i = \text{const}} = \frac{1}{2} i^2 \frac{dL(\theta)}{d\theta}$$

Under steady-state machine operation, terminal voltage balances internal induced electromotive force $E_a$ and winding impedance drops:

$$V_t = E_a \pm I_a R_a$$

where the sign reflects generator (+ for internal EMF) or motor (- for terminal supply) operating quadrants. Core loss segregation accounts for hysteresis loss $P_h = k_h f B_{\max}^n$ and eddy current loss $P_e = k_e f^2 B_{\max}^2$, providing complete physical fidelity.

§7.2 Self-excited shunt generation

7.2 Self-excited shunt generation

Physical model and mathematical relationship

Residual flux initiates emf, which excites the shunt field, increasing emf until magnetic saturation and field-circuit resistance determine equilibrium. Speed below critical may prevent voltage buildup. The core procedure is to select a reference polarity and sign convention, distinguish an ideal relation from an experimentally calibrated one, and account for quantities whose units differ. Electromagnetic quantities must be checked against the associated conservation law: electric current and flux linkage describe excitation; voltages and torques describe observable exchange; and losses reduce usable output. In rotating or time-varying equipment, steady-state expressions must not be extrapolated to startup without tracking stored energy.

§7.3 Compound and series generators

7.3 Compound and series generators

Physical model and mathematical relationship

Series excitation adds load-sensitive flux while shunt excitation supplies near-constant baseline; cumulative compound generators can compensate terminal drop. Differential compounding can reduce output voltage with load. The core procedure is to select a reference polarity and sign convention, distinguish an ideal relation from an experimentally calibrated one, and account for quantities whose units differ.
