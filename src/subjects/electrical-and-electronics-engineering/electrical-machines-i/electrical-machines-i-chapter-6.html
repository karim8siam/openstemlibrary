§6.1 Back EMF and armature current

6.1 Back EMF and armature current

Physical model and mathematical relationship

For a DC motor V=E_b+I_a R_a (neglecting brush drop) and E_b=k_e Phi omega. Back EMF increases as the rotor speeds up, reducing armature current. The core procedure is to select a reference polarity and sign convention, distinguish an ideal relation from an experimentally calibrated one, and account for quantities whose units differ. Electromagnetic quantities must be checked against the associated conservation law: electric current and flux linkage describe excitation; voltages and torques describe observable exchange; and losses reduce usable output. In rotating or time-varying equipment, steady-state expressions must not be extrapolated to startup without tracking stored energy.

Fully worked numerical example

For V=220 V, R_a=0.5 ohm and I_a=20 A, back emf is 210 V and armature copper loss 200 W. Write the given quantities in SI units before substitution, then compute the predicted response and its physical meaning. If an ideal result conflicts with a device rating, the correct engineering action is to question the simplifying assumptions rather than force the physical device to satisfy the equation. For a measured system, use its nameplate, empirical characteristic curves and operating limits to refine the estimate.

Failure modes, units, and boundary conditions

At standstill E_b≈0, so a direct start can draw excessive current unless intentionally limited. A useful independent verification is dimensional analysis, followed by one limiting case: for example, zero excitation, zero frequency, or no mechanical load. In magnetic systems, material saturation invalidates proportional field laws; in electric machines, parasitic resistance and friction introduce heat; and in measurements, uncertainty prevents an exact empirical conclusion.

Comprehensive Theoretical Derivations & Analytical Modeling

In electrical machinery analysis, quantitative formulation begins from Maxwell's integral laws applied to ferromagnetic geometries. For 6.1 Back EMF and armature current, we define the magnetic circuit relationship:

$$\mathcal{F} = N I = \oint \vec{H} \cdot d\vec{l} = \Phi \left( \mathcal{R}_{\text{core}} + \mathcal{R}_{\text{gap}} \right)$$

where the reluctance $\mathcal{R}$ is governed by path length $l$, cross-sectional area $A$, and effective permeability $\mu = \mu_0 \mu_r$:

$$\mathcal{R} = \frac{l}{\mu A}$$

When analyzing electromechanical energy conversion in Unit 6, the differential mechanical force developed along coordinate $x$ is derived rigorously from the magnetic coenergy $W_f'(i, x)$:

$$F_e = \left. \frac{\partial W_f'(i, x)}{\partial x} \right|_{i = \text{const}} = \frac{1}{2} i^2 \frac{dL(x)}{dx}$$

For rotational devices, the developed electromagnetic torque becomes:

$$T_e = \left. \frac{\partial W_f'(i, \theta)}{\partial \theta} \right|_{i = \text{const}} = \frac{1}{2} i^2 \frac{dL(\theta)}{d\theta}$$

Under steady-state machine operation, terminal voltage balances internal induced electromotive force $E_a$ and winding impedance drops:

$$V_t = E_a \pm I_a R_a$$

where the sign reflects generator (+ for internal EMF) or motor (- for terminal supply) operating quadrants. Core loss segregation accounts for hysteresis loss $P_h = k_h f B_{\max}^n$ and eddy current loss $P_e = k_e f^2 B_{\max}^2$, providing complete physical fidelity.

6.2 Torque and speed characteristics

For a shunt motor with nearly constant flux, torque is approximately proportional to armature current and speed falls modestly under increasing load. 6.4 Speed control and braking

Physical model and mathematical relationship

Armature voltage control changes speed below base speed; field weakening raises speed at reduced torque capability, subject to safe overspeed limits. Regenerative and dynamic braking handle energy differently.
