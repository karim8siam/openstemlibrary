§5.1 Theoretical Framework and Overview: Transmission Line Theory, Transient Reflections, and Smith Chart

Unit 5: Waveguides, resonators and planar propagation

What the unit establishes

Relate electromagnetic confinement to cutoff, dispersion, stored energy, loss and conductor geometry; compare hollow metallic guides with PCB transmission structures.

Reproducible investigation

Choose a rectangular waveguide and calculate TE10 cutoff for three broad-wall dimensions, keeping the dielectric fixed. At an operating frequency above cutoff, determine β, guide wavelength, group velocity and phase velocity. Explain why phase speed exceeding c is not a causality violation. Move frequency gradually toward cutoff and identify how a narrowband pulse might become delayed and distorted. Sketch the transverse field structure relative to the conducting walls; state why TEM is not supported in the ideal hollow single-conductor geometry. Then define a cavity with a loaded resonance and compute Q from bandwidth and approximate stored-energy decay. Compare this with a PCB microstrip whose effective dielectric constant and characteristic impedance are governed by fringing fields rather than a hollow guide's cutoff equation.

§5.2 Hollow conducting waveguide and modal cutoff

U05-L01: Hollow conducting waveguide and modal cutoff

Book 22 · Unit 5 — Waveguides, resonators and planar propagation Learning objective: Reconstruct the physical model, calculate one representative case and explain where its assumptions cease to apply.

Physical principles

A hollow metallic waveguide can support transverse electric and transverse magnetic modes, each with field patterns constrained by conducting walls. A uniform TEM mode is not supported by a single-conductor hollow rectangular waveguide under the usual ideal assumptions. At a given frequency, modes below cutoff do not carry traveling power along an infinitely uniform guide; their amplitudes decay evanescently. TE10 is the lowest-order propagating mode of a rectangular guide with broad dimension a larger than narrow dimension b. Selecting dimensions ensures one mode is useful across an operating band, but real mechanical tolerances and wall losses also matter.

Derivation and governing equations

For an ideal rectangular guide filled with dielectric ε,μ, the cutoff wavenumber is kc=πsqrt[(m/a)²+(n/b)²] and fc=(1/(2sqrt(με)))sqrt[(m/a)²+(n/b)²]. For TE10, fc=v/(2a). Above cutoff, β=sqrt[(ωsqrt(με))²−kc²]. The cutoffs come from satisfying conductor boundary conditions, not from the transmission-line quarter-wave formula.

Fully specified worked example

For an air-filled guide with a=22.86 mm, TE10 cutoff is c/(2a)≈6.56 GHz. At 10 GHz TE10 propagates. A narrower a=15 mm raises its cutoff to approximately 10 GHz, leaving operation near the cutoff and making delay and loss highly sensitive.

Specify which inputs remain fixed. When the result involves a signed voltage or complex amplitude, report the chosen polarity or phase reference; a magnitude alone cannot settle the sign. Compare the result against an analytically known limit rather than blindly trusting significant figures from a calculator.

Engineering decision scenario

A microwave bench may use a nominal rectangular guide chosen to keep higher-order modes below cutoff throughout the operating band. An adapter can excite unwanted modes locally even if they decay away in uniform sections. Treat the feed region separately from the long uniform guide.

Scope, failure modes and falsification

Ideal cutoffs ignore finite wall conductivity, rounded corners and dielectric tolerances. At cutoff, β approaches zero while attenuation may become important; ideal formulas can misleadingly suggest arbitrarily large phase velocity. Higher-order mode coupling at bends and discontinuities requires mode matching or full-wave modeling.

§5.3 Guide phase velocity, group velocity and dispersion

U05-L02: Guide phase velocity, group velocity and dispersion

Book 22 · Unit 5 — Waveguides, resonators and planar propagation Learning objective: Reconstruct the physical model, calculate one representative case and explain where its assumptions cease to apply.

Physical principles

Waveguide dispersion arises because a confined mode has transverse spatial structure in addition to axial propagation. The axial phase velocity can exceed the material's uniform plane-wave speed, while the group velocity stays below it in the ideal lossless guide above cutoff. Their product is v² for this simple model. This does not violate causality: phase fronts of a modal carrier and the energy or information envelope are not identical objects. Near cutoff the group velocity becomes small, which increases delay and sensitivity to fabrication and operating frequency.

Derivation and governing equations

For a lossless hollow guide, β=k sqrt[1−(fc/f)²], v_phase=v/sqrt[1−(fc/f)²], and v_group=v sqrt[1−(fc/f)²]. Guided wavelength λ_g=2π/β=λ/sqrt[1−(fc/f)²]. For TE modes the wave impedance is Z_TE=ωμ/β; it differs from both intrinsic impedance and an arbitrary circuit reference impedance.

Fully specified worked example

An air-filled guide with fc=6.5 GHz at f=10 GHz gives sqrt[1−(0.65)²]≈0.760. Thus v_group≈0.760c, v_phase≈1.316c, and guided wavelength≈(0.030 m)/0.760≈39.5 mm. The superluminal phase velocity is not a superluminal signal transmission rate.

Specify which inputs remain fixed. When the result involves a signed voltage or complex amplitude, report the chosen polarity or phase reference; a magnitude alone cannot settle the sign. Compare the result against an analytically known limit rather than blindly trusting significant figures from a calculator.

Engineering decision scenario

Precision phase shifters and high-Q structures depend on guided wavelength rather than free-space wavelength. A design that uses 30 mm as the 10 GHz phase period in the above guide underestimates the axial distance for a 2π phase shift. Record which wavelength each dimensional choice refers to.

Scope, failure modes and falsification

Real waveguides dissipate energy in walls and dielectrics. Multimode operation, bends and nonlinear material effects invalidate a single real β. Pulse propagation very near cutoff may distort strongly, so one group velocity cannot describe a wideband transient.

§5.4 Cavity resonators, energy storage and quality factor

U05-L03: Cavity resonators, energy storage and quality factor

Book 22 · Unit 5 — Waveguides, resonators and planar propagation Learning objective: Reconstruct the physical model, calculate one representative case and explain where its assumptions cease to apply.

Physical principles

A resonant electromagnetic cavity stores electric and magnetic energy in coupled standing-wave patterns. A cavity's resonant frequencies are set by shape, materials and boundary conditions, and its Q relates stored energy to loss per cycle. Q is a measure of narrow resonance and energy decay, not a direct measure of field amplitude independent of coupling. A cavity has intrinsic conductor and dielectric loss plus energy leakage through ports; the loaded Q differs from the unloaded Q. A narrow peak may be desirable for frequency selection but problematic for wideband signaling or thermal stability.

Derivation and governing equations

Define Q=ω0 U/P_loss for steady-state stored energy U and average dissipated power P_loss. For a weakly damped resonator, energy decay has a time constant τ_E≈Q/ω0 in the unloaded one-pole model. The approximate 3 dB bandwidth is Δf≈f0/Q_loaded for a well-isolated resonance. For parallel independent loss mechanisms, 1/Q_total≈Σ 1/Q_i; port coupling contributes its own loss term.

Fully specified worked example

A cavity near 2 GHz with loaded Q=1000 has approximate 3 dB bandwidth of 2 MHz. If average stored energy is 1 μJ, Q=1000 corresponds to total power loss ω0 U/Q≈0.0126 W. Those values describe a steady harmonic state; they do not alone determine field distribution or peak electric stress.

Engineering decision scenario

In a frequency filter, tighter bandwidth can reduce out-of-band interference but increases sensitivity to temperature and manufacturing tolerances. Coupling ports deliberately trade loaded Q against insertion loss and bandwidth. A complete filter specification requires more than a calculated isolated-cavity resonance.

Scope, failure modes and falsification

Near-degenerate modes, nonlinear dielectric behavior and strong external coupling complicate the simple single-resonance interpretation. Thermal drift changes dimensions and permittivity. A high-Q electromagnetic model does not establish breakdown or mechanical safety of the physical assembly.

§5.5 Microstrip fields, effective permittivity and dispersion

U05-L04: Microstrip fields, effective permittivity and dispersion

Book 22 · Unit 5 — Waveguides, resonators and planar propagation Learning objective: Reconstruct the physical model, calculate one representative case and explain where its assumptions cease to apply.

Physical principles

A microstrip trace above a ground plane confines much, but not all, of its electric field to the substrate. Because some field propagates in air and some in dielectric, an effective permittivity is used for an approximate quasi-TEM model. Geometry, substrate permittivity and trace width determine characteristic impedance; copper thickness and surface roughness modify high-frequency behavior. A PCB line should be understood as an electromagnetic structure with a return path, not as a top copper shape alone. Switching from a solid ground plane to a slotted return can invalidate a seemingly correct impedance calculation.

Derivation and governing equations

A common thin-trace empirical approximation for w/h≤1 is Z0≈(60/sqrt(ε_eff)) ln(8h/w+w/(4h)), and ε_eff≈(ε_r+1)/2+(ε_r−1)/(2sqrt(1+12h/w)). These are engineering approximations with limited thickness and frequency validity, not exact Maxwell solutions. The guided phase velocity is approximately c/sqrt(ε_eff), and physical quarter-wave length is c/(4f sqrt(ε_eff)).

Fully specified worked example

For εr=4, w=h, ε_eff≈2.5+1.5/sqrt(13)≈2.916. The approximate Z0 is (60/sqrt2.916) ln(8.25)≈74.1 Ω. Manufacturing width and substrate permittivity can shift this. A refined solver should be used to target an exact 50 Ω line.

Engineering decision scenario

A high-speed board designer may be tempted to calculate microstrip impedance without considering solder mask or copper plating. Both can perturb the effective field geometry. Route return-current continuity, connector transitions and bends must be checked even if a straight uniform section meets its nominal impedance.

Scope, failure modes and falsification

The quoted formulas omit thickness corrections, field fringing details, strong dispersion and certain high-frequency losses. They should not be extrapolated to wide traces or integrated lines outside their stated range. Finite ground planes and nearby traces introduce coupling that changes the modal structure.

The problem and experimental model

Required evidence

Independent benchmark and failure test

Before generating graphs, derive a baseline from the four unit lessons. Establish a numerical prediction from known dimensions, wave speed or material law. Run a second numerical calculation that uses a different formulation or a limiting case. Inspect whether the model conserves charge or power where the assumptions require it; use the proper amplitude or power reference. Compare input perturbations one at a time so the direction of the observed effect can be explained physically.

Analysis requirements

A statement such as 'the signal became better' is incomplete without specifying the chosen metric and the control variables. Include a short error budget separating numerical resolution, material uncertainty, geometrical approximation and measurement effects that would matter in a real experiment. An unexplained mismatch must remain visible in the report rather than being edited out.

§5.7 Analytical Derivations and Assessment: Transmission Line Theory, Transient Reflections, and Smith Chart

Unit 5 assessment: Waveguides, resonators and planar propagation

Eight subject-specific questions, with explanatory answers. Attempt all questions before reading the key. These are educational examples, not external exam past papers.

Question 1

Find air-filled TE10 cutoff for a=3 cm.

Answer and rationale: fc=c/(2a)≈5.00 GHz.

Question 2

Why does a single-conductor hollow rectangular waveguide lack a TEM mode?

Answer and rationale: A nontrivial static transverse potential difference cannot be maintained between two separate conductors because only one conductor boundary is present.

Question 3

What is β as frequency approaches cutoff from above?

Answer and rationale: β→0 for an ideal lossless guide, making guided wavelength very large.

Question 4

Why can waveguide v_phase exceed c without carrying information faster than c?

Answer and rationale: It is the speed of the axial phase pattern; group velocity and causal signal transport are different.

Question 5

A resonator at 1 GHz has loaded Q=500. What approximate bandwidth?

Answer and rationale: Δf=f0/Q≈2 MHz for an isolated weakly damped resonance.

Question 6

What makes loaded Q different from unloaded Q?

Answer and rationale: External ports extract energy in addition to intrinsic conductor and dielectric losses.

Question 7

Why is microstrip ε_eff often between air and substrate εr?

Answer and rationale: Fringing electromagnetic fields occupy both air and dielectric substrate.

Question 8

What must be checked before using a thin-trace impedance formula quantitatively?

Answer and rationale: Geometry ratio, copper thickness, dielectric frequency response, ground-plane continuity and formula applicability.

Beyond the answer key

For at least two numerical problems, write a second route to the result using a limiting case or power/charge conservation argument. For conceptual questions, identify a counterexample to an overgeneralized version of the answer. A problem is not fully understood until the applicable physical assumptions are stated alongside the formula.
