§7.1 Theoretical Framework and Overview: Cavity Resonators, Dielectric Waveguides, and Microstrip Lines

Unit 7: Electromagnetic compatibility and measurement

What the unit establishes

Trace how unintended coupling becomes conducted or radiated interference. Separate instrument-calibrated measurements from models, and use shielding and S-parameters without relying on misleading single-number claims.

Reproducible investigation

Compare three interference scenarios using realistic dimensional units: capacitive coupling from a switching voltage, inductive coupling from a current ramp and a shared-impedance return. Do not merely list their formulas; choose two independent parameters for each mechanism and predict the response when one changes. Construct a diagnostic plan to distinguish the mechanisms using waveform timing, loop geometry and routing. Next quantify absorption-only shielding for a homogeneous conductor, and deliberately add a seam as a separate leakage mechanism. Explain why an enormous theoretical absorption number cannot rescue a poorly bonded enclosure. Finish with complex network measurements: interpret S11 and S21 magnitudes as incident/reflected waves with defined reference impedances, and describe the calibration evidence needed before using them for component acceptance.

§7.2 Conducted, capacitive and inductive coupling paths

U07-L01: Conducted, capacitive and inductive coupling paths

Book 22 · Unit 7 — Electromagnetic compatibility and measurement Learning objective: Reconstruct the physical model, calculate one representative case and explain where its assumptions cease to apply.

Physical principles

Electromagnetic interference reaches a victim circuit through several physically distinct paths. Shared conductor impedance converts another circuit's current into unwanted voltage; capacitive coupling transfers displacement current from a changing voltage; inductive coupling links changing magnetic flux into an adjacent loop. Each mechanism favors different mitigation: return-path design, spacing, filtering, loop-area reduction, shielding or controlled impedance. Naming every unexplained noise signal electromagnetic radiation can lead to expensive ineffective fixes. A systematic diagnosis starts from frequency, source spectrum, coupling geometry, victim impedance and measurable transfer changes when one path is altered.

Derivation and governing equations

For a small mutual capacitance C_m, displacement current approximately i_c=C_m d(v_aggressor−v_victim)/dt under a lumped model. For mutual inductance M, induced voltage magnitude approximately |v_M|=M |di/dt|, with sign set by dot orientation. A shared impedance Z_g carries common-mode or return current and creates coupled voltage ΔV=Z_g I. These approximations require dimensions small enough that distributed propagation is not dominant.

Fully specified worked example

If C_m=2 pF and a neighboring node slews by 1 V/ns, capacitive current is approximately 2 mA. If M=10 nH and an aggressor current changes at 100 A/μs, induced voltage magnitude is 1 V. These effects can differ dramatically despite both being described informally as 'crosstalk'.

Independent numerical verification. Reconstruct the arithmetic in the preceding illustration using SI base units, then change one input by a factor of two and predict the consequence from the actual governing expression. Specify which inputs remain fixed. When the result involves a signed voltage or complex amplitude, report the chosen polarity or phase reference; a magnitude alone cannot settle the sign. Compare the result against an analytically known limit rather than blindly trusting significant figures from a calculator.

Engineering decision scenario

In a mixed-signal PCB, an ADC may become noisy only when a nearby switching regulator changes state. Test whether rerouting a return plane, shortening a loop or adding filtering changes the response. Compare time alignment with the switching edge and with the sampling instant, rather than immediately blaming the ADC quantizer.

Scope, failure modes and falsification

Lumped C and M are insufficient at high frequencies or across long cables, where transmission-line and antenna coupling dominate. Parasitic parameters are configuration-dependent. An estimated mutual capacitance does not account for victim amplifier input nonlinearity or frequency-dependent filtering.

§7.3 Shielding effectiveness, seams and aperture limits

U07-L02: Shielding effectiveness, seams and aperture limits

Book 22 · Unit 7 — Electromagnetic compatibility and measurement Learning objective: Reconstruct the physical model, calculate one representative case and explain where its assumptions cease to apply.

Physical principles

An enclosure can reduce coupled electromagnetic energy by reflecting, absorbing and redirecting fields, but its shielding performance depends strongly on field regime. Thin metal may block an electric field efficiently while struggling against low-frequency magnetic fields. Apertures, seams, conductive gaskets and cable penetrations can dominate leakage despite excellent bulk conductor conductivity. Shielding effectiveness is usually a measured ratio under a specified field setup; it is not a universal material property independent of frequency, orientation and source distance. A useful design tests the full assembly, not only a metal coupon.

Derivation and governing equations

One field-based shielding effectiveness convention is SE_E=20log10(|E_unshielded|/|E_shielded|) dB at matched observation geometry. In a simple good conductor, amplitude attenuation through thickness t can be approximated e^{-t/δ}, contributing 8.686 t/δ dB of absorption only, not the complete enclosure SE. Reflection, interference and apertures require additional modeling. For low-frequency magnetic fields, high-permeability materials can be more useful than conductivity alone.

Fully specified worked example

With an idealized conductor thickness of 3δ, pure absorption amplitude factor is e^-3≈0.0498, corresponding to 26.1 dB from that mechanism. A 1 mm open seam may defeat the expected benefit at relevant frequencies; the calculation does not predict the finished enclosure's shielding number.

Specify which inputs remain fixed. When the result involves a signed voltage or complex amplitude, report the chosen polarity or phase reference; a magnitude alone cannot settle the sign. Compare the result against an analytically known limit rather than blindly trusting significant figures from a calculator.

Engineering decision scenario

Suppose an instrument enclosure passes a laboratory shielding coupon test but fails radiated emission measurements. Investigate the cable entry and lid seam first. A long slit can behave as a slot radiator when electrically significant. Improving bulk wall thickness may do little compared with bonding a seam or providing a proper cable feedthrough filter.

Scope, failure modes and falsification

Near-field geometry, polarization, aperture resonance and measurement arrangement all affect SE. The absorption term alone cannot predict low-frequency shielding or free-space far-field enclosure performance. A simulation with ideal perfect electric conductor walls overstates the benefit of a real leaky assembly.

§7.4 Scattering parameters and network calibration

U07-L03: Scattering parameters and network calibration

Book 22 · Unit 7 — Electromagnetic compatibility and measurement Learning objective: Reconstruct the physical model, calculate one representative case and explain where its assumptions cease to apply.

Physical principles

At microwave frequencies it is often easier to measure incident and outgoing traveling waves at calibrated ports than to define every terminal voltage and current unambiguously. Scattering parameters relate normalized traveling-wave amplitudes for a linear network. S11 describes input reflection when other ports meet specified reference terminations; S21 describes forward transmission under the same conditions. A dB trace does not tell the whole story: phase response, reference impedance, calibration plane and uncertainty can decide whether a network is suitable for timing or power transfer.

Derivation and governing equations

For a two-port with normalized waves b=S a, S11=b1/a1 for a2=0 and S21=b2/a1 for a2=0. Return loss often means −20log10|S11| and insertion loss −20log10|S21| under equal real reference impedances and matched conditions. A passive lossless reciprocal two-port has S†S=I and S12=S21 under compatible port normalization; passive lossy networks satisfy a nonincreasing power constraint.

Fully specified worked example

If S11 magnitude is 0.1, return loss is 20 dB and reflected power is 1%. If |S21|=0.8, transmission magnitude is −1.94 dB and transmitted wave power fraction is 64%. The missing 35% after 1% reflection may be dissipation or another mode/port under the stipulated normalization and assumptions.

Specify which inputs remain fixed. When the result involves a signed voltage or complex amplitude, report the chosen polarity or phase reference; a magnitude alone cannot settle the sign. Compare the result against an analytically known limit rather than blindly trusting significant figures from a calculator.

Engineering decision scenario

A network analyzer calibrated at instrument connectors measures device-plus-cables unless de-embedding or calibration moves the reference planes. A small cable flex can perturb phase dramatically at microwave frequencies. Save Touchstone traces with calibration date, reference impedances, stimulus power, fixture description and temperature when reproducibility matters.

Scope, failure modes and falsification

An uncalibrated trace is not a material property. Nonlinearity, time variation and active gain violate simple passive scattering assumptions. If a network supports unmeasured radiation or higher modes, a two-port balance may misattribute power loss to heat.

§7.5 Wireless propagation, interference and engineering margins

U07-L04: Wireless propagation, interference and engineering margins

Book 22 · Unit 7 — Electromagnetic compatibility and measurement Learning objective: Reconstruct the physical model, calculate one representative case and explain where its assumptions cease to apply.

Physical principles

A real wireless channel combines path attenuation, shadowing, multipath, fading, delay spread and interference. The free-space path-loss equation is a reference, not an urban coverage guarantee. Fast multipath fading can change received power dramatically over distances comparable to a wavelength, while slow obstruction changes average power more gradually. Communication reliability depends on modulation, coding, receiver sensitivity, antenna diversity and medium access. Optimizing antenna gain while ignoring interference can fail: stronger received desired signal does not always improve the signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio by the same amount.

Derivation and governing equations

A log-distance average path-loss model can be written PL(d)=PL(d0)+10n log10(d/d0)+Xσ, with site-dependent exponent n and random shadow term Xσ. Instantaneous SINR=P_signal/(P_interference+P_noise) in linear power units. Receiver noise floor depends on kTB and noise figure; it is approximately −174 dBm/Hz thermal noise density near room temperature before device noise figure.

Fully specified worked example

For a site-specific n=3, increasing distance from 10 m to 100 m adds 30 dB mean path loss. If the receiver has 1 MHz bandwidth, room-temperature thermal noise is near −114 dBm before noise figure. With 6 dB noise figure the approximate input-referred noise floor becomes −108 dBm, ignoring interference.

Specify which inputs remain fixed. When the result involves a signed voltage or complex amplitude, report the chosen polarity or phase reference; a magnitude alone cannot settle the sign. Compare the result against an analytically known limit rather than blindly trusting significant figures from a calculator.

Engineering decision scenario

Before deploying a wireless IoT network, collect field measurements at representative locations and times. Record packet-delivery statistics, not only a snapshot of RSSI. Analyze the weakest required coverage location, battery lifetime and legal transmit constraints; topology and gateway placement may solve the problem more cheaply than a higher-power transmitter.

Scope, failure modes and falsification

The log-distance exponent and shadowing statistics must be measured for the relevant environment; inventing one and presenting it as an observed channel parameter is invalid. SINR thresholds depend on waveform and receiver design. The model does not capture moving objects or spatially correlated interference by itself.

Unit 7 engineering project: Identify the actual EMC coupling path

Type: Computational desk investigation using synthetic data only. No mains wiring or high-voltage hardware work.

The problem and experimental model

Construct three synthetic coupling cases: mutual capacitance, mutual inductance and shared ground impedance. Change slew rates and current-loop geometry separately to compare predicted interference. Propose a different mitigation for each case and justify why sheet-metal thickness alone may not help a seam-dominated enclosure.

Required evidence

Include source/victim diagrams, predicted units, before/after sensitivity plots, and a plan for a calibrated instrument test rather than claiming simulated traces are observations.

Independent benchmark and failure test

Before generating graphs, derive a baseline from the four unit lessons. Establish a numerical prediction from known dimensions, wave speed or material law. Run a second numerical calculation that uses a different formulation or a limiting case. Inspect whether the model conserves charge or power where the assumptions require it; use the proper amplitude or power reference. Compare input perturbations one at a time so the direction of the observed effect can be explained physically.

Analysis requirements

Every plot must label input units, dependent quantities, coordinate reference and whether the curve is synthetic. A statement such as 'the signal became better' is incomplete without specifying the chosen metric and the control variables. Include a short error budget separating numerical resolution, material uncertainty, geometrical approximation and measurement effects that would matter in a real experiment. An unexplained mismatch must remain visible in the report rather than being edited out.

Deliverables and grading

A concise technical report should include: purpose and assumptions (15%), derivation and units (20%), numerical verification (25%), parameter sensitivity and omitted physics (25%), and evidence-based engineering recommendation (15%). Name one specific datum whose collection would change the recommendation. Do not claim that a synthetic dataset confirms real equipment performance.

§7.7 Analytical Derivations and Assessment: Cavity Resonators, Dielectric Waveguides, and Microstrip Lines

Unit 7 assessment: Electromagnetic compatibility and measurement

Eight subject-specific questions, with explanatory answers. Attempt all questions before reading the key. These are educational examples, not external exam past papers.

Question 1

What is induced voltage for M=20 nH and di/dt=50 A/μs?

Answer and rationale: M di/dt=20e-9×50e6=1 V magnitude.

Question 2

Why can reducing signal loop area mitigate magnetic crosstalk?

Answer and rationale: Smaller loop area generally reduces linked time-varying magnetic flux under comparable field conditions.

Question 3

What absorption-only field attenuation follows thickness t=2δ?

Answer and rationale: e^-2≈0.135 amplitude, or 17.4 dB; it is not the total enclosure shielding effectiveness.

Question 4

Can a seam dominate leakage when sheet-metal absorption is excellent?

Answer and rationale: Yes, a long aperture can provide a strong transmission path or resonant slot-like radiation.

Question 5

If |S11|=0.2, what power fraction is reflected?

Answer and rationale: 4% under equal real power-wave reference definitions.

Question 6

Why does moving a VNA calibration reference plane matter?

Answer and rationale: Extra line propagation rotates S-parameter phase and can add loss or mismatch.

Question 7

With n=3, what mean path-loss increase accompanies doubling distance?

Answer and rationale: 10n log10(2)≈9.03 dB under the log-distance empirical model.

Question 8

Why is RSSI alone insufficient to guarantee packet reliability?

Answer and rationale: Interference, noise, multipath, waveform, coding, receiver behavior and temporal fading affect packet delivery.

Beyond the answer key

For at least two numerical problems, write a second route to the result using a limiting case or power/charge conservation argument. For conceptual questions, identify a counterexample to an overgeneralized version of the answer. A problem is not fully understood until the applicable physical assumptions are stated alongside the formula.
