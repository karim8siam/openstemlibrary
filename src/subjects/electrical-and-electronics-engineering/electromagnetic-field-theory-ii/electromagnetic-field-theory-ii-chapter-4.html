§4.1 Theoretical Framework and Overview: Reflection, Refraction, and Oblique Incidence Boundaries

Unit 4: Transmission lines and impedance matching

What the unit establishes

Build the distributed RLGC model, follow traveling waves through discontinuities, calculate reflected power and standing-wave patterns, and evaluate finite-length matching approaches.

Reproducible investigation

Model a 50 Ω line terminated with 25, 50 and 100 Ω loads. For each, derive the voltage reflection coefficient, returned power and standing-wave ratio. Verify the phase change when shifting the reference plane by one-eighth wavelength. Launch a synthetic step and predict the sign and arrival time of the first reflected edge; convert its delay into a discontinuity location using a declared propagation velocity. Introduce two discontinuities and ask which portions of the trace involve re-reflection rather than direct first returns. End by calculating a quarter-wave section matching two real impedances at one center frequency. Compare its impedance transformation at the center with two off-center frequencies, explaining in words why matching is fundamentally a band-limited design process.

§4.2 RLGC parameters and the telegrapher equations

U04-L01: RLGC parameters and the telegrapher equations

Book 22 · Unit 4 — Transmission lines and impedance matching Learning objective: Reconstruct the physical model, calculate one representative case and explain where its assumptions cease to apply.

Physical principles

A transmission line has continuously distributed inductance and capacitance per length, plus resistive and dielectric losses per length. Its voltage and current vary with both distance and time, unlike a lumped circuit node with one universal voltage. The telegrapher equations capture signal propagation and attenuation without resolving every microscopic field detail. The RLGC parameters summarize a chosen frequency band and transverse geometry; they may vary with frequency due to skin and dielectric effects. The characteristic impedance is a relation between forward voltage and forward current waves, not necessarily the DC resistance between the two conductors.

Derivation and governing equations

The time-domain equations are ∂V/∂z=−R′I−L′∂I/∂t and ∂I/∂z=−G′V−C′∂V/∂t. In phasor form γ=sqrt[(R′+jωL′)(G′+jωC′)] and Z0=sqrt[(R′+jωL′)/(G′+jωC′)]. With R′=G′=0, Z0=sqrt(L′/C′), v=1/sqrt(L′C′), and voltage admits V+=V0+e^{-jβz} and V−=V0−e^{+jβz}.

Fully specified worked example

For L′=250 nH/m and C′=100 pF/m, Z0=sqrt(250e-9/100e-12)=50 Ω. Propagation speed is 1/sqrt(2.5e-17)=2e8 m/s, corresponding to an effective dielectric factor (c/v)²≈2.25. A 1 m section introduces 5 ns of one-way delay in the lossless approximation.

Reconstruct the arithmetic in the preceding illustration using SI base units, then change one input by a factor of two and predict the consequence from the actual governing expression. Specify which inputs remain fixed. When the result involves a signed voltage or complex amplitude, report the chosen polarity or phase reference; a magnitude alone cannot settle the sign. Compare the result against an analytically known limit rather than blindly trusting significant figures from a calculator.

Engineering decision scenario

High-speed digital links include a signal conductor and return path, forming an electromagnetic field structure. The topology of the return and the dielectric stack determine characteristic impedance. A PCB routing change affects L′ and C′ simultaneously; it is insufficient to calculate only the copper resistance. Use a field solver or validated geometry formulas for quantitative PCB design.

Scope, failure modes and falsification

The quasi-TEM distributed model assumes a cross-section admitting a useful voltage/current definition. Near a higher-order-mode cutoff, a connector discontinuity or a complex three-dimensional bend, a single RLGC pair may be inadequate. Frequency-independent parameters also fail when dielectric and conductor losses change significantly across the signal spectrum.

§4.3 Traveling waves and load reflection coefficient

U04-L02: Traveling waves and load reflection coefficient

Book 22 · Unit 4 — Transmission lines and impedance matching Learning objective: Reconstruct the physical model, calculate one representative case and explain where its assumptions cease to apply.

Physical principles

A finite line terminated in a load generally supports both forward and backward waves. The mismatch at the load creates the reflected voltage wave while current sign reverses for that backward traveling component. The reflection coefficient Γ is complex and varies with reference plane. Moving a measurement plane along a lossless line changes phase but not |Γ|. Matching Z_L=Z0 eliminates load reflection in the single-mode lossless case, a property independent of the line length. A matched load absorbs the incident power; an open or short returns it in the ideal lossless steady-state model.

Derivation and governing equations

At the load Γ_L=(Z_L−Z0)/(Z_L+Z0). For real positive load and line impedances, reflected power fraction=|Γ|². On lossless line Γ(z)=Γ_L exp(2jβz) under one coordinate convention, and VSWR=(1+|Γ|)/(1−|Γ|) when |Γ|<1. The input impedance Z_in=Z0(Z_L+jZ0 tanβl)/(Z0+jZ_L tanβl) for a lossless line.

Fully specified worked example

With Z0=50 Ω and Z_L=100 Ω, Γ=1/3, returned power fraction=1/9≈11.1%, and VSWR=2. With a 10 W forward incident wave, ideal reflected power is 1.11 W; accepted load power is 8.89 W. These are wave powers at the reference plane, not total transmitter DC power.

Specify which inputs remain fixed. When the result involves a signed voltage or complex amplitude, report the chosen polarity or phase reference; a magnitude alone cannot settle the sign. Compare the result against an analytically known limit rather than blindly trusting significant figures from a calculator.

Engineering decision scenario

A vector network analyzer reads a calibrated complex S11 at a connector plane. If a cable precedes the load, a phase rotation changes the measured angle, and cable loss reduces returned amplitude. A technician must know the calibration reference and frequency band before declaring the physical load well matched from a single reading.

Scope, failure modes and falsification

The simple formulas assume one propagating mode and linear terminations. A real source mismatch causes additional bounces, and a lossy line makes reflected power at the instrument plane smaller than at the load. A negative real resistance can yield |Γ|>1 and violates the passive-load assumptions behind VSWR interpretation.

§4.4 Time-domain reflectometry and step transients

U04-L03: Time-domain reflectometry and step transients

Book 22 · Unit 4 — Transmission lines and impedance matching Learning objective: Reconstruct the physical model, calculate one representative case and explain where its assumptions cease to apply.

Physical principles

A fast voltage step launched down a line travels to impedance discontinuities and returns as a reflected step. Time-domain reflectometry estimates the round-trip travel time, and hence location, using a known propagation speed. The sign of the returning voltage distinguishes higher from lower impedance at a simple boundary: an open-ended high impedance gives a positive reflection, while a short-ended low impedance gives a negative reflection. Multiple discontinuities create re-reflections, and a finite-bandwidth step blurs close features. A raw time trace without calibration can misidentify connector mismatch as a distant cable fault.

Derivation and governing equations

For a discontinuity at distance d and propagation speed v, a first return arrives approximately at t=2d/v. With a launched unit step, the first reflected step has amplitude Γ=(Z_next−Z0)/(Z_next+Z0) under the local one-step interface model. For a 50-to-75 Ω discontinuity Γ=0.2; the returned voltage step is positive and one fifth the incident amplitude.

Fully specified worked example

A 12 m line with v=2×10^8 m/s gives round-trip time 120 ns. A measured return at 80 ns under the same speed assumption suggests a feature 8 m away. If the true velocity is 1.8×10^8 m/s, the location estimate becomes 7.2 m; dielectric uncertainty directly enters fault position uncertainty.

Reconstruct the arithmetic in the preceding illustration using SI base units, then change one input by a factor of two and predict the consequence from the actual governing expression. Specify which inputs remain fixed. When the result involves a signed voltage or complex amplitude, report the chosen polarity or phase reference; a magnitude alone cannot settle the sign. Compare the result against an analytically known limit rather than blindly trusting significant figures from a calculator.

Engineering decision scenario

When surveying a coaxial cable installed in a building, first measure a known cable to estimate its effective propagation speed, then inspect the test cable. Keep bends, adapters and probe bandwidth recorded. The returned trace is a convolution of the launch pulse, the line response and measurement instrument bandwidth.

Scope, failure modes and falsification

Several closely spaced discontinuities may not be individually resolvable. Severe attenuation can hide a distant reflection, and multiple reflections complicate simple location-by-time calculations. TDR results are diagnostic inferences, not direct images of interior cable structure.

§4.5 Quarter-wave matching and Smith-chart reasoning

U04-L04: Quarter-wave matching and Smith-chart reasoning

Book 22 · Unit 4 — Transmission lines and impedance matching Learning objective: Reconstruct the physical model, calculate one representative case and explain where its assumptions cease to apply.

Physical principles

Impedance matching converts a load into an input impedance acceptable to a source, usually over a finite frequency range. A lossless quarter-wave line interposed between real resistances transforms impedance approximately as Z_in=Z_t²/Z_L at its design frequency. A Smith chart compactly plots normalized complex impedance and reflection coefficient; moving along a lossless line follows a constant-|Γ| circle. Matching does not destroy load mismatch by magic: it creates a structure whose multiple reflections cancel at a chosen reference plane under the design assumptions. Bandwidth, power handling and realizable line impedance set the practical limits.

Derivation and governing equations

For real Z_S and Z_L, choose transformer impedance Z_t=sqrt(Z_S Z_L) and electrical length λ/4 at f0. The input equals Z_t²/Z_L=Z_S at f0. A lossless line of physical length l=λ_g/4 uses the guided wavelength, not free-space wavelength. The Smith chart impedance coordinate z=(1+Γ)/(1−Γ) and Γ=(z−1)/(z+1) with z=Z/Z0.

Fully specified worked example

To match 50 Ω to 100 Ω at 1 GHz using a medium with propagation speed 2×10^8 m/s, Z_t=sqrt(5000)=70.71 Ω and λ_g=0.20 m. The quarter-wave physical length is 0.050 m. At 500 MHz the same structure is λ/8, so perfect design-frequency matching cannot be assumed.

Specify which inputs remain fixed. When the result involves a signed voltage or complex amplitude, report the chosen polarity or phase reference; a magnitude alone cannot settle the sign. Compare the result against an analytically known limit rather than blindly trusting significant figures from a calculator.

Engineering decision scenario

A radio front end may use a microstrip quarter-wave transformer because it is simple to fabricate. Layout must account for dielectric effective permittivity, fringing at transitions, conductor loss and substrate manufacturing tolerance. When bandwidth is demanding, a multisection transformer or lumped matching network may be more suitable.

Scope, failure modes and falsification

The formula assumes a real load and source, a lossless ideal line and exact design frequency. Reactive loads require additional transformations. A matched input under one source condition does not guarantee maximum bandwidth, optimal noise figure, minimal distortion or safe voltage stress.

Distinguish a perfect matched test from a mismatched network-analyzer trace.

Required evidence

Independent benchmark and failure test

Before generating graphs, derive a baseline from the four unit lessons. Establish a numerical prediction from known dimensions, wave speed or material law. Run a second numerical calculation that uses a different formulation or a limiting case. Inspect whether the model conserves charge or power where the assumptions require it; use the proper amplitude or power reference. Compare input perturbations one at a time so the direction of the observed effect can be explained physically.

Analysis requirements

Every plot must label input units, dependent quantities, coordinate reference and whether the curve is synthetic. A statement such as 'the signal became better' is incomplete without specifying the chosen metric and the control variables. Include a short error budget separating numerical resolution, material uncertainty, geometrical approximation and measurement effects that would matter in a real experiment. An unexplained mismatch must remain visible in the report rather than being edited out.

§4.7 Analytical Derivations and Assessment: Reflection, Refraction, and Oblique Incidence Boundaries

Unit 4 assessment: Transmission lines and impedance matching

Eight subject-specific questions, with explanatory answers. Attempt all questions before reading the key. These are educational examples, not external exam past papers.

Question 1

For L′=400 nH/m, C′=100 pF/m, determine Z0.

Answer and rationale: sqrt(400e-9/100e-12)=sqrt(4000)≈63.25 Ω.

Question 2

Why is line Z0 not generally the copper DC resistance?

Answer and rationale: Z0 is the traveling voltage/current ratio related primarily to distributed field energy, while DC resistance is a dissipative quantity.

Question 3

Calculate Γ at a 50 Ω line terminated by 25 Ω.

Answer and rationale: Γ=(25−50)/(25+50)=−1/3, indicating reflected voltage polarity reversal.

Question 4

What VSWR results from |Γ|=0.5?

Answer and rationale: (1+0.5)/(1−0.5)=3.

Question 5

A return arrives 200 ns after launch on a line at 2e8 m/s. How far away is the feature?

Answer and rationale: d=v t/2=20 m.

Question 6

What instrumental effect limits resolution of close TDR discontinuities?

Answer and rationale: Finite step rise time and bandwidth blur multiple returns into overlapping features.

Question 7

What Zt matches 75 Ω to 300 Ω using a quarter-wave transformer?

Answer and rationale: sqrt(75×300)=150 Ω at the single design frequency.

Question 8

Why does a λ/4 transformer not ensure broadband match?

Answer and rationale: Electrical length and impedance transformation vary with frequency; ideal matching is centered on a specified design condition.

Beyond the answer key

For at least two numerical problems, write a second route to the result using a limiting case or power/charge conservation argument. For conceptual questions, identify a counterexample to an overgeneralized version of the answer. A problem is not fully understood until the applicable physical assumptions are stated alongside the formula.
