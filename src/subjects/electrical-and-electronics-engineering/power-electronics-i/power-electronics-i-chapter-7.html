§7.1 Foundational Principles & Curriculum Scope: Three-Phase Bridge Rectifiers: Commutation Overlap and Harmonic Profiles

Unit 7 — Small-signal dynamics and feedback

The engineering argument

A controlled converter is not just a static voltage ratio. Stored energy, load impedance, control delay, sensors and saturation shape its dynamic response. A compensator designed from an inappropriate low-frequency approximation may oscillate when the real converter has additional poles or zeros.

We linearize a buck stage near a nonzero operating point and compare open-loop plant dynamics with a closed-loop response. The objective is to understand what a feedback controller can and cannot fix using measured states.

Boost conversion contains a nonminimum-phase effect in continuous voltage-mode control: raising duty initially reduces the fraction of cycle delivering current to the output even though it can eventually raise the voltage. This limits achievable voltage-loop speed.

Dynamic models, regulation and delay. A static conversion equation is only an equilibrium condition. For feedback design, the inductor current and capacitor voltage obey differential equations; a control input changes their rates rather than changing the output instantaneously. Averaging yields useful low-frequency dynamics while hiding individual switching edges.

The reference command, measured output, actuator duty and applied duty should appear separately in every feedback validation plot. If the requested duty saturates while a numerical integrator keeps accumulating error, an apparently mysterious recovery overshoot has a specific mechanism: windup.

An additional delay in sampling, computation, filtering or PWM update shifts phase. It may be negligible relative to a very slow loop but decisive for a loop pushed toward switching frequency. The proper comparison is delay expressed as a fraction of the loop period.

A boost stage exhibits a particularly important limitation when commanded duty produces an initial output response in the direction opposite the eventual steady change. That nonminimum-phase effect requires a deliberately conservative loop design rather than an optimistic retuning of proportional gain.

Sequence and learning objectives

In the first lesson, identify the model, boundary and relevant physical state. In the second, compute the dominant currents, voltages or losses under a specified operating point. In the third, investigate where the ideal result ceases to hold. In the fourth, convert the model into a design review with independently falsifiable tests. This sequence is intended to prevent equation memorization without comprehension. The central question is not whether a formula can be remembered but whether the learner can recognize when it should not be used.

§7.2 Averaged buck state equations

Lesson 7.1 — Averaged buck state equations

Unit: Small-signal dynamics and feedback. Focus: energy balance, switching-state modeling, numerical prediction, design limitations, verification. Prerequisites: basic circuit analysis, the definition of average and RMS values, and the relevant preceding lessons. Safety: This is mathematical and simulated content; it does not license direct connection to mains or high-energy battery systems.

What the model is trying to explain

In the ideal continuous-conduction buck model, L di_L/dt = d Vin − v_C and C dv_C/dt = i_L − v_C/R. Equilibrium requires v_C=D Vin and i_L=v_C/R. These differential equations reveal transients absent from the static voltage-ratio formula.

The starting point is an electrical system with a stated input, a desired output, and at least one physical storage or loss mechanism. Choose voltage and current arrows before writing any balance equation. This apparently clerical step determines whether a negative term represents energy returned to a source, dissipated heat, or a mismatch of sign conventions. For a switching system the state of the controlled semiconductor changes the governing differential equation; a mean-value formula follows only after the switching states and the relevant steady-state conditions have been established. A time-domain numerical result should therefore always be accompanied by the state assumptions under which it was computed.

Complete numerical walkthrough

With Vin=24 V, d=0.5 and R=6 ohms, equilibrium voltage is 12 V and inductor current is 2 A. For L=100 microhenrys and C=470 microfarads, the undamped LC natural frequency is 1/(2π√LC)≈734 Hz before including load damping and parasitics.

How to verify the arithmetic independently. First convert every milliohm, microsecond, microfarad, kilohertz and microhenry into consistent base units. Next substitute the stated values into the governing relation in one line before rounding. Keep a separate column for the measured or assumed quantity and the calculated quantity. After calculation, check the physical limiting case: at zero load there should be no load power, at zero switching interval there should not be energy transfer through an ideal PWM command, and the sign of a voltage cannot be changed by efficiency alone. These simple limiting checks often expose a wrong topology or an invalid formula more reliably than another decimal place.

For a useful second calculation, change one design variable while holding the others fixed. If a capacitor is changed, do not simultaneously alter switching frequency and load current, because the effect becomes difficult to attribute. Record which quantities are genuinely controlled and which are consequences of the change. Where the model is an approximation, report its domain of validity beside the number; a precise numerical answer derived outside the model's domain can be more misleading than a rough answer with honest uncertainty.

Why a technically plausible answer may be wrong

Averaging filters out switching ripple; it cannot predict MOSFET commutation spikes or subharmonic instability of a specific peak-current-mode controller without extra states.

In particular, distinguish a device rating, a nominal operating point, a predicted steady-state waveform and a measured transient peak. The four are not interchangeable. An ideal average voltage says little about a nanosecond-level overshoot; a calculated RMS current does not establish that a junction temperature is acceptable; a controller response without saturation says nothing about a hard overcurrent event. The reader should identify the first assumption likely to fail at an extreme input, a very light load, a startup event, or a component temperature different from the nominal value.

Engineering decision and defensible tradeoff

Write the model in matrix form for states [i_L,v_C]. Find the equilibrium and compare it to one numerical integration of the averaged differential equations.

State the requirement as a measurable inequality or a bounded interval instead of using only adjectives such as fast, small, efficient or robust. For example, a requirement for output voltage has to name the input-voltage range, load range, transient duration and maximum allowed deviation. A requirement for low loss has to identify the ambient-temperature condition and whether the gate driver or controller is included in the loss accounting. A comparison among device options is not reproducible unless all alternatives are evaluated under the same service conditions.

Also note what is sacrificed when the selected variable is improved. Raising frequency can decrease filter size but increase switching heat; lowering channel resistance may increase gate-charge cost; larger capacitance can improve low-frequency hold-up while increasing inrush; stronger voltage-loop action can improve a small load step but erode phase margin at a different point. Engineering is the management of coupled constraints rather than the maximization of one attractive metric.

Laboratory thinking and falsifiable observations

Investigative question: What information is missing from the averaged model when selecting a voltage probe bandwidth?

Develop a small simulated experiment with two deliberately different operating conditions and one held-constant reference. The output of the experiment should be a table with physical units, a plotted response and an explicit criterion that would disprove the initial interpretation. Include a counterexample that changes the topology or its conduction mode. If your model predicts an impossible output at a duty limit, do not hide that point; explain which neglected physical mechanism becomes dominant and replace the formula with an appropriate higher-fidelity model before continuing.

To compare simulation with future measurements, define where voltage is measured, how current direction is assigned, the required bandwidth, and the averaging interval. If the result is efficiency, synchronize input and output data. If the result is a switching peak, use appropriately rated equipment and identify probe-induced artifacts. If the result is a protection threshold, include comparator tolerance and intervention latency. This is a review workflow, not an instruction to energize unvalidated hardware.

§7.3 Load regulation, PI compensation and windup

Lesson 7.2 — Load regulation, PI compensation and windup

What the model is trying to explain

§7.4 Boost nonminimum-phase response

Lesson 7.3 — Boost nonminimum-phase response

Unit: Small-signal dynamics and feedback. Focus: energy balance, switching-state modeling, numerical prediction, design limitations, verification. Prerequisites: basic circuit analysis, the definition of average and RMS values, and the relevant preceding lessons. Safety: This is mathematical and simulated content; it does not license direct connection to mains or high-energy battery systems.

What the model is trying to explain

In continuous-conduction boost voltage-mode control, increasing duty lengthens the interval when the inductor does not deliver current to the output. The output capacitor can initially discharge faster, even as the eventual steady voltage rises. This inverse response is associated with a right-half-plane zero in the duty-to-output transfer function.

Competing design alternatives

Compare two configurations with a fixed source, load and service requirement. One should prioritize lower conduction loss or larger energy storage, and the other should prioritize faster switching, smaller size or more economical control. Build a scorecard with heat, component stress, signal noise, cost and verification effort. Give each criterion a unit or a precise definition rather than assigning arbitrary star ratings.

The objective is to learn why there is rarely one universally best component.

§7.5 Control bandwidth, sensing and digital delay

What the model is trying to explain

A closed-loop power stage samples a filtered output and applies duty updates after computation and PWM timing delays. Delay contributes phase lag, while sensor noise grows at high bandwidth. A sound loop design trades fast disturbance rejection against switching ripple, noise and stability margin.

Engineering is the management of coupled constraints rather than the maximization of one attractive metric.
