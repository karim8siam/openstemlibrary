§8.1 Foundational Principles & Curriculum Scope: Passive Component Selection: High-Frequency Inductors and Capacitors

Unit 8 — Magnetics, layout, EMC and safe validation

The engineering argument

Converters combine high di/dt current loops with high dv/dt nodes, magnetic fields and thermal gradients. Compact layouts, suitable magnetics and carefully planned measurements are required to turn a correct equation into a repeatable circuit. The physical return path matters as much as the drawn schematic line.

This unit closes the volume with practical, low-energy laboratory and documentation habits: magnetic loss estimates, current loop geometry, ripple measurement, thermal uncertainty and an end-to-end converter verification case.

EMC performance and electrical safety are not proven by simulation or a quiet oscilloscope trace. Layout revisions, cables and enclosure changes alter conducted and radiated behavior; compliance requires the applicable formal testing.

Physical construction and uncertainty. Magnetic components are not single ideal inductance values over all frequency and current. Winding copper has resistive and proximity effects; core material loses energy each cycle and can saturate. Nominal inductance measured with a small signal is not a warranty that the part will maintain that value under fault current.

The highest-frequency switching current seeks a short local commutation loop. A long path with small but finite inductance generates voltage overshoot under rapid current change. An effective board review starts by identifying the fast-current loops on the actual layout, not by admiring the logical schematic.

Measurement changes the circuit. An oversized probe ground lead can add an LC resonance and make a switching node appear worse than it is. At the opposite extreme, inadequate bandwidth can hide damaging spikes. Both failure modes demand suitable and documented measurement setup.

Release evidence is strongest when mathematical checks, simulation, instrument calibration, thermal measurements and independent design review agree within stated uncertainty. The word verified must refer to a named test with a clear pass criterion, not simply to the existence of a graph.

Sequence and learning objectives

In the first lesson, identify the model, boundary and relevant physical state. In the second, compute the dominant currents, voltages or losses under a specified operating point. In the third, investigate where the ideal result ceases to hold. In the fourth, convert the model into a design review with independently falsifiable tests. This sequence is intended to prevent equation memorization without comprehension. The central question is not whether a formula can be remembered but whether the learner can recognize when it should not be used.

§8.2 Inductor saturation and magnetic loss

Lesson 8.1 — Inductor saturation and magnetic loss

Unit: Magnetics, layout, EMC and safe validation. Focus: energy balance, switching-state modeling, numerical prediction, design limitations, verification. Prerequisites: basic circuit analysis, the definition of average and RMS values, and the relevant preceding lessons. Safety: This is mathematical and simulated content; it does not license direct connection to mains or high-energy battery systems.

What the model is trying to explain

A real inductor has copper loss, core loss and finite current-dependent inductance. Saturation lowers effective inductance, increasing di/dt and peak current, sometimes creating a runaway stress sequence during overload. Core loss depends on flux swing, frequency and material rather than DC resistance alone.

The starting point is an electrical system with a stated input, a desired output, and at least one physical storage or loss mechanism. Choose voltage and current arrows before writing any balance equation. This apparently clerical step determines whether a negative term represents energy returned to a source, dissipated heat, or a mismatch of sign conventions. For a switching system the state of the controlled semiconductor changes the governing differential equation; a mean-value formula follows only after the switching states and the relevant steady-state conditions have been established. A time-domain numerical result should therefore always be accompanied by the state assumptions under which it was computed.

Complete numerical walkthrough

An inductor with DCR=45 milliohms carrying 3 A RMS dissipates 0.405 W in simplified copper loss. If the real operating frequency drives 0.7 W additional core loss, total magnetics heat is about 1.105 W; its temperature rise still needs a thermal model.

How to verify the arithmetic independently. First convert every milliohm, microsecond, microfarad, kilohertz and microhenry into consistent base units. Next substitute the stated values into the governing relation in one line before rounding. Keep a separate column for the measured or assumed quantity and the calculated quantity. After calculation, check the physical limiting case: at zero load there should be no load power, at zero switching interval there should not be energy transfer through an ideal PWM command, and the sign of a voltage cannot be changed by efficiency alone. These simple limiting checks often expose a wrong topology or an invalid formula more reliably than another decimal place.

For a useful second calculation, change one design variable while holding the others fixed. If a capacitor is changed, do not simultaneously alter switching frequency and load current, because the effect becomes difficult to attribute. Record which quantities are genuinely controlled and which are consequences of the change. Where the model is an approximation, report its domain of validity beside the number; a precise numerical answer derived outside the model's domain can be more misleading than a rough answer with honest uncertainty.

Why a technically plausible answer may be wrong

A label reading 100 microhenrys need not remain valid at 6 A. Manufacturer curves may define saturation at a chosen 10% or 20% inductance reduction, not a sharp physical cliff.

In particular, distinguish a device rating, a nominal operating point, a predicted steady-state waveform and a measured transient peak. The four are not interchangeable. An ideal average voltage says little about a nanosecond-level overshoot; a calculated RMS current does not establish that a junction temperature is acceptable; a controller response without saturation says nothing about a hard overcurrent event. The reader should identify the first assumption likely to fail at an extreme input, a very light load, a startup event, or a component temperature different from the nominal value.

Engineering decision and defensible tradeoff

Estimate magnetic ripple and current peaks at maximum Vin, then evaluate worst-case core heating at frequency extremes. Record whether the chosen inductance is rated hot, cold or at a special bias.

State the requirement as a measurable inequality or a bounded interval instead of using only adjectives such as fast, small, efficient or robust. For example, a requirement for output voltage has to name the input-voltage range, load range, transient duration and maximum allowed deviation. A requirement for low loss has to identify the ambient-temperature condition and whether the gate driver or controller is included in the loss accounting. A comparison among device options is not reproducible unless all alternatives are evaluated under the same service conditions.

Also note what is sacrificed when the selected variable is improved. Raising frequency can decrease filter size but increase switching heat; lowering channel resistance may increase gate-charge cost; larger capacitance can improve low-frequency hold-up while increasing inrush; stronger voltage-loop action can improve a small load step but erode phase margin at a different point. Engineering is the management of coupled constraints rather than the maximization of one attractive metric.

Laboratory thinking and falsifiable observations

Investigative question: How can two inductors with identical nominal L and DCR behave differently at the same switching frequency?

Develop a small simulated experiment with two deliberately different operating conditions and one held-constant reference. The output of the experiment should be a table with physical units, a plotted response and an explicit criterion that would disprove the initial interpretation. Include a counterexample that changes the topology or its conduction mode. If your model predicts an impossible output at a duty limit, do not hide that point; explain which neglected physical mechanism becomes dominant and replace the formula with an appropriate higher-fidelity model before continuing.

To compare simulation with future measurements, define where voltage is measured, how current direction is assigned, the required bandwidth, and the averaging interval. If the result is efficiency, synchronize input and output data. If the result is a switching peak, use appropriately rated equipment and identify probe-induced artifacts. If the result is a protection threshold, include comparator tolerance and intervention latency. This is a review workflow, not an instruction to energize unvalidated hardware.

Parameter-sensitivity notebook

Construct two more operating points around the worked example by reducing and increasing the selected key parameter by 10%. Repeat the arithmetic and compare the percent response change to the percent parameter change. If the response is nonlinear, explain why its sensitivity grows or shrinks. Examples include the inverse factor 1/(1−D) at high duty, the quadratic I²R dependence of channel heat, and the inverse dependence of ripple on L, C or switching frequency.

For the same change, decide whether the numerical model has moved closer to a known mode boundary, a component maximum rating, a closed-loop stability restriction or a measurement limitation. A derivative taken inside a valid smooth model is useful; it is not sufficient to predict behavior across a discontinuous-conduction boundary or a fault-protection trip. Identify which conclusion is local and which remains valid over the full stated range.

§8.3 Switching-loop parasitics and voltage spikes

Lesson 8.2 — Switching-loop parasitics and voltage spikes

Unit: Magnetics, layout, EMC and safe validation. Focus: energy balance, switching-state modeling, numerical prediction, design limitations, verification. What the model is trying to explain

Fast current changes through trace and package inductance induce voltage v=L_parasitic di/dt. Placing ceramic bypass capacitance close to a half bridge can reduce the high-frequency commutation-loop area and associated ringing. Snubbers can damp energy but introduce loss and component stress.

Competing design alternatives

Compare two configurations with a fixed source, load and service requirement. One should prioritize lower conduction loss or larger energy storage, and the other should prioritize faster switching, smaller size or more economical control. Build a scorecard with heat, component stress, signal noise, cost and verification effort. Give each criterion a unit or a precise definition rather than assigning arbitrary star ratings.

A defensible conclusion should change when the service envelope changes. For example, a low-resistance transistor can be attractive under heavy current while a lower-gate-charge device can be preferable at light load and high frequency. A large bulk capacitor can improve hold-up but worsen startup inrush. Demonstrate at least one such reversal without changing unrelated assumptions at the same time. The objective is to learn why there is rarely one universally best component.

§8.4 Ripple and efficiency measurement uncertainty

Lesson 8.3 — Ripple and efficiency measurement uncertainty

Unit: Magnetics, layout, EMC and safe validation. Focus: energy balance, switching-state modeling, numerical prediction, design limitations, verification. Prerequisites: basic circuit analysis, the definition of average and RMS values, and the relevant preceding lessons. Safety: This is mathematical and simulated content; it does not license direct connection to mains or high-energy battery systems.

What the model is trying to explain

High-frequency switching creates narrow pulses that require appropriate probe voltage ratings, bandwidth, grounding and time alignment. Efficiency obtained from two power instruments is sensitive to offset and calibration, especially when input and output powers differ by only a few watts.

Review at a nonnominal condition

Assume the environment becomes hotter, the supply reaches its minimum allowed voltage and the output experiences a short overload. Which number in the worked example is likely to become obsolete first? Rewrite the corresponding balance or stress relation with a conservative placeholder for the uncertain quantity. If no trusted bound is available, leave the conclusion unresolved. An engineering report should explicitly state what has not been established.

Laboratory thinking and falsifiable observations

Investigative question: What is the smallest set of independent checks that could falsify an overoptimistic efficiency and temperature claim?

Develop a small simulated experiment with two deliberately different operating conditions and one held-constant reference. The output of the experiment should be a table with physical units, a plotted response and an explicit criterion that would disprove the initial interpretation. Include a counterexample that changes the topology or its conduction mode. If your model predicts an impossible output at a duty limit, do not hide that point; explain which neglected physical mechanism becomes dominant and replace the formula with an appropriate higher-fidelity model before continuing.

To compare simulation with future measurements, define where voltage is measured, how current direction is assigned, the required bandwidth, and the averaging interval. If the result is efficiency, synchronize input and output data. If the result is a switching peak, use appropriately rated equipment and identify probe-induced artifacts. If the result is a protection threshold, include comparator tolerance and intervention latency. This is a review workflow, not an instruction to energize unvalidated hardware.

Unit 8 design study — Low-energy engineering release checklist

Objective. Show that the reader can connect switching equations to a bounded engineering requirement, rather than only repeat a nominal calculation.

Scenario and data

Review a proposed 12 V to 5 V, 0.5 A converter as a simulated design. Engineering notebook procedure

1. Requirements table. Write columns for demanded function, allowed input range, load range, maximum stress, accuracy tolerance, missing information and evidence. Reject adjectives without an associated test. Where values have not been provided, label them 'to be determined' and explain the measurement or datasheet that will resolve them.

2. Energy and switching state diagram. Give the current path in each interval and show inductor voltage or capacitor current with consistent sign conventions. State which approximation lets the calculation use cycle averages. If conduction mode can change, identify the condition at which the derivation stops being applicable.

3. Hand calculation. Carry through at least one numerical case from this unit's lessons, report intermediate values with units, check a limiting case, and recompute it with a 20% parameter variation. Separate assumed input values from computed results and ensure no conclusion depends on a hidden choice of waveform convention.

4. Comparison with a different model. Run the companion offline simulation at three operating points; record how its assumptions differ from the hand calculation. The model must not be described as validated physical hardware. If the simulation refuses an impossible operating point, that refusal is a finding rather than a reason to bypass the guardrail.

5. Failure analysis. Identify at least two independent failures: one electrical stress or thermal failure and one sensing, timing, protection or measurement failure. For each propose an observable symptom and a method that could falsify the simplified design claim.

6. Review and decision. Assemble a one-page design summary with chosen assumptions, calculated quantities, sensitivity, unresolved safety risks, measurement uncertainty and a justified next test. Do not specify live-mains experiments or unsupervised high-energy hardware as part of this project.

Evaluation rubric (20 points)

  • Four points: correct switching-state or control-volume representation with reference directions.
  • Four points: independently checkable arithmetic, correct SI units and a valid domain of application.
  • Four points: design tradeoff and a nonnominal sensitivity study.
  • Four points: an explicit falsification or failure test and appropriate safety bounds.
  • Four points: clear interpretation of simulation limitations and remaining uncertainty.

Instructor checkpoint

A submission that uses the right numerical formula with the wrong conduction regime cannot receive full credit. Similarly, a convincing waveform plot does not compensate for missing component limits. The strongest submissions explain what would change their conclusion. Ask learners to present a one-minute defense of the least reliable assumption and to identify the next measurement or calculation that is most likely to overturn the result.

Optional deeper investigation

Vary temperature, parasitic resistance or sensor delay separately. Compare the relative importance of each effect at light load versus rated load. Use an uncertainty table rather than drawing more decimal places. Explain if the most important uncertainty is irreducible without physical measurement, or whether a manufacturer specification supplies a conservative bound. Finally, distinguish the verified result—perhaps an algebraic equality or a checked simulator calculation—from an unverified implication about real hardware.
