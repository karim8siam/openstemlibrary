§4.1 Foundational Principles & Curriculum Scope: Buck Converters: Inductor Volt-Second Balance, Ripple, and CCM/DCM Operation

Unit 4 — AC–DC rectification and line-side quality

The engineering argument

Rectification converts alternating voltage into a unidirectional waveform, often followed by energy storage or active shaping. It must be analyzed with line-frequency RMS values and with the highly non-sinusoidal currents that many capacitor-input rectifiers draw. Power factor is not simply the cosine of an angle for distorted current.

A modeled isolated transformer secondary provides 24 V RMS at 50 Hz. We compare ideal sinusoidal rectification, bridge diode drops, capacitor hold-up and controlled delay while avoiding any instructions to handle mains directly.

An output smoothing capacitor changes both ripple voltage and the input current waveform. Small ripple does not imply high input power factor. Real utility interfaces require isolation, grounding, surge and compliance engineering.

Line-frequency waveform modeling. For AC–DC stages the word DC typically means a unidirectional mean, not zero ripple. The average of an absolute sine wave, its RMS value, its peak and the capacitor-held level are different quantities. State which quantity the load needs before applying a formula.

A reservoir capacitor reduces the time for which rectifier diodes conduct. The narrow charging pulses may contain high RMS and peak current even when the DC load is relatively modest. This is a clear case where an improved-looking voltage trace hides degraded input stress.

In a controlled rectifier, the gate pulse is only a timing trigger in many device configurations; it does not alone command arbitrary forced turn-off. Device commutation and load current continuity determine which firing-angle expression can be applied.

When investigating input power quality, store synchronized line voltage and current records. Determine real power through instantaneous multiplication and averaging, RMS through square averaging, and power factor through their ratio. A plotted phase angle cannot replace those operations for distorted current.

Sequence and learning objectives

In the first lesson, identify the model, boundary and relevant physical state. In the second, compute the dominant currents, voltages or losses under a specified operating point. In the third, investigate where the ideal result ceases to hold. In the fourth, convert the model into a design review with independently falsifiable tests. This sequence is intended to prevent equation memorization without comprehension. The central question is not whether a formula can be remembered but whether the learner can recognize when it should not be used.

§4.2 Single-phase bridge rectifier waveforms

Lesson 4.1 — Single-phase bridge rectifier waveforms

Unit: AC–DC rectification and line-side quality. Focus: energy balance, switching-state modeling, numerical prediction, design limitations, verification. Prerequisites: basic circuit analysis, the definition of average and RMS values, and the relevant preceding lessons. Safety: This is mathematical and simulated content; it does not license direct connection to mains or high-energy battery systems.

What the model is trying to explain

A full bridge selects two diodes on each half-cycle so that the load sees the magnitude of the sinusoidal input minus two conducting diode drops. In an ideal resistive-load model the average rectified voltage is 2 V_peak/π; load current follows the absolute sine waveform.

The starting point is an electrical system with a stated input, a desired output, and at least one physical storage or loss mechanism. Choose voltage and current arrows before writing any balance equation. This apparently clerical step determines whether a negative term represents energy returned to a source, dissipated heat, or a mismatch of sign conventions. For a switching system the state of the controlled semiconductor changes the governing differential equation; a mean-value formula follows only after the switching states and the relevant steady-state conditions have been established. A time-domain numerical result should therefore always be accompanied by the state assumptions under which it was computed.

Complete numerical walkthrough

An isolated 24 V RMS secondary has V_peak≈33.94 V. The ideal bridge average is about 21.61 V with no storage capacitor. With two assumed 0.7 V conducting drops, a rough average is 20.21 V only if the diodes conduct through the relevant portions; near the zero crossings the constant-drop approximation breaks down.

How to verify the arithmetic independently. First convert every milliohm, microsecond, microfarad, kilohertz and microhenry into consistent base units. Next substitute the stated values into the governing relation in one line before rounding. Keep a separate column for the measured or assumed quantity and the calculated quantity. After calculation, check the physical limiting case: at zero load there should be no load power, at zero switching interval there should not be energy transfer through an ideal PWM command, and the sign of a voltage cannot be changed by efficiency alone. These simple limiting checks often expose a wrong topology or an invalid formula more reliably than another decimal place.

For a useful second calculation, change one design variable while holding the others fixed. If a capacitor is changed, do not simultaneously alter switching frequency and load current, because the effect becomes difficult to attribute. Record which quantities are genuinely controlled and which are consequences of the change. Where the model is an approximation, report its domain of validity beside the number; a precise numerical answer derived outside the model's domain can be more misleading than a rough answer with honest uncertainty.

Why a technically plausible answer may be wrong

A peak measurement of 33.94 V is not the average DC output. A rectified sinusoid has substantial ripple and must not be described as regulated 24 V DC.

In particular, distinguish a device rating, a nominal operating point, a predicted steady-state waveform and a measured transient peak. The four are not interchangeable. An ideal average voltage says little about a nanosecond-level overshoot; a calculated RMS current does not establish that a junction temperature is acceptable; a controller response without saturation says nothing about a hard overcurrent event. The reader should identify the first assumption likely to fail at an extreme input, a very light load, a startup event, or a component temperature different from the nominal value.

Engineering decision and defensible tradeoff

Compute average, RMS, diode conduction interval and ripple frequency. Explain why adding a bulk capacitor changes the appropriate formula for load voltage.

State the requirement as a measurable inequality or a bounded interval instead of using only adjectives such as fast, small, efficient or robust. For example, a requirement for output voltage has to name the input-voltage range, load range, transient duration and maximum allowed deviation. A requirement for low loss has to identify the ambient-temperature condition and whether the gate driver or controller is included in the loss accounting. A comparison among device options is not reproducible unless all alternatives are evaluated under the same service conditions.

Also note what is sacrificed when the selected variable is improved. Raising frequency can decrease filter size but increase switching heat; lowering channel resistance may increase gate-charge cost; larger capacitance can improve low-frequency hold-up while increasing inrush; stronger voltage-loop action can improve a small load step but erode phase margin at a different point. Engineering is the management of coupled constraints rather than the maximization of one attractive metric.

Laboratory thinking and falsifiable observations

Investigative question: How does the output ripple frequency compare with 50 Hz line frequency?

Develop a small simulated experiment with two deliberately different operating conditions and one held-constant reference. The output of the experiment should be a table with physical units, a plotted response and an explicit criterion that would disprove the initial interpretation. Include a counterexample that changes the topology or its conduction mode. If your model predicts an impossible output at a duty limit, do not hide that point; explain which neglected physical mechanism becomes dominant and replace the formula with an appropriate higher-fidelity model before continuing.

To compare simulation with future measurements, define where voltage is measured, how current direction is assigned, the required bandwidth, and the averaging interval. If the result is efficiency, synchronize input and output data. If the result is a switching peak, use appropriately rated equipment and identify probe-induced artifacts. If the result is a protection threshold, include comparator tolerance and intervention latency. This is a review workflow, not an instruction to energize unvalidated hardware.

Repeat the arithmetic and compare the percent response change to the percent parameter change. If the response is nonlinear, explain why its sensitivity grows or shrinks. §4.3 Reservoir capacitors and inrush

Lesson 4.2 — Reservoir capacitors and inrush

What the model is trying to explain

Competing design alternatives

Compare two configurations with a fixed source, load and service requirement. One should prioritize lower conduction loss or larger energy storage, and the other should prioritize faster switching, smaller size or more economical control. Build a scorecard with heat, component stress, signal noise, cost and verification effort. Give each criterion a unit or a precise definition rather than assigning arbitrary star ratings.

A large bulk capacitor can improve hold-up but worsen startup inrush. Demonstrate at least one such reversal without changing unrelated assumptions at the same time. The objective is to learn why there is rarely one universally best component.

§4.4 Controlled rectifiers and firing delay

Lesson 4.3 — Controlled rectifiers and firing delay

Unit: AC–DC rectification and line-side quality. Focus: energy balance, switching-state modeling, numerical prediction, design limitations, verification. What the model is trying to explain

A phase-controlled rectifier delays a controlled device turn-on relative to the AC waveform. In a simplified fully controlled single-phase bridge with continuous DC load current, average output is V_dc=(2V_peak/π)cos α. §4.5 Power factor, distortion and input harmonics

Lesson 4.4 — Power factor, distortion and input harmonics

Unit: AC–DC rectification and line-side quality. Focus: energy balance, switching-state modeling, numerical prediction, design limitations, verification. Prerequisites: basic circuit analysis, the definition of average and RMS values, and the relevant preceding lessons. Safety: This is mathematical and simulated content; it does not license direct connection to mains or high-energy battery systems.

What the model is trying to explain

For sinusoidal voltage and arbitrary periodic current, true power factor is P/(V_rms I_rms). It combines displacement and distortion effects; a high-frequency switching front end may actively shape line current to reduce harmonics and better use the supply. The simple cosine of voltage-current phase shift is insufficient with a distorted current.

At 24 V RMS, 2 A RMS, and measured real power 36 W, PF=36/(24×2)=0.75. If the fundamental current component has unity displacement factor, the remaining reduction can still come from waveform distortion.

Also note what is sacrificed when the selected variable is improved. Raising frequency can decrease filter size but increase switching heat; lowering channel resistance may increase gate-charge cost; larger capacitance can improve low-frequency hold-up while increasing inrush; stronger voltage-loop action can improve a small load step but erode phase margin at a different point. Engineering is the management of coupled constraints rather than the maximization of one attractive metric.

Laboratory thinking and falsifiable observations

Investigative question: If two rectifiers draw the same average power, how can one require a much higher input RMS current?

The output of the experiment should be a table with physical units, a plotted response and an explicit criterion that would disprove the initial interpretation. Include a counterexample that changes the topology or its conduction mode. If your model predicts an impossible output at a duty limit, do not hide that point; explain which neglected physical mechanism becomes dominant and replace the formula with an appropriate higher-fidelity model before continuing.

To compare simulation with future measurements, define where voltage is measured, how current direction is assigned, the required bandwidth, and the averaging interval. If the result is a protection threshold, include comparator tolerance and intervention latency. This is a review workflow, not an instruction to energize unvalidated hardware.

1. Requirements table. Write columns for demanded function, allowed input range, load range, maximum stress, accuracy tolerance, missing information and evidence. 3. Hand calculation. Carry through at least one numerical case from this unit's lessons, report intermediate values with units, check a limiting case, and recompute it with a 20% parameter variation. 4. Comparison with a different model. Run the companion offline simulation at three operating points; record how its assumptions differ from the hand calculation. The model must not be described as validated physical hardware. If the simulation refuses an impossible operating point, that refusal is a finding rather than a reason to bypass the guardrail.

5. Failure analysis. Identify at least two independent failures: one electrical stress or thermal failure and one sensing, timing, protection or measurement failure. For each propose an observable symptom and a method that could falsify the simplified design claim.

  • Four points: an explicit falsification or failure test and appropriate safety bounds.
  • Four points: clear interpretation of simulation limitations and remaining uncertainty.
