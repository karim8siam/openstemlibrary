§3.1 Foundational Principles & Curriculum Scope: Switching Overvoltages: Line Energization, Current Chopping, and Ferroresonance

Unit 3: Switching transients and breaker duties

Engineering overview

Circuit switching can excite network capacitance, inductance and trapped charge. At contact separation the transient recovery voltage is not simply the steady system voltage: electromagnetic energy and pole-specific events determine a waveform that may rise rapidly after interruption.

Four separate studies are developed in this unit: Interruption and transient recovery voltage, Current chopping and inductive interruption, Restrikes, trapped charge and phase selection, and Switching study validation and event evidence. Their models have intentionally different input assumptions. They should not be combined simply because their numerical outputs share units. A source voltage, local electric field, risk probability and event count answer different questions and can have different uncertainty structures.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the unit, a reader should be able to state the physical source of the phenomenon; calculate a constrained model result with consistent units; identify its boundary conditions and initial data; conduct a controlled sensitivity test; and construct a short engineering review that explicitly identifies omitted mechanisms. The reader should also be able to separate a documented equipment rating from a quantity invented for an instructional example. A defensible result includes both a number and a statement of its limitations.

Evidence workflow

1. Source: identify what event or operating condition creates the stress or observation. Write down its time reference and appropriate signal representation.

2. Propagation or internal response: trace the signal through the relevant network, insulating material or sensor. Use the model only where its assumptions apply.

3. Measurement: name the quantities that would have to be observed for a real analysis, noting uncertainty, calibration and reference location.

4. Decision: state an actionable conclusion only when the acceptance criterion, equipment definition and hazard boundary are independently documented.

Integrated software case

Study an idealized post-interruption oscillator, then list the omitted transient-recovery-voltage envelope, pole factors, system topology and certified breaker-duty evidence. Start with the default inputs and write a small table of three cases: baseline, one input increased, and a contrasting configuration. Give units for every column. Do not treat a “better-looking” curve as a demonstration that a real dielectric is more reliable; the curve was produced by the stipulated equations. Where the unit involves event probability or a failure model, compute exposure and population definitions before interpreting the percentage.

Reading order

  • Lesson 3.1: Interruption and transient recovery voltage — Do not compare only the asymptote 100 kV to a hardware duty claim when the transient may overshoot significantly.
  • Lesson 3.2: Current chopping and inductive interruption — Reducing inductive energy or providing a controlled path can reduce idealized voltage escalation, but device design is standards-dependent.
  • Lesson 3.3: Restrikes, trapped charge and phase selection — A post-switching waveform recorded without initial trapped-charge conditions cannot be reproduced uniquely.
  • Lesson 3.4: Switching study validation and event evidence — Always perform timestep convergence and check conserved quantities rather than accepting the first simulation output.

Expected outputs

Submit a two-page technical note, the three-case numerical table, your answer to each lesson's three problems, and one diagram identifying missing evidence. The project and the unit assessment are separate; the project tests reasoning about a whole subsystem, while the assessment checks multiple independent concepts. A valid submission can conclude that physical validation is currently unavailable, provided the logical and numerical work is reproducible.

Safety boundary. This is entirely a software and analytical teaching resource. No live high-voltage test or energized hardware construction is included. Lightning, insulation switching and stored electrical energy can cause fatal electric shock or arc events even after isolation. Physical testing requires properly trained personnel, site-specific risk assessments, approved procedures, interlocked laboratories and calibrated equipment; numerical examples provide no permission to undertake those tests.

§3.2 Interruption and transient recovery voltage

Lesson 3.1 — Interruption and transient recovery voltage

Unit: Switching transients and breaker duties Scope: mathematical explanation, traceable worked cases, no energized experiments. Prerequisites: electromagnetism, circuit theory, calculus, introductory probability and numerical reasoning. Status: instructional draft, expert review required.

Question that motivates the calculation

After current interruption, contacts experience a recovering voltage determined by the surrounding network. The waveform may oscillate and its rate of rise can be relevant to interrupting duty. Consider what an engineer would need to know before computing a number. A source condition and an observation location must be named; the disturbance travels through a physical structure, and the inference must distinguish an equivalent network quantity from a local material stress. This lesson focuses on the specific observables relevant to interruption and transient recovery voltage, rather than treating every high-voltage problem as a breakdown-field calculation.

Governing relation and conditions

Working relation: An ideal damped proxy is v(t)=V∞[1−e^(−t/τ)cos(2πf t)]. Initial v(0)=0 and long-time v→V∞.

Worked engineering illustration

For V∞=100 kV, f=5 kHz, τ=0.5 ms, the first half-period t=0.1 ms gives v≈100[1+exp(−0.2)]≈181.9 kV.
