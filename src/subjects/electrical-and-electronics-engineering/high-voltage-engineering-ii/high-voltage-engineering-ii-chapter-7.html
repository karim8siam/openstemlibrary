§7.1 Foundational Principles & Curriculum Scope: Corona Phenomena, Power Loss, Audible Noise, and Radio Interference

Unit 7: Diagnostics, interpretation and uncertainty

Engineering overview

Diagnostic signals are noisy evidence, not unambiguous labels. PD pulses, dielectric loss, thermal imaging, dissolved gases and acoustic events have different selectivity and uncertainty. The rate of false alarms matters particularly when a defect is rare.

Four separate studies are developed in this unit: Diagnostic sensitivity and specificity, Measurement uncertainty and calibration transfer, Competing faults and data fusion, and Trend detection, censoring and action thresholds. Their models have intentionally different input assumptions. They should not be combined simply because their numerical outputs share units. A source voltage, local electric field, risk probability and event count answer different questions and can have different uncertainty structures.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the unit, a reader should be able to state the physical source of the phenomenon; calculate a constrained model result with consistent units; identify its boundary conditions and initial data; conduct a controlled sensitivity test; and construct a short engineering review that explicitly identifies omitted mechanisms. The reader should also be able to separate a documented equipment rating from a quantity invented for an instructional example. A defensible result includes both a number and a statement of its limitations.

Evidence workflow

1. Source: identify what event or operating condition creates the stress or observation. Write down its time reference and appropriate signal representation.

2. Propagation or internal response: trace the signal through the relevant network, insulating material or sensor. Use the model only where its assumptions apply.

3. Measurement: name the quantities that would have to be observed for a real analysis, noting uncertainty, calibration and reference location.

4. Decision: state an actionable conclusion only when the acceptance criterion, equipment definition and hazard boundary are independently documented.

Integrated software case

Use Bayes theorem to calculate the positive predictive value for a hypothetical monitor. Distinguish model-based probability from measured calibration, and ask how the action threshold should account for failure consequence. Start with the default inputs and write a small table of three cases: baseline, one input increased, and a contrasting configuration. Give units for every column. Do not treat a “better-looking” curve as a demonstration that a real dielectric is more reliable; the curve was produced by the stipulated equations. Where the unit involves event probability or a failure model, compute exposure and population definitions before interpreting the percentage.

Reading order

  • Lesson 7.1: Diagnostic sensitivity and specificity — A diagnostic alarm should trigger proportionate verification rather than immediate confidence in a fault location.
  • Lesson 7.2: Measurement uncertainty and calibration transfer — Document sensor settings and calibration traceability before interpreting small changes as material degradation.
  • Lesson 7.3: Competing faults and data fusion — A reviewer needs to know sensor physics, shared noise paths and conditional dependence before treating agreement as validation.
  • Lesson 7.4: Trend detection, censoring and action thresholds — The alarm threshold must be justified by cost of missed events and false actions, not optimized only on historical accuracy.

Expected outputs

Submit a two-page technical note, the three-case numerical table, your answer to each lesson's three problems, and one diagram identifying missing evidence. The project and the unit assessment are separate; the project tests reasoning about a whole subsystem, while the assessment checks multiple independent concepts. A valid submission can conclude that physical validation is currently unavailable, provided the logical and numerical work is reproducible.

Safety boundary. This is entirely a software and analytical teaching resource. No live high-voltage test or energized hardware construction is included. Lightning, insulation switching and stored electrical energy can cause fatal electric shock or arc events even after isolation. Physical testing requires properly trained personnel, site-specific risk assessments, approved procedures, interlocked laboratories and calibrated equipment; numerical examples provide no permission to undertake those tests.

§7.2 Diagnostic sensitivity and specificity

Lesson 7.1 — Diagnostic sensitivity and specificity

Unit: Diagnostics, interpretation and uncertainty Scope: mathematical explanation, traceable worked cases, no energized experiments. Prerequisites: electromagnetism, circuit theory, calculus, introductory probability and numerical reasoning. Status: instructional draft, expert review required.

Question that motivates the calculation

A detector sensitivity describes how often it finds a true fault, and specificity how often it correctly rejects a nonfault. A positive result is less predictive for rare faults than many dashboards imply. Consider what an engineer would need to know before computing a number. A source condition and an observation location must be named; the disturbance travels through a physical structure, and the inference must distinguish an equivalent network quantity from a local material stress. This lesson focuses on the specific observables relevant to diagnostic sensitivity and specificity, rather than treating every high-voltage problem as a breakdown-field calculation.

Governing relation and conditions

Working relation: PPV=Se·p/[Se·p+(1−Sp)(1−p)], where p is a stated defect prevalence.

Worked engineering illustration

For Se=0.90, Sp=0.95 and p=0.02, PPV=0.018/(0.018+0.049)≈26.9%; most positives are false positives under these assumptions.

First robustness check

Compare the same sensor at p=2% and p=20% to see how the base rate changes action value. To turn this into a sensitivity analysis, change one input while keeping all the others fixed. If the model predicts an output proportional to an input, a ten percent increase produces a ten percent change. If the relation is a square law, the same input change can produce a twenty-one percent change. If the relation contains logarithms, exponential decay or a nonlinear power, compute the actual new value; do not apply a linear rule automatically. State the chosen parameter, the updated input, and whether the original physical assumptions still hold.

Second robustness check

Real prevalence, label quality and correlated event windows are often unknown; classroom PPV is not a site-calibrated probability. Identify which omitted effect would most likely overturn the numerical conclusion. Ask whether the apparent physical change could instead be a measurement-location effect, a mode not included in the circuit, a transient initial condition or an unverified population parameter. Rank these omissions by their causal relevance to the particular conclusion; not all unspecified real-world complexities deserve equal emphasis. A non-converged software calculation should be rejected before arguing about possible dielectric ageing.

Competing mechanisms and decision boundary

A diagnostic alarm should trigger proportionate verification rather than immediate confidence in a fault location. Compare the same sensor at p=2% and p=20% to see how the base rate changes action value. Two physical explanations can yield similar single-point voltage or event counts, so a useful experiment must predict different observations. For a transient network, compare arrival time, reflection polarity or waveform shape at two locations. For insulation, compare temperature, condition history or discharge signatures with controlled confounders. For asset probabilities, compare rates normalized to the same exposure rather than absolute totals. Always identify who is authorized to collect actual high-voltage measurements; this document does not authorize them.

Independent random standard uncertainties may be combined quadratically, but correlated sources cannot. Consider what an engineer would need to know before computing a number. Unit: Diagnostics, interpretation and uncertainty Scope: mathematical explanation, traceable worked cases, no energized experiments. Prerequisites: electromagnetism, circuit theory, calculus, introductory probability and numerical reasoning. Status: instructional draft, expert review required.

Question that motivates the calculation

Partial-discharge pulse trains, temperature anomalies and switching artifacts can overlap. Evidence may be dependent; naively multiplying correlated likelihoods exaggerates certainty. Consider what an engineer would need to know before computing a number. A source condition and an observation location must be named; the disturbance travels through a physical structure, and the inference must distinguish an equivalent network quantity from a local material stress. This lesson focuses on the specific observables relevant to competing faults and data fusion, rather than treating every high-voltage problem as a breakdown-field calculation.

Governing relation and conditions

Working relation: If evidence tests A,B are conditionally independent given H, posterior odds equal prior odds times LR_A×LR_B. Independence must be checked.

Worked engineering illustration

If prior odds are 1:19 and two independent signals each have LR=4, posterior odds become 16:19, or probability ≈45.7%.
