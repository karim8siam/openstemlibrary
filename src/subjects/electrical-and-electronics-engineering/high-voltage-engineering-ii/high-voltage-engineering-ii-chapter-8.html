§8.1 Foundational Principles & Curriculum Scope: Non-Destructive Insulation Testing: Partial Discharge Detection and Dielectric Tan-Delta

Unit 8: Reliability and engineering decisions

Engineering overview

Asset management integrates stress, condition, failure consequence, uncertainty and maintenance logistics. A low calculated event probability is not a guarantee and a high alarm score is not automatically a reason for immediate replacement. System engineers justify decisions through defined evidence and responsibility.

Four separate studies are developed in this unit: Failure hazard and survival models, Failure consequence and expected loss, Maintenance availability and outages, and Decision records and change control. Their models have intentionally different input assumptions. They should not be combined simply because their numerical outputs share units. A source voltage, local electric field, risk probability and event count answer different questions and can have different uncertainty structures.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the unit, a reader should be able to state the physical source of the phenomenon; calculate a constrained model result with consistent units; identify its boundary conditions and initial data; conduct a controlled sensitivity test; and construct a short engineering review that explicitly identifies omitted mechanisms. The reader should also be able to separate a documented equipment rating from a quantity invented for an instructional example. A defensible result includes both a number and a statement of its limitations.

Evidence workflow

1. Source: identify what event or operating condition creates the stress or observation. Write down its time reference and appropriate signal representation.

2. Propagation or internal response: trace the signal through the relevant network, insulating material or sensor. Use the model only where its assumptions apply.

3. Measurement: name the quantities that would have to be observed for a real analysis, noting uncertainty, calibration and reference location.

4. Decision: state an actionable conclusion only when the acceptance criterion, equipment definition and hazard boundary are independently documented.

Integrated software case

Build a basic time-to-event model and a decision register, explicitly including detection delays, external dependencies and qualified review before operational change. Start with the default inputs and write a small table of three cases: baseline, one input increased, and a contrasting configuration. Give units for every column. Do not treat a “better-looking” curve as a demonstration that a real dielectric is more reliable; the curve was produced by the stipulated equations. Where the unit involves event probability or a failure model, compute exposure and population definitions before interpreting the percentage.

Reading order

  • Lesson 8.1: Failure hazard and survival models — Use survival analysis as one scenario, not as a deterministic lifetime or warranty.
  • Lesson 8.2: Failure consequence and expected loss — A repair investment should not be sold on a hypothetical avoided-loss claim without probabilities grounded in data.
  • Lesson 8.3: Maintenance availability and outages — Reducing repair delay may be as effective as lowering failure incidence for some non-safety-critical equipment.
  • Lesson 8.4: Decision records and change control — The correct output of incomplete evidence may be a hold point and commissioning review, rather than a fabricated pass certificate.

Expected outputs

Submit a two-page technical note, the three-case numerical table, your answer to each lesson's three problems, and one diagram identifying missing evidence. The project and the unit assessment are separate; the project tests reasoning about a whole subsystem, while the assessment checks multiple independent concepts. A valid submission can conclude that physical validation is currently unavailable, provided the logical and numerical work is reproducible.

Safety boundary. This is entirely a software and analytical teaching resource. No live high-voltage test or energized hardware construction is included. Lightning, insulation switching and stored electrical energy can cause fatal electric shock or arc events even after isolation. Physical testing requires properly trained personnel, site-specific risk assessments, approved procedures, interlocked laboratories and calibrated equipment; numerical examples provide no permission to undertake those tests.

§8.2 Failure hazard and survival models

Lesson 8.1 — Failure hazard and survival models

Unit: Reliability and engineering decisions Scope: mathematical explanation, traceable worked cases, no energized experiments. Prerequisites: electromagnetism, circuit theory, calculus, introductory probability and numerical reasoning. Status: instructional draft, expert review required.

Question that motivates the calculation

Failure probability over an exposure interval is a time-dependent statement, not a calendar guarantee. Exponential survival is a useful benchmark but cannot capture wear-out, repair state and event clustering. Consider what an engineer would need to know before computing a number. A source condition and an observation location must be named; the disturbance travels through a physical structure, and the inference must distinguish an equivalent network quantity from a local material stress. This lesson focuses on the specific observables relevant to failure hazard and survival models, rather than treating every high-voltage problem as a breakdown-field calculation.

Governing relation and conditions

Working relation: For constant hazard λ, survival is S(t)=exp(−λt) and failure probability F(t)=1−S(t).

Worked engineering illustration

For λ=0.02/year and t=5 years, F≈1−e^(−0.1)≈9.52%; a five-year event is not certain even though expected risk is nonzero.

First robustness check

Compare equal instantaneous hazards with increasing-hazard Weibull deterioration over a ten-year horizon. To turn this into a sensitivity analysis, change one input while keeping all the others fixed. If the model predicts an output proportional to an input, a ten percent increase produces a ten percent change. If the relation is a square law, the same input change can produce a twenty-one percent change. If the relation contains logarithms, exponential decay or a nonlinear power, compute the actual new value; do not apply a linear rule automatically. State the chosen parameter, the updated input, and whether the original physical assumptions still hold.

Second robustness check

Ageing may raise hazard with time, and a fitted hazard from few failures has large statistical uncertainty. Identify which omitted effect would most likely overturn the numerical conclusion. Ask whether the apparent physical change could instead be a measurement-location effect, a mode not included in the circuit, a transient initial condition or an unverified population parameter. Rank these omissions by their causal relevance to the particular conclusion; not all unspecified real-world complexities deserve equal emphasis. A non-converged software calculation should be rejected before arguing about possible dielectric ageing.

Competing mechanisms and decision boundary

Use survival analysis as one scenario, not as a deterministic lifetime or warranty. Compare equal instantaneous hazards with increasing-hazard Weibull deterioration over a ten-year horizon. Two physical explanations can yield similar single-point voltage or event counts, so a useful experiment must predict different observations. For a transient network, compare arrival time, reflection polarity or waveform shape at two locations. For insulation, compare temperature, condition history or discharge signatures with controlled confounders. For asset probabilities, compare rates normalized to the same exposure rather than absolute totals. Always identify who is authorized to collect actual high-voltage measurements; this document does not authorize them.

Design brief

A hypothetical network operator requests a desk-based technical comparison of two options affecting reliability and engineering decisions. Both options are abstractions; no physical components, voltages or laboratory procedures are supplied. You may use the teaching equations and the interactive model, but you must not turn synthetic assumptions into claimed equipment specifications.

Required deliverables

A. Analytical framework. Draft a block diagram connecting the initiating phenomenon, medium or circuit, local stress, sensor evidence and possible decision.
