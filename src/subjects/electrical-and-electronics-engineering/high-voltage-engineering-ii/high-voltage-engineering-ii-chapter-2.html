§2.1 Foundational Principles & Curriculum Scope: Lightning Phenomenon: Shielding Failure, Back-Flashover, and Overhead Protection

Unit 2: Lightning performance of overhead lines

Engineering overview

A lightning flash affects overhead equipment through multiple paths: direct conductor strikes, shield-wire interceptions, induced surges, tower potential rise and backflashover. Lightning occurrence, strike attachment and insulation performance are stochastic. A power-frequency short-circuit equation is not a lightning attachment model.

Four separate studies are developed in this unit: Cloud-to-ground stroke and attachment uncertainty, Tower potential rise and footing behavior, Shielding failure versus backflashover, and Outage rate interpretation and exposure. Their models have intentionally different input assumptions. They should not be combined simply because their numerical outputs share units. A source voltage, local electric field, risk probability and event count answer different questions and can have different uncertainty structures.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the unit, a reader should be able to state the physical source of the phenomenon; calculate a constrained model result with consistent units; identify its boundary conditions and initial data; conduct a controlled sensitivity test; and construct a short engineering review that explicitly identifies omitted mechanisms. The reader should also be able to separate a documented equipment rating from a quantity invented for an instructional example. A defensible result includes both a number and a statement of its limitations.

Evidence workflow

1. Source: identify what event or operating condition creates the stress or observation. Write down its time reference and appropriate signal representation.

2. Propagation or internal response: trace the signal through the relevant network, insulating material or sensor. Use the model only where its assumptions apply.

3. Measurement: name the quantities that would have to be observed for a real analysis, noting uncertainty, calibration and reference location.

4. Decision: state an actionable conclusion only when the acceptance criterion, equipment definition and hazard boundary are independently documented.

Integrated software case

Perform a sensitivity study of assumed lightning current and tower impedance. State that the voltage product is a diagnostic idealization and cannot yield a field outage rate without stroke statistics, geometry, grounding and insulation withstand data. Start with the default inputs and write a small table of three cases: baseline, one input increased, and a contrasting configuration. Give units for every column. Do not treat a “better-looking” curve as a demonstration that a real dielectric is more reliable; the curve was produced by the stipulated equations. Where the unit involves event probability or a failure model, compute exposure and population definitions before interpreting the percentage.

Reading order

  • Lesson 2.1: Cloud-to-ground stroke and attachment uncertainty — A reliability prediction must separate how often lightning occurs from how it couples to the insulation system.
  • Lesson 2.2: Tower potential rise and footing behavior — Do not use this voltage product as an acceptance criterion for line-string withstand or an energized grounding arrangement.
  • Lesson 2.3: Shielding failure versus backflashover — Line modifications that reduce one failure mode can leave the other unchanged, so mitigation must match the failure mechanism.
  • Lesson 2.4: Outage rate interpretation and exposure — A value of 0.20/year describes expected incidence, not a five-year guarantee or a uniform calendar schedule.

Expected outputs

Submit a two-page technical note, the three-case numerical table, your answer to each lesson's three problems, and one diagram identifying missing evidence. The project and the unit assessment are separate; the project tests reasoning about a whole subsystem, while the assessment checks multiple independent concepts. A valid submission can conclude that physical validation is currently unavailable, provided the logical and numerical work is reproducible.

Safety boundary. This is entirely a software and analytical teaching resource. No live high-voltage test or energized hardware construction is included. Lightning, insulation switching and stored electrical energy can cause fatal electric shock or arc events even after isolation. Physical testing requires properly trained personnel, site-specific risk assessments, approved procedures, interlocked laboratories and calibrated equipment; numerical examples provide no permission to undertake those tests.

§2.2 Cloud-to-ground stroke and attachment uncertainty

Lesson 2.1 — Cloud-to-ground stroke and attachment uncertainty

Unit: Lightning performance of overhead lines Scope: mathematical explanation, traceable worked cases, no energized experiments. Prerequisites: electromagnetism, circuit theory, calculus, introductory probability and numerical reasoning. Status: instructional draft, expert review required.

Question that motivates the calculation

Lightning performance prediction begins with assumptions about local ground flash density, peak-current distribution, shielding geometry and attachment. No single median stroke represents an entire site exposure. Consider what an engineer would need to know before computing a number. A source condition and an observation location must be named; the disturbance travels through a physical structure, and the inference must distinguish an equivalent network quantity from a local material stress. This lesson focuses on the specific observables relevant to cloud-to-ground stroke and attachment uncertainty, rather than treating every high-voltage problem as a breakdown-field calculation.

Governing relation and conditions

Working relation: An illustrative exceedance model may be P(I>i)=exp(−i/I₀), but actual stroke-current distributions require empirical source data and location-specific calibration.

Worked engineering illustration

If an artificial scale I₀=25 kA is assumed, the model gives P(I>50 kA)=e^(−2)≈0.135. It is a teaching distribution, not an estimated site probability.

To turn this into a sensitivity analysis, change one input while keeping all the others fixed. If the model predicts an output proportional to an input, a ten percent increase produces a ten percent change. State the chosen parameter, the updated input, and whether the original physical assumptions still hold.

Second robustness check

The attachment path is dependent on conductor height and geometry; the toy exponential omits physical incidence and shielding. §2.6 Engineering Design Project & Numerical Investigation: Lightning Phenomenon: Shielding Failure, Back-Flashover, and Overhead Protection

Design brief

A hypothetical network operator requests a desk-based technical comparison of two options affecting lightning performance of overhead lines. Both options are abstractions; no physical components, voltages or laboratory procedures are supplied. You may use the teaching equations and the interactive model, but you must not turn synthetic assumptions into claimed equipment specifications.

A lightning flash affects overhead equipment through multiple paths: direct conductor strikes, shield-wire interceptions, induced surges, tower potential rise and backflashover. Lightning occurrence, strike attachment and insulation performance are stochastic. A power-frequency short-circuit equation is not a lightning attachment model.

Required deliverables

A. Analytical framework. Draft a block diagram connecting the initiating phenomenon, medium or circuit, local stress, sensor evidence and possible decision. Include at least two feedback or hidden variables. For the chosen operating condition, describe why cloud-to-ground stroke and attachment uncertainty and shielding failure versus backflashover may have competing impacts. Demonstrate with one of the unit's equations, with dimensional units shown.

B. Three-case calculation. Perform a sensitivity study of assumed lightning current and tower impedance. State that the voltage product is a diagnostic idealization and cannot yield a field outage rate without stroke statistics, geometry, grounding and insulation withstand data. Make the baseline and both variations reproducible. Label input values as invented, externally sourced, or assumptions. Where the change is nonlinear, explain the physical intuition and derive the relevant numerical scaling rather than claiming every input behaves linearly.

C. Sensitivity and model breakdown. Consider specifically: Soil ionization, inductive paths, multi-tower coupling and flashover dynamic response are excluded. and Correlated storms, repair policy, repeated strikes and underreported events weaken the stationary/independent assumption. These are different failure mechanisms; decide which one would most affect the proposed comparative result. Record at least one missing distribution, geometry, time profile, material constant or calibration measurement.

D. Verification plan. Propose a staged desk review: compare limit cases, check units, reproduce one point independently, vary numerical timestep if appropriate, compare an independent data source, and request qualified engineering review before any real installation recommendation. No energized experiment or switchgear operation is permitted as part of this project.

E. Decision memorandum. State a conservative conditional conclusion: what the toy model predicts, what remains unknown and which documented evidence could change the decision. Include at least two alternatives and explain why choosing the most attractive plot alone is insufficient.

Rubric

Model correctness 25%, units and reproduction 20%, sensitivity reasoning 20%, evidence and uncertainty 20%, decision clarity 15%. A polished figure without a correct scope declaration earns no credit for validation.

Safety boundary. This is entirely a software and analytical teaching resource. No live high-voltage test or energized hardware construction is included. Physical testing requires properly trained personnel, site-specific risk assessments, approved procedures, interlocked laboratories and calibrated equipment; numerical examples provide no permission to undertake those tests.
