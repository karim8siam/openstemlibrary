§4.1 Foundational Principles & Curriculum Scope: Breakdown in Solid Dielectrics: Intrinsic, Electromechanical, Thermal, and Treeing

Unit 4: Transient overvoltages and impulse waveforms

Why this subject matters

Lightning and switching events produce fast and slow overvoltages for different physical reasons. The amplitude, front, tail and measurement chain matter; a peak reading alone discards information needed to compare a simulated transient with a test waveform.

Common reference case and assumptions

Use a normalized double-exponential impulse shape as an illustrative waveform, without pretending its coefficients automatically satisfy IEC-defined wave-shape parameters. A separate time-domain sampling model makes quantization and trigger uncertainty visible.

Outcomes and verification

Distinguish impulse energy and peak voltage, inspect wave-shape sensitivity, and correctly state what a waveform model has not verified.

A researcher's reading protocol

Distinguish continuous analytic waveforms from sampled instrument output. State the normalization, time origin, front descriptor, tail descriptor, polarity, uncertainty, and any filtering. Do not silently substitute a double-exponential parameter for a normative impulse parameter. The model is intentionally limited to analytical and offline-computation exercises. A student should identify the quantity being predicted and ask what observational evidence would be needed to validate it. The location of the maximum physical field is especially significant: an average stress may conceal a sharp local concentration at a boundary, edge, void, accessory or transient discontinuity.

When reading Lessons 4.1–4.4, first reconstruct the derivation, then change one variable in the supplied worked example. Carry the physical units consistently and ask whether a plotted curve can be traced back to the same symbol definitions as the equation. Use the [interactive software model](../simulations/model-04.html) after the hand calculation so the plot serves as a diagnostic rather than an opaque answer generator.

What counts as evidence

The unit challenge is avoiding false equivalence between a visually similar synthesized pulse and a verified standard test waveform. Distinguish proof of code execution, numerical agreement with a mathematical model, calibration evidence, dielectric qualification, and safe operation. These are five separate accomplishments; none implies the next automatically. All practice in this book is software-only. Do not build test apparatus, charge capacitors or obtain energized equipment for these exercises.

Suggested learning route

Read all four lessons and reproduce the arithmetic in a notebook. Complete the project with a baseline case and three documented variations. Attempt the unit assessment before consulting its solution notes. Record a clear status for each conclusion: derived, numerically checked, simulated, empirically supported or unresolved. There are no fabricated laboratory observations in this book.

§4.2 Lightning impulses and double-exponential models

Lesson 4.1: Lightning impulses and double-exponential models

Unit: Transient overvoltages and impulse waveforms. Mode: theoretical analysis and offline simulation. Prerequisites: elementary electromagnetism, circuits, differential equations, logarithms and unit conversion. Review status: teaching draft.

Physical mechanism

A fast voltage transient is often described by a rapid front and a slower decay. A double exponential is a convenient mathematical approximation, but its two time constants are not themselves the IEC definitions of front time and time to half-value. Normalization must be done after finding the actual peak.

Governing equations and interpretation

Let v(t)=K[exp(−αt)−exp(−βt)] for β>α>0. The peak time is tp=ln(β/α)/(β−α). Set K so that the calculated maximum has the selected amplitude.

Fully stated worked example

For α=1/70 µs⁻¹ and β=1/0.5 µs⁻¹, tp≈2.49 µs, and the raw peak factor is about 0.958. Peak-time is not synonymous with standard lightning impulse front-time.

Reconstructing the model

Define every boundary condition and symbol before calculating. Derive the supplied relation from a recognizable physical law: Gauss’s law, conservation of charge, electromagnetic-wave continuity, equivalent-circuit impedance or probability bookkeeping as appropriate. If a derivative or spatial gradient appears, write what is being held constant and what geometric symmetry is assumed. The derivation must connect to an experimentally meaningful quantity rather than stop at formal algebra.

Failure modes, model exclusions, and hazard boundary

Actual waveform identification must follow the applicable measurement and testing standard, including filtering, overshoot and uncertainty treatment.

§4.3 Switching overvoltages and timescales

Lesson 4.2: Switching overvoltages and timescales

Unit: Transient overvoltages and impulse waveforms. Mode: theoretical analysis and offline simulation. Prerequisites: elementary electromagnetism, circuits, differential equations, logarithms and unit conversion. Review status: teaching draft.

Physical mechanism

A switching transient depends on network inductance, capacitance, breaker action and residual energy. It usually evolves on a different time scale than an idealized lightning impulse. A single voltage peak cannot reveal the rise time, oscillation or protective-device energy duty.

Governing equations and interpretation

A lossless LC angular frequency is ω0=1/√(LC) and f0=ω0/(2π). The energy exchanges between ½ L i² and ½ C v² when the model is ideal and isolated.

Fully stated worked example

L=10 mH and C=1 µF give f0≈1592 Hz and period 0.628 ms. This is an ideal natural frequency, not a switching-impulse withstand specification.

§4.4 Waveform sampling and instrument bandwidth

Lesson 4.3: Waveform sampling and instrument bandwidth

Unit: Transient overvoltages and impulse waveforms. Mode: theoretical analysis and offline simulation. Prerequisites: elementary electromagnetism, circuits, differential equations, logarithms and unit conversion. Review status: teaching draft.

Physical mechanism

Digitized transient data have finite sampling resolution, anti-alias constraints, trigger jitter and channel bandwidth. Capturing a curve does not prove that its maximum or front time was measured accurately. Uncertainty must be attached to the inferred quantity.

Governing equations and interpretation

Sampling interval Δt=1/fs. For a sinusoidal pole at f, a first-order bandwidth model has |H|=1/√[1+(f/fc)²], demonstrating amplitude attenuation near cutoff fc.

Fully stated worked example

At fs=100 MS/s, Δt=10 ns. With a single-pole 10 MHz bandwidth and a 10 MHz sinusoidal component, the amplitude estimate is attenuated to about 70.7%.

Comparing alternative mechanisms

Write down at least two competing explanations for the same observation. One can be the intended low-order model, and the other can be an omitted physical process such as moisture, a floating conductor, spatial inhomogeneity, instrument loading, dielectric ageing, or waveform bandwidth. Explain what additional observation would discriminate the hypotheses. A falsifiable comparison is more useful than repeating a likely answer.

Failure modes, model exclusions, and hazard boundary

A transient has a wide spectrum; this one-pole model and the Nyquist sampling rule are not sufficient to certify a measurement chain.

§4.5 Transient energy, polarity and insulation response

Lesson 4.4: Transient energy, polarity and insulation response

Unit: Transient overvoltages and impulse waveforms. Mode: theoretical analysis and offline simulation. Prerequisites: elementary electromagnetism, circuits, differential equations, logarithms and unit conversion. Review status: teaching draft.

Physical mechanism

A transient voltage rating must be considered together with polarity, wave shape, repetition and insulation system condition. Stored electrical energy is an upper-bound bookkeeping quantity in a lumped capacitor; the energy delivered into a target depends on the circuit and losses.

Governing equations and interpretation

W_C=½CV². For a resistor receiving a decaying capacitor discharge in a deliberately isolated ideal model, the total released energy equals initial stored energy if no alternative path exists.

Fully stated worked example

A mathematical 50 pF terminal capacitance at 100 kV has energy 0.25 J. This does not include source supply, cable capacitance or other energy sources and must never be treated as a safe touch-energy estimate.

Making a controlled decision

Set a baseline, change only one controlled variable, and recalculate the metric using the stated formula. Report the change numerically, including units. Then ask whether the change also introduces an adverse effect: higher stored energy, field enhancement, leakage current, uncertainty, ageing stress, or loss of a valid approximation. End with an evidence request that a qualified technical reviewer could act on.

Failure modes, model exclusions, and hazard boundary

Hidden source energy and uncertain line capacitance can make a nominally small stored-energy estimate dangerously misleading.
