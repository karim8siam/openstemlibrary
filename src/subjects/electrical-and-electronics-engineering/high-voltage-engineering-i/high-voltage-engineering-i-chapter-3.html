§3.1 Foundational Principles & Curriculum Scope: Breakdown Phenomena in Liquid Dielectrics: Commercial Oils and Suspended Particles

Unit 3: Solid, liquid, and composite insulation

Why this subject matters

Bulk insulation has multiple coupled failure paths. Electrical trees, thermal aging, moisture ingress, void discharge, contamination and interfaces have distinct time scales. A measured permittivity or dielectric-loss tangent cannot by itself predict remaining useful life.

Common reference case and assumptions

Consider a simplified insulating layer where capacitance and loss depend on geometry and excitation frequency. Make explicit when a parallel dielectric model, a series mixture, or a laminated interface model is being used.

Outcomes and verification

Estimate stored energy and dielectric losses, explain field redistribution, and contrast intrinsic breakdown with progressive service degradation.

A researcher's reading protocol

Consider the duty cycle and temperature when comparing materials. Chemical state, morphology, moisture and interfaces are not secondary details, because they can control failure long before a published intrinsic dielectric strength is approached. The model is intentionally limited to analytical and offline-computation exercises. A student should identify the quantity being predicted and ask what observational evidence would be needed to validate it. The location of the maximum physical field is especially significant: an average stress may conceal a sharp local concentration at a boundary, edge, void, accessory or transient discontinuity.

When reading Lessons 3.1–3.4, first reconstruct the derivation, then change one variable in the supplied worked example. Carry the physical units consistently and ask whether a plotted curve can be traced back to the same symbol definitions as the equation. Use the [interactive software model](../simulations/model-03.html) after the hand calculation so the plot serves as a diagnostic rather than an opaque answer generator.

What counts as evidence

The unit challenge is to distinguish a short-duration laboratory number from a decades-long service-life argument without assuming a universal acceleration factor. Distinguish proof of code execution, numerical agreement with a mathematical model, calibration evidence, dielectric qualification, and safe operation. These are five separate accomplishments; none implies the next automatically. All practice in this book is software-only. Do not build test apparatus, charge capacitors or obtain energized equipment for these exercises.

Suggested learning route

Read all four lessons and reproduce the arithmetic in a notebook. Complete the project with a baseline case and three documented variations. Attempt the unit assessment before consulting its solution notes. Record a clear status for each conclusion: derived, numerically checked, simulated, empirically supported or unresolved. There are no fabricated laboratory observations in this book.

§3.2 Permittivity, capacitance and dielectric energy

Lesson 3.1: Permittivity, capacitance and dielectric energy

Unit: Solid, liquid, and composite insulation. Mode: theoretical analysis and offline simulation. Prerequisites: elementary electromagnetism, circuits, differential equations, logarithms and unit conversion. Review status: teaching draft.

Physical mechanism

The dielectric constant affects capacitance and field distribution, while dielectric strength is a different property requiring a separately qualified measurement. Polarization varies with frequency, temperature and material formulation. A low-loss material is not automatically high-breakdown.

Governing equations and interpretation

For flat plates C=ε0 εr A/d and W=½ C V². Under fixed voltage and ideal geometry, doubling εr doubles capacitance and stored energy, not voltage withstand.

Fully stated worked example

With A=0.01 m², d=1 mm and εr=4, C≈354 pF. At a mathematical 2 kV potential, W≈0.708 mJ.

The derivation must connect to an experimentally meaningful quantity rather than stop at formal algebra.

Failure modes, model exclusions, and hazard boundary

Fringing, electrode roughness, space charge and dielectric dispersion can matter in a real specimen.

§3.3 Dielectric loss, tan delta and heat production

Lesson 3.2: Dielectric loss, tan delta and heat production

Unit: Solid, liquid, and composite insulation. Mode: theoretical analysis and offline simulation. Prerequisites: elementary electromagnetism, circuits, differential equations, logarithms and unit conversion. Review status: teaching draft.

Physical mechanism

A lossy dielectric dissipates real power under AC excitation even when its dominant current is capacitive. In a linear low-loss equivalent circuit, the loss tangent quantifies the ratio of in-phase to quadrature current. Heating can change the dielectric properties, leading to a feedback problem.

Governing equations and interpretation

Under sinusoidal voltage at angular frequency ω, P_loss≈ω C Vrms² tanδ for a capacitance C and small loss tangent tanδ.

Fully stated worked example

At 50 Hz, C=2 nF, Vrms=1 kV and tanδ=0.01, P≈6.28 mW. At double frequency with the same assumed properties, the simple model predicts twice that loss.

§3.4 Voids, electric trees and progressive deterioration

Lesson 3.3: Voids, electric trees and progressive deterioration

Unit: Solid, liquid, and composite insulation. Mode: theoretical analysis and offline simulation. Prerequisites: elementary electromagnetism, circuits, differential equations, logarithms and unit conversion. Review status: teaching draft.

Physical mechanism

A gas-filled void within solid insulation changes the local field because the phases have different permittivity. Repeated local discharges can erode the void wall, creating a tree-like deterioration path. The observable terminal pulse depends on measurement geometry and coupling.

Governing equations and interpretation

For a planar series idealization D is equal in both layers and Evoid/Esolid≈εsolid/εvoid. This is a geometry-limited estimate, not a general void field factor for a spherical cavity.

Fully stated worked example

If εr solid=3 and εr void≈1, the ideal normal planar-field ratio is about 3. A 0.5 MV/m solid-field estimate suggests a 1.5 MV/m void field, without predicting PD inception.

Comparing alternative mechanisms

Write down at least two competing explanations for the same observation. One can be the intended low-order model, and the other can be an omitted physical process such as moisture, a floating conductor, spatial inhomogeneity, instrument loading, dielectric ageing, or waveform bandwidth. Explain what additional observation would discriminate the hypotheses. A falsifiable comparison is more useful than repeating a likely answer.

Failure modes, model exclusions, and hazard boundary

Cavity shape, gas pressure, aging products, polarity and discharge repetition govern actual damage; voltage-only ranking is insufficient.

§3.5 Liquids, interfaces and moisture sensitivity

Lesson 3.4: Liquids, interfaces and moisture sensitivity

Unit: Solid, liquid, and composite insulation. Mode: theoretical analysis and offline simulation. Prerequisites: elementary electromagnetism, circuits, differential equations, logarithms and unit conversion. Review status: teaching draft.

Physical mechanism

Insulating liquids combine electrical insulation with cooling, but moisture, particles, dissolved gas and interface condition affect their performance. Liquid test results can be highly sensitive to sample preparation and electrode configuration. A high breakdown reading is not a full service-quality certificate.

Governing equations and interpretation

For a simple well-mixed thermal capacity model, ΔT≈Q/(m cp) for a rapid energy input Q before significant heat exchange; this estimates bulk temperature change, not local bubble formation.

Fully stated worked example

Depositing an illustrative 100 J uniformly into 0.5 kg of liquid with cp=1800 J/(kg·K) raises bulk temperature by only 0.111 K. Local discharge heating can be much more concentrated.

Making a controlled decision

Set a baseline, change only one controlled variable, and recalculate the metric using the stated formula. Report the change numerically, including units. Then ask whether the change also introduces an adverse effect: higher stored energy, field enhancement, leakage current, uncertainty, ageing stress, or loss of a valid approximation. End with an evidence request that a qualified technical reviewer could act on.

Failure modes, model exclusions, and hazard boundary

This bulk-energy calculation omits localized heating, phase change, streaming electrification, oxidation and chemical compatibility.
