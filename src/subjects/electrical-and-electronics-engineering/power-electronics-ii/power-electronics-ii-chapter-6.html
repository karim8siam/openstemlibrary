§6.1 Foundational Principles & Curriculum Scope: Multi-Level Inverter Topologies: Diode-Clamped, Flying-Capacitor, and Cascaded H-Bridge

Unit 6: Active rectifiers and power-factor correction

Engineering situation

High input power factor requires an appropriate input-current waveform, not merely a clean output-voltage display. A diode-capacitor rectifier draws narrow charging pulses; an active power-factor correction stage attempts to shape current approximately with input voltage while regulating a DC energy buffer.

Shared numerical scenario

The shared exercise uses a 230 V RMS sinusoidal mathematical source and a 400 V DC-link reference. These values are simulation-only and must not be replicated on mains. We compute RMS current, displacement effects, low-frequency energy variation and simplified two-loop control constraints.

Intended competence

Learners will calculate real and apparent power, estimate a boost PFC duty trajectory, explain why the current loop and voltage loop need different response characteristics, and assess when interleaving reduces input-current ripple rather than total energy consumption.

Assumptions and model boundary

This unit uses specific equations at specific operating points; a model must not be extended merely because it produces a number outside the stated range. In particular, the switching network may be averaged over a PWM period for a slow state calculation, while magnetic reset, terminal-voltage stress and semiconductor switching energy demand individual switching intervals. Keep the mechanical, magnetic and electrical energy variables separate where appropriate. Every diagram should identify a reference node, current direction and whether the axes show peak, average, or RMS quantities. Where an external network or motor is discussed, the waveform is software-generated for learning and does not authorize connection to an energized system.

Unit reading route

Read Lessons 6.1 through 6.4 in sequence, then use the [unit simulation](../simulations/model-06.html). Complete the unit project before the assessment. Preserve the original scenario as a baseline so parameter changes are attributable; do not change source, loading and control law simultaneously. When citing a numerical result, write the values, units and limit cases, then label it as ideal analytical, simplified dynamic model, computed simulation or actual measurement. No measurement claims are made here.

Technical review checkpoint

Input-voltage sinusoidal shape does not guarantee input-current sinusoidal shape. Real power is a cycle-average product; peak current, RMS current and displacement require different calculations. The energy-buffer condition can impose a twice-line-frequency ripple even when input power factor is ideal.

Plot instantaneous v(t), i(t), their product p(t), and capacitor energy on comparable but distinct axes. Verify numerically that integrating p(t) over a complete line cycle recovers the declared average real power.

A good explanation specifies the assumptions under which a relation is true and names an experiment or simulation capable of detecting when it fails. A numerical waveform can agree with its own programmed equations and still be incapable of predicting a real apparatus. Use the following assessment to distinguish those two forms of evidence.

§6.2 Power factor and nonsinusoidal current

Lesson 6.1: Power factor and nonsinusoidal current

Position: Unit 6, Active rectifiers and power-factor correction. Reading level: undergraduate circuit analysis, switching converters and elementary calculus. Learning claim: distinguish the mathematical approximation from the physical device-level consequences. Status: unreviewed teaching chapter.

Physical picture and mechanism

Displacement factor and true power factor are not interchangeable for distorted current waveforms. With a sinusoidal supply and an arbitrary periodic current, real power depends on the in-phase fundamental current component; RMS current includes harmonics that raise apparent power without providing proportional real power.

The practical boundary of the system matters. Draw where energy enters, where useful energy leaves, what storage changes within the process and where heat or field energy is likely to be lost. Define all voltage polarities before changing reference frames; a reported sign is physically meaningful only relative to a declared node or direction. The natural interval for averaging must also be stated: a switching-period equation, a line-frequency RMS result and a slowly varying thermal estimate describe different aspects of the same apparatus. One cannot simply average all three over an arbitrary interval and call the result a design guarantee.

Governing mathematical relation

Power factor PF=P/(Vrms Irms). When supply voltage is sinusoidal and current has fundamental RMS I1 with angle φ1, PF=(I1/Irms)cosφ1. The I1/Irms term accounts for current distortion; it equals 1 only for a pure sinusoidal current.

Write the baseline relation symbolically first, then test it against a dimensional check and an elementary limiting case. For a variable expressed as a rate, verify what time base is implicit: per switching period, per electrical cycle, per unit mechanical rotation or per second. If the equation assumes continuous conduction, a linear magnetic inductance, an ideal waveform or a balanced three-phase input, say so before substituting numerical values. For a measured value, specify sensor position and measurement bandwidth; for a computed value, identify the exact source equation.

Worked numerical example with interpretation

If Vrms=230 V, Irms=10 A, fundamental current I1=8 A and cosφ1=0.95, P=230×8×0.95=1748 W and apparent power is 2300 VA. PF≈0.76 even though the fundamental displacement factor is 0.95.

A credible numerical answer includes both a displayed number and its physical meaning. If a computed voltage is a peak, it cannot be directly used as an RMS rating. If a frequency is a natural resonance, it cannot be presented as a measured closed-loop bandwidth. If a result is an ideal output voltage, it cannot be equated with the real part's dielectric withstand requirement. Use at least three significant digits in the intermediate steps and round the final display to the precision actually supported by input parameters. A second independent check, such as energy conservation, a complementary voltage, or the expected response as one variable approaches zero, should be written beside the arithmetic.

Derivation workflow

Start from a physical definition and then introduce any simplification. Before calculating, sketch or describe the states that make the claimed equation meaningful. A derived relation is usually an operating-point statement, not a guarantee across startup, overload and faulted conditions. Distinguish independent inputs from outputs: a duty ratio may be commanded by the regulator, but a peak current is determined jointly by source voltage, load, storage and event timing.

Check dimensions at every substitution. In particular, microseconds and nanoseconds differ by a factor of one thousand; degrees and radians require explicit conversion before inserting a frequency; and a quantity in electrical radians per second is not a shaft speed in revolutions per minute. The direction of energy flow should be consistent with the current-arrow convention shown beside the circuit or graph.

Important failure modes and nonideal boundaries

An oscilloscope that reports a small phase angle between voltage and current peaks may conceal substantial harmonic distortion. Never equate cosφ measured at the fundamental with measured true power factor without a current-waveform check.

These limitations are not a minor footnote: they are the conditions under which this chapter's conclusion could reverse. Separate the design intent from the semiconductor's transient stress, and the requested controller action from the physical switching state. A controller display can be numerically smooth while its switching node is overshooting; a motor speed command can be well behaved while the DC bus is absorbing excessive regenerative energy. A waveform generated by a hand-written program is only as complete as the modeled parasitics and fault behavior.

Engineering judgment

Specify harmonic measurement bandwidth and the instrument definition of PF. Compare corrective actions for reactive displacement and diode-bridge harmonic distortion; they are not the same problem.

An engineering comparison should use a common reference case and explicitly report one metric that improves and one that could worsen. For example, increasing switching frequency may reduce some storage ripple yet increase switching heat and radiated emissions; adding capacitance may smooth a voltage while raising surge energy and discharge requirements; increasing control bandwidth may improve tracking while reducing delay tolerance. The tradeoff depends on this lesson's topology and assumptions. Where the text omits a datasheet value, test configuration or certification criterion, the correct output is 'not established' rather than an invented margin.

§6.3 Boost PFC voltage and current shaping

Lesson 6.2: Boost PFC voltage and current shaping

Position: Unit 6, Active rectifiers and power-factor correction. Reading level: undergraduate circuit analysis, switching converters and elementary calculus. Learning claim: distinguish the mathematical approximation from the physical device-level consequences. Status: unreviewed teaching chapter.

Physical picture and mechanism

A boost PFC stage aims to make input current approximately proportional to rectified input voltage while holding a higher DC output. The reference has a zero crossing, where sampling noise, minimum on-time and diode conduction become important; a steady-state duty expression is least trustworthy during these intervals.

The practical boundary of the system matters. Draw where energy enters, where useful energy leaves, what storage changes within the process and where heat or field energy is likely to be lost. Define all voltage polarities before changing reference frames; a reported sign is physically meaningful only relative to a declared node or direction. The natural interval for averaging must also be stated: a switching-period equation, a line-frequency RMS result and a slowly varying thermal estimate describe different aspects of the same apparatus. One cannot simply average all three over an arbitrary interval and call the result a design guarantee.

Governing mathematical relation

For ideal CCM boost operation, Vout=Vin/(1−D), so D≈1−Vin/Vout for positive instantaneous rectified input voltage. Current shaping is a controlled target, not an automatic consequence of this conversion ratio.

Write the baseline relation symbolically first, then test it against a dimensional check and an elementary limiting case. For a variable expressed as a rate, verify what time base is implicit: per switching period, per electrical cycle, per unit mechanical rotation or per second. If the equation assumes continuous conduction, a linear magnetic inductance, an ideal waveform or a balanced three-phase input, say so before substituting numerical values. For a measured value, specify sensor position and measurement bandwidth; for a computed value, identify the exact source equation.

Worked numerical example with interpretation

On an illustrative rectified source with instantaneous positive voltage 200 V and a 400 V output, ideal D≈0.5. At 100 V, D≈0.75. A real high-line peak of 230√2≈325 V implies D≈0.187 near the peak. These are steady switching-cycle estimates and ignore bridge losses and zero crossings.

Model comparison workflow

A second model is useful only if it changes an identifiable assumption. Compare an ideal lossless expression with a bounded nonideal version, or a fixed load with a changed load; do not modify several unrelated quantities and attribute the entire difference to a single mechanism. Make a table showing which equations survive the changed operating regime and which must be derived again.

Include the neglected process in plain language even when it cannot be computed: leakage inductance, dead time, load-angle error, winding temperature or non-sinusoidal current can change a conclusion. §6.4 Double-line ripple and DC-link energy

Lesson 6.3: Double-line ripple and DC-link energy

Position: Unit 6, Active rectifiers and power-factor correction. Reading level: undergraduate circuit analysis, switching converters and elementary calculus. Learning claim: distinguish the mathematical approximation from the physical device-level consequences. Status: unreviewed teaching chapter.

Physical picture and mechanism

Single-phase instantaneous input power varies at twice the AC frequency even when current is sinusoidal and in phase. A DC load demanding nearly constant power forces the link capacitor or active buffer to absorb and release the difference. The result is a predictable low-frequency energy variation.

The practical boundary of the system matters. Draw where energy enters, where useful energy leaves, what storage changes within the process and where heat or field energy is likely to be lost. Define all voltage polarities before changing reference frames; a reported sign is physically meaningful only relative to a declared node or direction. The natural interval for averaging must also be stated: a switching-period equation, a line-frequency RMS result and a slowly varying thermal estimate describe different aspects of the same apparatus. One cannot simply average all three over an arbitrary interval and call the result a design guarantee.

Governing mathematical relation

With v=√2V sinωt and i=√2I sinωt, instantaneous power is p(t)=VI(1−cos2ωt). The oscillatory energy amplitude is P/(2ω). For a small capacitor ripple around a DC voltage Vdc, energy variation is approximately C Vdc ΔVpeak.

Write the baseline relation symbolically first, then test it against a dimensional check and an elementary limiting case. For a variable expressed as a rate, verify what time base is implicit: per switching period, per electrical cycle, per unit mechanical rotation or per second. If the equation assumes continuous conduction, a linear magnetic inductance, an ideal waveform or a balanced three-phase input, say so before substituting numerical values. For a measured value, specify sensor position and measurement bandwidth; for a computed value, identify the exact source equation.

Worked numerical example with interpretation

At P=1000 W on 50 Hz single-phase input, the ripple-energy amplitude is 1000/(2×2π×50)≈1.59 J. For C=1000 µF and Vdc=400 V, the ideal small-signal voltage ripple amplitude is ≈1.59/(0.001×400)=3.98 V, or around 7.96 V peak-to-peak.

If a frequency is a natural resonance, it cannot be presented as a measured closed-loop bandwidth. If a result is an ideal output voltage, it cannot be equated with the real part's dielectric withstand requirement. A second independent check, such as energy conservation, a complementary voltage, or the expected response as one variable approaches zero, should be written beside the arithmetic.

Measurement and inference workflow

Define what an instrument would actually observe, even though this exercise is software only. A spectrum analyzer reports a frequency-domain estimate, a current sensor reports a bandwidth-limited sample, and a thermal measurement reports temperature at a location and time. None directly measures every term in an analytical state equation. Design an independent cross-check using conservation of energy or symmetry before trusting a displayed result.

Record the intended sample rate, window, triggering event and reference node. For transient switching analyses distinguish a 10 ns peak from a 1 ms average and identify whether the probe itself could perturb the observed circuit. If a claim needs actual bench data, mark it explicitly as not yet established.

Important failure modes and nonideal boundaries

Do not substitute peak-to-peak ripple for its amplitude in the energy equation. Aging, capacitor ESR, control dynamics and load transients add effects beyond this low-frequency model.

These limitations are not a minor footnote: they are the conditions under which this chapter's conclusion could reverse. §6.5 Interleaved phases and current ripple

Lesson 6.4: Interleaved phases and current ripple

Position: Unit 6, Active rectifiers and power-factor correction. Reading level: undergraduate circuit analysis, switching converters and elementary calculus. Learning claim: distinguish the mathematical approximation from the physical device-level consequences. Status: unreviewed teaching chapter.

Physical picture and mechanism

Interleaving shifts the switching clocks of parallel converter phases, so portions of their high-frequency input-current ripple cancel in the combined source current. The average power and total energy balance do not vanish, and cancellation depends on phase count, duty ratio, matched components and operating conditions.

The practical boundary of the system matters. Draw where energy enters, where useful energy leaves, what storage changes within the process and where heat or field energy is likely to be lost. Define all voltage polarities before changing reference frames; a reported sign is physically meaningful only relative to a declared node or direction. The natural interval for averaging must also be stated: a switching-period equation, a line-frequency RMS result and a slowly varying thermal estimate describe different aspects of the same apparatus. One cannot simply average all three over an arbitrary interval and call the result a design guarantee.

Governing mathematical relation

For N phases, a common symmetric phase shift is 360°/N electrical at the switching frequency. At N=2, the clocks are 180° apart. If two equal triangular ripple waveforms oppose at a particular duty ratio, source-current ripple can be substantially lower than either individual phase ripple.

Write the baseline relation symbolically first, then test it against a dimensional check and an elementary limiting case. For a variable expressed as a rate, verify what time base is implicit: per switching period, per electrical cycle, per unit mechanical rotation or per second. If the equation assumes continuous conduction, a linear magnetic inductance, an ideal waveform or a balanced three-phase input, say so before substituting numerical values. For a measured value, specify sensor position and measurement bandwidth; for a computed value, identify the exact source equation.

Worked numerical example with interpretation

With a two-phase converter at 100 kHz, one phase repeats every 10 µs and the other is offset by 5 µs. If each phase carries 5 A average at equal load share, the combined input average is 10 A; ripple cancellation does not change that 10 A mean.

Design-release workflow

A mathematical design is complete only relative to its declared requirements. Produce a decision matrix connecting efficiency, voltage stress, current stress, temperature, dynamics, isolation where applicable, EMC and fault response. Each column has a different pass criterion; a high numerical efficiency cannot compensate for a failed voltage rating or missing fault isolation.

Audit the design under the worst credible input, the largest and smallest loads, startup, shutdown and a sensor fault. State which cases have been derived, which have only been simulated, and which demand independent qualified testing. A student solution can be rigorous while acknowledging that specific manufacturers' datasheets and safety standards have not been checked.

Bridge to the unit model

True AC source loading: Input-voltage sinusoidal shape does not guarantee input-current sinusoidal shape. Real power is a cycle-average product; peak current, RMS current and displacement require different calculations. The energy-buffer condition can impose a twice-line-frequency ripple even when input power factor is ideal.
