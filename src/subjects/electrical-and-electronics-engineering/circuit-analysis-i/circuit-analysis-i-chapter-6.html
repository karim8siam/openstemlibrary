§6.1 Natural RC Response

U06-L01 · Natural RC Response

Unit: 6 — First-Order Circuits Core relation: v_C(t)=V_0 e^(−t/RC) Essential ideas: time constant, initial charge, and exponential decay.

Learning objectives

After this lesson, explain natural rc response and its circuit context, assign reference polarities and directions, derive or use v_C(t)=V_0 e^(−t/RC) with SI units, solve a numerical example, and evaluate its validity using conservation laws and a practical limiting case.

Physical and mathematical foundation

A first-order system forgets its initial condition exponentially. The selected current arrow and voltage polarity give meaning to the signs in an equation; they do not force a physical quantity to flow in that direction. Nodes define equal-potential connection points in the lumped model. Branches define component connections, and a chosen ground provides a mathematical reference rather than a physical energy sink. Interpreting the equation v_C(t)=V_0 e^(−t/RC) requires knowing both the connected terminals and the applicable ideal-component assumptions.

Within first-order circuits, distinguish a source-imposed quantity from a circuit response. A voltage source constrains one terminal difference, while a current source constrains a branch current. An element's constitutive relation describes its response but cannot replace Kirchhoff's laws. If a diagram changes, the electrical topology may change even when the drawn symbols and their numerical values remain the same.

Concept 1 — Time constant. Identify the branch or terminals to which this idea applies, state its units, and connect it with v_C(t)=V_0 e^(−t/RC). Explain what changes when the source or component parameter increases; predict the direction of the change before doing arithmetic. Consider whether reference directions, instrument loading, or tolerance could distort the interpretation.

Concept 2 — Initial charge. Identify the branch or terminals to which this idea applies, state its units, and connect it with v_C(t)=V_0 e^(−t/RC). Explain what changes when the source or component parameter increases; predict the direction of the change before doing arithmetic. Consider whether reference directions, instrument loading, or tolerance could distort the interpretation.

Concept 3 — And exponential decay. Identify the branch or terminals to which this idea applies, state its units, and connect it with v_C(t)=V_0 e^(−t/RC). Explain what changes when the source or component parameter increases; predict the direction of the change before doing arithmetic. Consider whether reference directions, instrument loading, or tolerance could distort the interpretation.

Worked case and result

Case. A 10 kΩ resistor discharges a 100 µF capacitor from 10 V.

Result. The time constant is 1 s; after 1 s the voltage is about 3.679 V. First translate all prefixes into SI units and state the formula before substituting. Verify that the dimensions of the answer agree with the quantity being requested. Reverse the calculation, or test a meaningful limiting case, rather than trusting the displayed calculator result alone.

Interpretation. A first-order system forgets its initial condition exponentially. A negative signed answer may only mean that a current or voltage points opposite to its reference. It does not indicate an impossible circuit. When multiple elements appear, check their node-current balance, the signed sum of drops around a loop, and the sum of absorbed and delivered powers.

A reliable solution method

  1. Draw the topology, identify nodes, and choose polarities and branch-current arrows; write down which source and element quantities are known.
  2. Combine the component relation v_C(t)=V_0 e^(−t/RC) with KCL, KVL, and state continuity where relevant; solve symbolically before substituting numbers.
  3. Convert units, calculate, and cross-check using a conservation equation or limiting case. Explain why the result is physically plausible.
  4. Identify the dominant idealization. Never confuse the 63.2% rise fraction with the 36.8% remaining fraction on decay. If any predicted value approaches a component rating, the analysis is only a starting point for a safety-qualified design review.

Exercises

1. Concept: Explain why time constant needs a clear reference direction, voltage polarity, or system boundary. Provide one counterexample.

2. Numerical: Reproduce the worked case, showing each unit conversion. Increase one supplied value by 20% and compute the resulting change.

3. Verification: Construct a KCL, KVL, energy, or state-continuity check appropriate to this circuit; explain which assumption the check tests.

4. Application: Specify a current-limited low-voltage demonstration and describe how meter input impedance would change the result.

5. Extension: Change one boundary condition and explain whether `v_C(t)=V_0 e^(−t/RC)` remains valid and which previous shortcut fails.

Key limitation: Never confuse the 63.2% rise fraction with the 36.8% remaining fraction on decay.
