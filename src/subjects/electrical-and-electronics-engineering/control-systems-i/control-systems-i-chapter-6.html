§6.1 Proportional control and offset

Lesson 6.1 — Proportional control and offset

Unit: P, PI, PD and PID control. Prerequisites: differential equations, complex numbers, and the earlier lessons in the unit. Study: derive the relationship, reproduce the numerical case, investigate one changed assumption and complete the four problems. All numbers are synthetic and the models are not validated for hardware use.

Physical meaning and governing model

A proportional controller sets u=Kp(r−y) and requires nonzero error to sustain nonzero output in most type-0 plants. Increasing Kp generally reduces some low-frequency offsets but also increases control effort and can expose delay or neglected poles. A gain that is appropriate for a constant reference may be poor for fast commands.

A response can be fast and still fail accuracy or actuator requirements. Decide which metric matters before choosing gain.

Governing relationship: For P(s)=1/(s+1), T(s)=Kp/(s+1+Kp)

The formula should not be used until its input, output, initial state, sign convention and units have been declared. Distinguish a plant characteristic from the characteristic of a feedback interconnection. An algebraically valid transfer function is not an empirical model until its parameters and operating regime have supporting evidence. If an idealized element has no physical saturation or bandwidth, identify the missing implementation constraint before attempting design.

Worked example and interpretation

At Kp=4, a unit-step command yields final y=4/5=0.8, offset 0.2 and closed-loop time constant 0.2 s. Raising Kp to 9 reduces offset to 0.1 but makes initial controller demand 9 units.

Check the magnitude against a relevant limit. In a continuous model, a pole measured in reciprocal seconds gives a time constant through its reciprocal; in a digital model, a pole must be inside the unit circle. A calculation of an output amplitude does not identify a suitable control input unless the actuator relationship is also known. For this particular example, copy the expression into a fresh worksheet, write the intermediate substitution with units and test whether the result changes in the expected direction when one parameter changes.

Limits, counterexamples and model evidence

With a finite voltage supply a proportional step may immediately saturate. An anti-noise or filtering requirement can also limit usable gain.

A useful counterexample changes one important feature at a time: initial state, operating point, saturation level, sensor filtering, delay or an unmodeled fast mode. The fact that a trace is well-fitted is not proof of predictive validity. A good validation experiment uses data withheld during fitting; it should also record the actual input, since the requested command may not equal the delivered signal. If evidence is missing, write a conditional conclusion instead of inventing a parameter or tolerance.

Application perspective — robot wheel velocity control

Encoder quantization and friction near zero speed can dominate the low-speed error, while motor current limits dominate accelerations. A gain selected from one operating point should be tested at multiple commanded speeds and load torques. The model must be rewritten in consistent angular units if rpm is used for telemetry but rad/s is used in derivations.

The experiment should define the performance metric before looking at a plot. Appropriate measures differ by task: a transient model can be checked by peak, delay and settling behavior; a frequency response by gain and phase; a state model by observable signals and sensitivity to starting conditions. A controller that improves one metric but violates current, force, thermal, bandwidth or latency limits is not an acceptable solution. Explain which additional measurement best distinguishes the model from its main competing explanation.

§6.2 Integral action and PI control

Lesson 6.2 — Integral action and PI control

Unit: P, PI, PD and PID control. Prerequisites: differential equations, complex numbers, and the earlier lessons in the unit. Study: derive the relationship, reproduce the numerical case, investigate one changed assumption and complete the four problems. All numbers are synthetic and the models are not validated for hardware use.

Physical meaning and governing model

Integral action adds a state proportional to accumulated tracking error, helping remove a constant error when the closed loop stabilizes and the steady command is feasible. In parallel notation C(s)=Kp+Ki/s, Ki has different units from Kp. The integrator must be initialized and limited in a realizable implementation.

The double pole at −2 makes a critically damped characteristic, but numerator zeros mean the output step is not automatically identical to a canonical zero-free second-order response.

Governing relationship: For P(s)=1/(s+1), C(s)=Kp+Ki/s; denominator s²+(1+Kp)s+Ki

The formula should not be used until its input, output, initial state, sign convention and units have been declared. Distinguish a plant characteristic from the characteristic of a feedback interconnection. An algebraically valid transfer function is not an empirical model until its parameters and operating regime have supporting evidence. If an idealized element has no physical saturation or bandwidth, identify the missing implementation constraint before attempting design.

Worked example and interpretation

Choose Kp=3 and Ki=4: the characteristic is s²+4s+4=(s+2)². The loop is asymptotically stable and, with no saturation or bias, has zero error for a constant step.

§6.3 Derivative action and filtered PID

Lesson 6.3 — Derivative action and filtered PID

Unit: P, PI, PD and PID control. Prerequisites: differential equations, complex numbers, and the earlier lessons in the unit. Study: derive the relationship, reproduce the numerical case, investigate one changed assumption and complete the four problems. All numbers are synthetic and the models are not validated for hardware use.

Physical meaning and governing model

Derivative action reacts to how the measured output changes and can add phase lead, but ideal differentiation amplifies unbounded high-frequency measurement noise. An implementable derivative branch uses a filter, often Kd s/(1+τf s), and derivative-on-measurement avoids a setpoint kick.

A small-amplitude, high-frequency noise source can overwhelm an actuator when differentiated. Filtering limits this at the cost of some phase lead and an additional dynamic state.

Governing relationship: u=Kpe+Kiintegral(e)−Kd*d(y_filtered)/dt

The formula should not be used until its input, output, initial state, sign convention and units have been declared. Distinguish a plant characteristic from the characteristic of a feedback interconnection. An algebraically valid transfer function is not an empirical model until its parameters and operating regime have supporting evidence. If an idealized element has no physical saturation or bandwidth, identify the missing implementation constraint before attempting design.

Worked example and interpretation

If measured noise is n(t)=0.01 sin(200t) V, its ideal derivative amplitude is 0.01×200=2 V/s. Multiplying by Kd=0.5 produces a control ripple amplitude of 1 in the chosen consistent units.

Check the magnitude against a relevant limit. In a continuous model, a pole measured in reciprocal seconds gives a time constant through its reciprocal; in a digital model, a pole must be inside the unit circle. §6.4 Saturation, windup and bumpless transfer

Lesson 6.4 — Saturation, windup and bumpless transfer

Unit: P, PI, PD and PID control. Prerequisites: differential equations, complex numbers, and the earlier lessons in the unit. Study: derive the relationship, reproduce the numerical case, investigate one changed assumption and complete the four problems. All numbers are synthetic and the models are not validated for hardware use.

Physical meaning and governing model

When u_cmd exceeds available actuator authority, the difference between command and actual actuation can drive an integrator in the wrong direction. Conditional integration or back-calculation can mitigate windup, but the antiwindup logic and state transitions must be tested. Bumpless transfer aligns integrator states when switching between manual and automatic modes.

During saturation, the plant behavior is dominated by physical limits rather than by the linear closed-loop poles. Avoid drawing conclusions from a purely linear pole table.

Governing relationship: u_actual=clip(u_unsat,umin,umax); I_dot=e+Kaw*(u_actual−u_unsat)

The formula should not be used until its input, output, initial state, sign convention and units have been declared. Distinguish a plant characteristic from the characteristic of a feedback interconnection. An algebraically valid transfer function is not an empirical model until its parameters and operating regime have supporting evidence. If an idealized element has no physical saturation or bandwidth, identify the missing implementation constraint before attempting design.

Worked example and interpretation

Suppose a PI output requests 12 V while the supply permits 5 V. With Kaw=0.5 s⁻¹, the back-calculation term contributes −3.5 V-equivalent per second in an appropriately scaled integrator equation, opposing excessive accumulation.

Check the magnitude against a relevant limit. In a continuous model, a pole measured in reciprocal seconds gives a time constant through its reciprocal; in a digital model, a pole must be inside the unit circle. A calculation of an output amplitude does not identify a suitable control input unless the actuator relationship is also known. For this particular example, copy the expression into a fresh worksheet, write the intermediate substitution with units and test whether the result changes in the expected direction when one parameter changes.

Limits, counterexamples and model evidence

An antiwindup method can itself cause undesirable transitions if measurement noise, quantization or switching logic is poorly handled. Its implementation requires defined units.

§6.5 Engineering Design Project: Frequency-Response Techniques: Bode Plots and Relative Stability Margins

Unit 6 engineering project — PI control with actuator saturation

Research question. Can a compact model predict a second operating condition reliably, and what do its residuals mean? This is a synthetic computation-only assignment. No energized or safety-critical tests are required.

Case data and required work

Use plant y_dot=−y+u and parallel PI gains Kp=2, Ki=1, then Kp=4, Ki=4. Start with a unit step, then a reference of 10 while |u|≤2, and return to zero. Compare integrator state with and without conditional antiwindup. Report tracking, command clipping, recovery and whether any demanded reference is impossible.

Reproducible workflow

  1. Prepare a model card containing the independent variable, measured output, units, initial conditions, parameter values and restrictions. Distinguish fixed known parameters from values estimated using the training observations. Set a reproducible numerical tolerance and record tool versions if code is used.
  2. Write the parent differential equation or characteristic relationship. Derive one check by hand and one by independent numerical evaluation. Use enough precision internally to avoid arithmetic drift, but round reported results to the underlying input precision.
  3. Build a graph that includes a physically meaningful axis range, a legend, a command trace, model output and any withheld validation output. If a logarithmic frequency axis is used, never include zero frequency as a point on that axis.
  4. Investigate at least two plausible parameter alternatives. Report which output feature changes most and whether that sensitivity points to an important new measurement. If a parameter cannot be estimated uniquely, say why instead of choosing a convenient number.
  5. Document the principal discrepancy between model and validation trace. State whether the discrepancy is likely random noise, a coherent time delay, a calibration offset, unmodeled dynamics or a regime change. Propose a specific follow-up experiment capable of distinguishing at least two possibilities.
  6. Finish with a decision memo stating what the experiment establishes, what it cannot establish, and whether further evidence is needed before any real use.

Measurement and interpretation lens

Actuator saturation and integrator recovery: Apply reference steps that are both achievable and unachievable, and compare PI loops with and without antiwindup. Record the integrator state, actual saturation-limited command and recovery time after reducing the reference. The control signal is as important as the plotted output.

The baseline computation should be viewed as a model prediction, not a design certificate. The next experiment should be chosen for information gain: an extra trace is helpful only if it can distinguish competing explanations. A practical model is sometimes deliberately simpler than the physical plant; defend simplification on the basis of bandwidth, residuals, risk, and data rather than convenience. Be explicit about initial states, since two identical inputs can yield different measured trajectories when starting conditions differ.

Required submitted artifacts

  • A short derivation with dimensions, sign convention and limits.
  • A machine-readable table of inputs and outputs, with units in headers and an explanation of any synthetic perturbation.
  • Two labeled plots: an explanatory baseline plot and a separate uncertainty or hold-out prediction plot.
  • An audit table separating assumptions, directly measured quantities, fitted parameters and conditions outside the model.
  • One paragraph on model usefulness; one paragraph on the strongest counterexample or failure scenario.

Evaluation rubric and common mistakes

25% theory: valid differential equation, transfer function, characteristic equation or discrete update with correct signals. 25% arithmetic: independently checked numerical values and declared units. 20% evaluation: withheld-case prediction, uncertainty and an honestly labeled residual plot. 15% communication: reproducible data, readable graphs and explicit limitations. 15% safe experimental reasoning: no substitution of classroom simulation for real commissioning or protective design.

A common failure is tuning a parameter on every available point and then citing the same points as validation. Another is reporting an impressive percentage error despite a missing or nearly zero denominator. Do not hide cases with poor fit. If the model predicts instability or impossible inputs, document the finding and stop at simulation; do not seek to reproduce an unsafe condition on a real instrument.
