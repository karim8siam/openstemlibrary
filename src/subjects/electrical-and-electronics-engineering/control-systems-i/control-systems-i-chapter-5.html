§5.1 Sinusoidal steady-state response

Lesson 5.1 — Sinusoidal steady-state response

Unit: Frequency response and robustness margins. Prerequisites: differential equations, complex numbers, and the earlier lessons in the unit. Study: derive the relationship, reproduce the numerical case, investigate one changed assumption and complete the four problems. All numbers are synthetic and the models are not validated for hardware use.

Physical meaning and governing model

In a stable LTI system, evaluating G(jω) gives the complex steady-state gain at angular frequency ω. Its magnitude scales sinusoidal amplitude and its argument supplies phase shift, after natural transients decay. This frequency response is not meaningful as a guaranteed bounded steady state if unstable modes dominate.

The frequency is 5 rad/s, not 5 Hz; confusing them shifts design interpretations by a factor of 2π. Frequency response is a useful experimental identification tool if excitation remains safe and within the linear region.

Governing relationship: For G(s)=1/(taus+1): |G(jω)|=1/sqrt(1+(omegatau)^2)

The formula should not be used until its input, output, initial state, sign convention and units have been declared. Distinguish a plant characteristic from the characteristic of a feedback interconnection. An algebraically valid transfer function is not an empirical model until its parameters and operating regime have supporting evidence. If an idealized element has no physical saturation or bandwidth, identify the missing implementation constraint before attempting design.

Worked example and interpretation

With tau=0.2 s and ω=5 rad/s, magnitude is 1/√2≈0.707 and phase is −45°. A 2 V amplitude input yields approximately 1.414 V amplitude output at that frequency.

Check the magnitude against a relevant limit. In a continuous model, a pole measured in reciprocal seconds gives a time constant through its reciprocal; in a digital model, a pole must be inside the unit circle. A calculation of an output amplitude does not identify a suitable control input unless the actuator relationship is also known. For this particular example, copy the expression into a fresh worksheet, write the intermediate substitution with units and test whether the result changes in the expected direction when one parameter changes.

Limits, counterexamples and model evidence

A sinusoidal response can be obscured by drift or harmonic distortion. Measure phase against a common time reference and allow sufficient cycles after transients.

A useful counterexample changes one important feature at a time: initial state, operating point, saturation level, sensor filtering, delay or an unmodeled fast mode. The fact that a trace is well-fitted is not proof of predictive validity. A good validation experiment uses data withheld during fitting; it should also record the actual input, since the requested command may not equal the delivered signal. If evidence is missing, write a conditional conclusion instead of inventing a parameter or tolerance.

Application perspective — audio amplifier feedback

The feedback loop can improve small-signal fidelity and yet be sensitive to capacitive loads or additional high-frequency phase lag. A narrowband test at a comfortable audio frequency is insufficient evidence of wideband stability. A laboratory plan must identify which physical measurements are safe and what separate protective circuitry limits energy.

The experiment should define the performance metric before looking at a plot. Appropriate measures differ by task: a transient model can be checked by peak, delay and settling behavior; a frequency response by gain and phase; a state model by observable signals and sensitivity to starting conditions. A controller that improves one metric but violates current, force, thermal, bandwidth or latency limits is not an acceptable solution. Explain which additional measurement best distinguishes the model from its main competing explanation.

1. Computation and sensitivity. Repeat the calculation at 5 Hz; demonstrate how dramatically the response differs from the 5 rad/s result. Show how the relevant parameter enters the governing equation. If the data do not determine a single number, list the missing information and work symbolically.

2. Measurement planning. Name a second performance variable that could become worse while the headline performance metric improves. Explain how the proposed measurement separates a parameter estimate from an unobserved initial condition.

3. Failure analysis. Which measured quantity would most directly separate an initial-condition error from a plant-parameter error? Give the signal units and observation window. Trace the proposed change through input, state and measured output rather than saying only that “the system may become unstable.”

4. Comparison. Relate this model to the following lesson, Constructing Bode plots. Name one assumption shared by the two topics and one assumption requiring a different experiment or mathematical analysis.

§5.2 Constructing Bode plots

Lesson 5.2 — Constructing Bode plots

Unit: Frequency response and robustness margins. Prerequisites: differential equations, complex numbers, and the earlier lessons in the unit. Study: derive the relationship, reproduce the numerical case, investigate one changed assumption and complete the four problems. All numbers are synthetic and the models are not validated for hardware use.

Physical meaning and governing model

A Bode plot separates magnitude in decibels and phase in degrees as functions of logarithmic angular frequency. Poles introduce slopes near their break frequencies and zeros introduce compensating slope changes. The straight-line approximation is a planning device; exact evaluation is needed near crossovers.

At each break the exact magnitude departs from the naive corner-intersection sketch. Phase lags accumulate smoothly, not as an instantaneous 90° jump at a break.

Governing relationship: G(s)=10/[(1+s/2)(1+s/20)]

The formula should not be used until its input, output, initial state, sign convention and units have been declared. Distinguish a plant characteristic from the characteristic of a feedback interconnection. An algebraically valid transfer function is not an empirical model until its parameters and operating regime have supporting evidence. If an idealized element has no physical saturation or bandwidth, identify the missing implementation constraint before attempting design.

Worked example and interpretation

DC magnitude is 20 dB. The first pole at 2 rad/s introduces an approximate −20 dB/decade trend, and after 20 rad/s a second pole steepens the asymptote toward −40 dB/decade.

Check the magnitude against a relevant limit. In a continuous model, a pole measured in reciprocal seconds gives a time constant through its reciprocal; in a digital model, a pole must be inside the unit circle. §5.3 Gain and phase margin interpretation

Lesson 5.3 — Gain and phase margin interpretation

Unit: Frequency response and robustness margins. Prerequisites: differential equations, complex numbers, and the earlier lessons in the unit. Study: derive the relationship, reproduce the numerical case, investigate one changed assumption and complete the four problems. All numbers are synthetic and the models are not validated for hardware use.

Physical meaning and governing model

A classical phase margin is measured at a frequency where the loop magnitude is 1, comparing phase to −180° for the standard negative-feedback convention. Gain margin concerns loop magnitude where phase reaches −180°. The definitions need attention for multiple crossings, unstable open loops and delays.

Delay has unit magnitude yet adds frequency-dependent phase. It can destabilize a loop that appears attractive from its gain plot alone.

Governing relationship: PM=180°+arg[L(jω_gc)] where |L(jω_gc)|=1

The formula should not be used until its input, output, initial state, sign convention and units have been declared. Distinguish a plant characteristic from the characteristic of a feedback interconnection. An algebraically valid transfer function is not an empirical model until its parameters and operating regime have supporting evidence. If an idealized element has no physical saturation or bandwidth, identify the missing implementation constraint before attempting design.

Worked example and interpretation

If |L| crosses unity at 4 rad/s and the unwrapped phase is −135°, phase margin is 45°. If an extra 0.05 s pure delay is inserted, it adds about −11.46° at 4 rad/s and reduces local phase margin to roughly 33.5°.

Check the magnitude against a relevant limit. In a continuous model, a pole measured in reciprocal seconds gives a time constant through its reciprocal; in a digital model, a pole must be inside the unit circle. A calculation of an output amplitude does not identify a suitable control input unless the actuator relationship is also known. §5.4 Nyquist reasoning and loop shaping

Lesson 5.4 — Nyquist reasoning and loop shaping

Unit: Frequency response and robustness margins. Prerequisites: differential equations, complex numbers, and the earlier lessons in the unit. Study: derive the relationship, reproduce the numerical case, investigate one changed assumption and complete the four problems. All numbers are synthetic and the models are not validated for hardware use.

Physical meaning and governing model

The Nyquist criterion relates encirclements of the point −1 to open-loop right-half-plane poles and resulting closed-loop right-half-plane zeros of 1+L. Its power is handling systems whose open loop is unstable, where simple margin intuition can mislead. The contour and sign conventions must be stated explicitly.

The geometry emphasizes why phase and gain jointly matter. A point at magnitude 0.9 and angle −180° is only 0.1 from the critical point and may have very high sensitivity.

Governing relationship: Closed-loop characteristic: 1+L(s)=0; critical point L=−1

The formula should not be used until its input, output, initial state, sign convention and units have been declared. Distinguish a plant characteristic from the characteristic of a feedback interconnection. An algebraically valid transfer function is not an empirical model until its parameters and operating regime have supporting evidence. If an idealized element has no physical saturation or bandwidth, identify the missing implementation constraint before attempting design.

Worked example and interpretation

For a stable open loop with a polar curve that stays away from −1 and makes zero net encirclements, the closed loop has no right-half-plane poles under the standard criterion assumptions. By contrast, an open-loop right-half-plane pole requires a compensating encirclement to obtain stable feedback.

Check the magnitude against a relevant limit. In a continuous model, a pole measured in reciprocal seconds gives a time constant through its reciprocal; in a digital model, a pole must be inside the unit circle. A calculation of an output amplitude does not identify a suitable control input unless the actuator relationship is also known. For this particular example, copy the expression into a fresh worksheet, write the intermediate substitution with units and test whether the result changes in the expected direction when one parameter changes.

Limits, counterexamples and model evidence

Time delays require frequency-dependent phase; samples, right-half-plane zeros and hidden modes limit how aggressively a loop can be shaped. Numerical Nyquist plots require sufficient frequency coverage.

A useful counterexample changes one important feature at a time: initial state, operating point, saturation level, sensor filtering, delay or an unmodeled fast mode. §5.5 Engineering Design Project: Root Locus Analysis, Sketching Rules, and Parameter Sensitivity

Unit 5 engineering project — Bode loop-shaping investigation

Research question. Can a compact model predict a second operating condition reliably, and what do its residuals mean? This is a synthetic computation-only assignment. No energized or safety-critical tests are required.

Case data and required work

Use G(s)=10/((1+s/2)(1+s/20)). Evaluate logarithmic points from 0.1 to 200 rad/s, extract exact magnitude and phase, and overlay asymptotic straight-line segments. Examine a pure delay of 0.02 and 0.08 s. Explain how crossover phase changes, and never treat a gain-only plot as sufficient proof of robust stability.

Reproducible workflow

  1. Prepare a model card containing the independent variable, measured output, units, initial conditions, parameter values and restrictions. Distinguish fixed known parameters from values estimated using the training observations. Set a reproducible numerical tolerance and record tool versions if code is used.
  2. Write the parent differential equation or characteristic relationship. Derive one check by hand and one by independent numerical evaluation. Use enough precision internally to avoid arithmetic drift, but round reported results to the underlying input precision.
  3. Build a graph that includes a physically meaningful axis range, a legend, a command trace, model output and any withheld validation output. If a logarithmic frequency axis is used, never include zero frequency as a point on that axis.
  4. Investigate at least two plausible parameter alternatives. Report which output feature changes most and whether that sensitivity points to an important new measurement. If a parameter cannot be estimated uniquely, say why instead of choosing a convenient number.
  5. Document the principal discrepancy between model and validation trace. State whether the discrepancy is likely random noise, a coherent time delay, a calibration offset, unmodeled dynamics or a regime change. Propose a specific follow-up experiment capable of distinguishing at least two possibilities.
  6. Finish with a decision memo stating what the experiment establishes, what it cannot establish, and whether further evidence is needed before any real use.

Measurement and interpretation lens

Frequency sweep and delay margin: Perform a logarithmic sweep, collecting output amplitude and phase relative to the input at each frequency. At crossover interpolate magnitude and phase rather than choosing the nearest sample. Test the effect of adding a small explicit delay and distinguish modeled delay from sampling latency.

The baseline computation should be viewed as a model prediction, not a design certificate. The next experiment should be chosen for information gain: an extra trace is helpful only if it can distinguish competing explanations. A practical model is sometimes deliberately simpler than the physical plant; defend simplification on the basis of bandwidth, residuals, risk, and data rather than convenience. Be explicit about initial states, since two identical inputs can yield different measured trajectories when starting conditions differ.

Required submitted artifacts

  • A short derivation with dimensions, sign convention and limits.
  • A machine-readable table of inputs and outputs, with units in headers and an explanation of any synthetic perturbation.
  • Two labeled plots: an explanatory baseline plot and a separate uncertainty or hold-out prediction plot.
  • An audit table separating assumptions, directly measured quantities, fitted parameters and conditions outside the model.
  • One paragraph on model usefulness; one paragraph on the strongest counterexample or failure scenario.

Evaluation rubric and common mistakes

25% theory: valid differential equation, transfer function, characteristic equation or discrete update with correct signals. 25% arithmetic: independently checked numerical values and declared units. 20% evaluation: withheld-case prediction, uncertainty and an honestly labeled residual plot. 15% communication: reproducible data, readable graphs and explicit limitations. 15% safe experimental reasoning: no substitution of classroom simulation for real commissioning or protective design.

A common failure is tuning a parameter on every available point and then citing the same points as validation. Another is reporting an impressive percentage error despite a missing or nearly zero denominator. Do not hide cases with poor fit. If the model predicts instability or impossible inputs, document the finding and stop at simulation; do not seek to reproduce an unsafe condition on a real instrument.
