§4.1 HVDC architectures and converters

Unit 4, Lesson 1: HVDC architectures and converters

Course position: High-voltage DC transmission and power electronics. Study time: approximately 90–120 minutes including quantitative checks. Model domain: a synthetic, simplified calculation requiring interpretation. Safety classification: non-operational.

Physical idea and engineering context

A point-to-point DC link has converters at both ends and one or more DC conductors carrying controlled current. Monopolar, bipolar and symmetrical configurations have different voltage definitions, return paths and contingency capability. An LCC link requires a suitable AC commutation system, while a VSC uses self-commutated switches and can provide more flexible control of active and reactive power. Neither topology is automatically superior: project context dominates.

Governing model, boundaries and assumptions

P_DC≈V_DC×I_DC for the explicitly defined DC pole-pair convention; total losses include converter and conductor components

Worked quantitative example

With a simplified two-conductor link rated at 500 kV pole-to-pole DC voltage and 1 kA current, ideal transfer is 500 MW. If each station dissipates 5 MW and the cable pair has 10 MW resistive loss at that current, receiving-end power is 480 MW in the assumed steady condition. A bipolar ±500 kV scheme would have 1000 kV pole-to-pole; do not confuse these labels.

Interpretation and limiting cases

State whether the entered voltage is pole-to-ground or pole-to-pole before multiplying by current. Current return arrangement changes which conductor resistances are counted.

What could make this answer wrong?

A popular but misleading shortcut is to assume that an HVDC project is cheaper beyond one fixed distance. Converter terminals, routing and use of underground cable can shift the cross-over greatly.

Applied comparison and design reasoning

Compare connecting two asynchronous AC systems with connecting islands inside the same synchronous area. Identify which control and protection capabilities gain value from a DC link.

Guided problems

Problem A — first-principles check. Convert a ±250 kV pole voltage to pole-to-pole voltage and calculate power at 800 A under a defined two-conductor path. Show the relationship used, a fully labeled unit conversion if needed, the computed result and an interpretive sentence.

Problem B — alternate assumption. Point out the AC and DC measurement locations where converter losses may appear. State which terms in the equation change and which remain fixed. If the problem lacks a unique numerical answer, identify the missing measurements rather than guessing.

Problem C — critical reading. A diagram says “±320 kV” and an export table says “640 kV.” Show how both statements can be consistent and what current must accompany the power entry. Write a short technical response containing one quantitative or logical check, one serious counterargument, and one criterion that would falsify your initial conclusion.

§4.2 DC-line losses and operating limits

Unit 4, Lesson 2: DC-line losses and operating limits

Course position: High-voltage DC transmission and power electronics. Study time: approximately 90–120 minutes including quantitative checks. Model domain: a synthetic, simplified calculation requiring interpretation. Safety classification: non-operational.

Physical idea and engineering context

Conductor Joule loss grows approximately with current squared, whereas delivered power grows with current when voltage is controlled. At high loading, raising DC voltage can reduce current for the same transfer, but voltage insulation and converter limits constrain operation. Thermal environment and burial conditions influence conductor temperature and allowable current. The converter loss model itself may contain constant, linear and quadratic terms.

Governing model, boundaries and assumptions

P_line_loss = I_DC² R_total; for fixed sending DC power, I_DC=P_DC/V_DC, neglecting voltage changes from losses

Worked quantitative example

For 400 MW at a modeled 400 kV DC sending voltage, current is 1000 A. With total loop resistance 15 Ω, line loss is 15 MW. At 500 kV for the same 400 MW transfer and same resistance, current is 800 A and loss is 9.6 MW. This neglects converter operating-point changes and receiving-voltage drop; it is a comparison of selected simplified assumptions.

Interpretation and limiting cases

R_total is the sum for the actual current path, not resistance per kilometre unless multiplied by route length and conductor count correctly.

What could make this answer wrong?

Inferring thermal safety from I²R alone is incomplete because ambient temperature, cooling, bonding and operating history affect conductor temperature.

Applied comparison and design reasoning

An operator suggests increasing voltage to improve efficiency. Identify the converter voltage ceiling, cable insulation limits, control margin and equipment clearances that must be verified first.

Guided problems

Problem A — first-principles check. If current decreases by 20% with unchanged R, determine the new I²R loss fraction. Show the relationship used, a fully labeled unit conversion if needed, the computed result and an interpretive sentence.

Problem B — alternate assumption. Explain why higher voltage is not free from insulation and terminal-cost consequences. State which terms in the equation change and which remain fixed. If the problem lacks a unique numerical answer, identify the missing measurements rather than guessing.

Problem C — critical reading. Derive the percentage of sent energy lost over a 10-hour constant operation with the 400 kV case, and state why using this result for an annual business case is unreliable. Write a short technical response containing one quantitative or logical check, one serious counterargument, and one criterion that would falsify your initial conclusion.

§4.3 VSC active/reactive control and grid support

Unit 4, Lesson 3: VSC active/reactive control and grid support

Course position: High-voltage DC transmission and power electronics. Study time: approximately 90–120 minutes including quantitative checks. Model domain: a synthetic, simplified calculation requiring interpretation. Safety classification: non-operational.

Physical idea and engineering context

A VSC terminal can regulate active power and reactive power within its current and voltage capability. The P-Q capability envelope changes with AC voltage, DC voltage and the chosen operating mode. During faults, current limitation may force a trade-off between active and reactive injections. “Can supply reactive power” does not mean “can supply arbitrary reactive power” when already close to rated current.

Governing model, boundaries and assumptions

At balanced AC interface: S = sqrt(3) V_LL I; P²+Q²≤S_max² under a fixed voltage and current limit

Worked quantitative example

Take a VSC terminal with a 100 MVA AC current-limited apparent-power envelope at a chosen voltage. At P=80 MW, the ideal maximum |Q| is sqrt(100²−80²)=60 MVAr. At P=96 MW, the ideal maximum falls to 28 MVAr. This geometric envelope ignores thermal overload ranges and voltage-dependent limitations.

Interpretation and limiting cases

Reactive sign convention must be clear: a device supplying VARs to the AC system may be described as positive injection under one convention and negative consumption under another.

What could make this answer wrong?

Using a rectangular 100 MW by 100 MVAr capability picture would falsely permit combinations whose apparent power exceeds 100 MVA.

Applied comparison and design reasoning

A weak AC connection sees a voltage dip while a VSC is at high active export. Discuss the operational trade-off if reactive current must increase under a strict current ceiling.

Guided problems

Problem A — first-principles check. At Smax=50 MVA and P=30 MW, calculate ideal Q capability at fixed interface voltage. Show the relationship used, a fully labeled unit conversion if needed, the computed result and an interpretive sentence.

Problem B — alternate assumption. Describe one fault-voltage condition under which this steady circle ceases to be sufficient. State which terms in the equation change and which remain fixed. If the problem lacks a unique numerical answer, identify the missing measurements rather than guessing.

Problem C — critical reading. Compute the necessary derating of active power to provide 80 MVAr within the simplified 100 MVA circle. Write a short technical response containing one quantitative or logical check, one serious counterargument, and one criterion that would falsify your initial conclusion.

§4.4 Multiterminal DC and DC fault challenges

Unit 4, Lesson 4: Multiterminal DC and DC fault challenges

Course position: High-voltage DC transmission and power electronics. Study time: approximately 90–120 minutes including quantitative checks. Model domain: a synthetic, simplified calculation requiring interpretation. Safety classification: non-operational.

Physical idea and engineering context

A multiterminal DC network can route energy between more than two converter locations, but fault detection and isolation become more difficult because DC fault currents have no natural alternating zero crossing. Capacitors, AC sources through converter structures, inductors and conductor capacitances affect initial transients. DC breakers or converter-specific fault strategies must be coordinated with network protection design. The control hierarchy also needs a voltage-balancing responsibility.

Governing model, boundaries and assumptions

For a simple DC capacitance C at voltage V, stored energy E_C = 0.5 C V²; time-scale of discharge also depends on network impedance

Worked quantitative example

An idealized DC bus has C=5 mF at 20 kV. Its stored energy is 0.5×0.005×(20000)²=1,000,000 J or 1 MJ. If terminal voltage doubles with the same capacitance, stored energy quadruples. This does not tell us the peak fault current because discharge impedance, protective action and converter contributions remain unspecified.

Interpretation and limiting cases

A high stored-energy figure is not an arc-flash calculation and cannot define safe approach distance. The DC network is an electrical dynamic system, not just a constant-voltage battery.

What could make this answer wrong?

Assuming an AC circuit breaker behaves identically on DC neglects the absence of current zeros and device-specific arc interruption mechanisms.

Applied comparison and design reasoning

Imagine three DC terminals, one exporting and two importing. Identify who regulates DC voltage when the exporting terminal trips and what happens if no unit has sufficient margin.

Guided problems

Problem A — first-principles check. Determine stored energy of 2 mF at 10 kV and at 20 kV. Show the relationship used, a fully labeled unit conversion if needed, the computed result and an interpretive sentence.

Problem B — alternate assumption. Explain why equal capacitor energy does not imply equal DC fault current. State which terms in the equation change and which remain fixed. If the problem lacks a unique numerical answer, identify the missing measurements rather than guessing.

Problem C — critical reading. Why can two DC networks with identical voltage and capacitance experience different peak fault currents? Name at least three missing physical parameters. Write a short technical response containing one quantitative or logical check, one serious counterargument, and one criterion that would falsify your initial conclusion.

§4.7 System Dynamics and Grid Modeling: Power System Stability: Rotor Dynamics, Swing Equation, and Equal-Area Criterion

Unit 4: High-voltage DC transmission and power electronics

HVDC networks exchange power using converter stations and DC transmission conductors. Their comparison with AC transmission depends on distance, right of way, cable charging, terminal cost, losses, control requirements and reliability. Line-commutated converters and voltage-source converters differ in reactive-power behavior, commutation constraints, island-grid support and black-start potential. At the teaching level, steady DC voltage-current power is straightforward, but converter control and electromagnetic transient simulation require additional care. This unit avoids claiming universal economic break-even distances.

Deep reading — interacting limitations

DC power transfer has a tempting one-line P=VI relation, but power measured before an exporting converter, at the line entrance and after the receiving converter are three different quantities. Label the metering boundary before comparing efficiency. Conductor loss depends on the complete return circuit and current, while converter dissipation depends on technology and operating point. A two-terminal VSC link can exchange reactive power locally even when its active transfer setpoint is constrained by DC energy balance. Multiterminal DC adds control allocation and fault clearing challenges; stored capacitor energy can make initial fault behavior very rapid. Comparing AC and DC with one “crossover distance” omits terrain, cable type, reliability, siting and future expansion. In any proposed investment, verify the service requirement first and the technology second.

Case synthesis: compare corridors at consistent delivery boundaries

A proposed 400 MW interconnector can be implemented with AC or DC technology, but an honest comparison must first define whether the rating refers to power at the sending AC terminal, the DC line entrance or the receiving AC terminal. Suppose a toy DC study fixes power injected into a line at 400 MW and holds an aggregate return-loop resistance of 15 Ω. At a defined 400 kV line voltage, the approximate current is 1 kA and conductor loss 15 MW. If the receiving converter loses 5 MW, the receiving AC delivery is about 380 MW. It would be invalid to call this 400 MW delivered simply because 400 MW entered the cable. A different choice of reference boundary would require adding the exporting converter’s losses and adjusting line current accordingly.

At the same input power, raising the idealized DC voltage to 500 kV reduces current to 0.8 kA and conductor loss to 9.6 MW. Delivered receiving AC power would be approximately 385.4 MW with the same receiving-terminal dissipation. This arithmetic shows the basic efficiency motive for higher voltage, not permission to raise an existing link’s operating voltage. Insulation, clearance, converter design and allowable DC voltage must be established independently. A VSC terminal also has active/reactive limits at its AC connection; its ability to assist voltage does not provide unlimited current at rated active-power transfer.

The economic question is harder. For a hypothetical corridor with a large fixed converter capital charge, a short route may favor AC in an elementary cost line, while a long cable route with charging constraints may favor DC. However, the assumed unit cost per kilometre depends on underground or overhead construction, environmental requirements, right of way, subsea installation and local procurement conditions. Reliability and planned outages may further modify the comparison. A single distance where two invented straight lines cross is merely a property of that chosen cost dataset, not a physical constant.

Multiterminal links add a network-level problem absent from the two-terminal arithmetic. When one exporting terminal trips, another converter must regulate DC voltage or a higher-level scheme must safely reduce imports. Fault isolation can be rapid because the DC network lacks the natural current zero of alternating power. Capacitor energy and the fault loop influence transient behavior; a steady P=VI model gives no basis for estimating breaker duty. A credible corridor proposal therefore contains separate steady losses, dynamic controls, protection philosophy, lifecycle costs and uncertainty—not one total efficiency number.
