§3.1 Unsymmetrical faults and sequence networks

Unit 3, Lesson 1: Unsymmetrical faults and sequence networks

Course position: Protection principles and fault studies. Study time: approximately 90–120 minutes including quantitative checks. Model domain: a synthetic, simplified calculation requiring interpretation. Safety classification: non-operational.

Physical idea and engineering context

Balanced three-phase faults usually admit a positive-sequence network solution. Single-line-to-ground, line-to-line and double-line-to-ground faults require different connections of positive, negative and zero-sequence networks. Zero-sequence current depends on the physical winding and grounding configuration; an ungrounded delta blocks certain zero-sequence paths even if its positive-sequence impedance is low. Fault resistance and prefault operating point can materially alter observed current.

Governing model, boundaries and assumptions

For a single-line-to-ground fault: I_1=V_phase/(Z_1+Z_2+Z_0+3Z_f); I_fault=3I_1, with consistent phase units

Worked quantitative example

At an 11 kV bus, assume prefault line-to-neutral voltage 11/√3=6.351 kV; Z1=j1 Ω, Z2=j1 Ω, Z0=j4 Ω and zero fault impedance. The series denominator is j6 Ω, so |I1|=1.0585 kA and the ground-fault conductor current magnitude is 3.175 kA. This is rms phasor magnitude in a highly simplified purely reactive network.

Interpretation and limiting cases

The factor of three connects sequence current to conductor fault current only for the specific single-line-to-ground connection. Do not apply it to all fault types.

What could make this answer wrong?

Ignoring the winding vector group may invent a ground-return path. Using nominal line-to-line voltage directly as V_phase overestimates the example magnitude by √3.

Applied comparison and design reasoning

Compare the same positive-sequence reactance with resistance-grounded, solidly grounded and delta-isolated zero-sequence paths; identify which sequence data changed.

Guided problems

Problem A — first-principles check. Evaluate LG current magnitude for Vph=6 kV, Z1=Z2=j2 Ω, Z0=j6 Ω, Zf=0. Show the relationship used, a fully labeled unit conversion if needed, the computed result and an interpretive sentence.

Problem B — alternate assumption. Explain the effect of a transformer that blocks zero-sequence current in the examined path. State which terms in the equation change and which remain fixed. If the problem lacks a unique numerical answer, identify the missing measurements rather than guessing.

Problem C — critical reading. A model reports zero ground-fault current in one topology and very high current in another. Before changing relay pickup, which transformer and grounding records must be verified? Write a short technical response containing one quantitative or logical check, one serious counterargument, and one criterion that would falsify your initial conclusion.

§3.2 Overcurrent, directional and differential concepts

Unit 3, Lesson 2: Overcurrent, directional and differential concepts

Course position: Protection principles and fault studies. Study time: approximately 90–120 minutes including quantitative checks. Model domain: a synthetic, simplified calculation requiring interpretation. Safety classification: non-operational.

Physical idea and engineering context

An overcurrent relay observes current exceeding some criterion, potentially with an intentional delay. Selectivity requires a downstream protective zone to clear an internal problem while upstream zones retain backup responsibility. Directional elements use voltage-current reference quantities to distinguish forward and reverse conditions. Differential protection compares properly compensated zone-boundary currents; nonzero differential current can also arise from measurement saturation or transformer magnetization during energization.

Governing model, boundaries and assumptions

For a protected zone with currents referenced inward: I_diff = |sum(I_k,compensated)|; percentage restraint may normalize using through current

Worked quantitative example

Two measured compensated currents entering a zone are +500 A and −495 A during an external through-current condition: |I_diff|=5 A. For an internal event the measured values might instead be +500 A and +250 A giving 750 A differential. The exercise illustrates phasor algebra only; CT accuracy, harmonics and actual restraint logic cannot be ignored.

Interpretation and limiting cases

The current reference direction must be marked at every transformer terminal. An incorrect polarity or uncompensated transformer phase shift can mimic an internal fault.

What could make this answer wrong?

Current-transformer saturation during a high through-fault creates spurious differential current. A simplistic sum-with-zero-threshold relay model is not suitable for a protective device.

Applied comparison and design reasoning

A radial feeder adds embedded generation. Compare fault current arriving from the substation with current fed by the embedded source; a non-directional scheme may see a different coordination problem.

Guided problems

Problem A — first-principles check. For inward-referenced currents +100 A and −98 A, compute differential magnitude and explain why it is not zero. Show the relationship used, a fully labeled unit conversion if needed, the computed result and an interpretive sentence.

Problem B — alternate assumption. Give two non-internal-fault causes of apparent differential current. State which terms in the equation change and which remain fixed. If the problem lacks a unique numerical answer, identify the missing measurements rather than guessing.

Problem C — critical reading. Explain why shortening every relay delay to its minimum can reduce selectivity and increase outage area even when all relays are functioning. Write a short technical response containing one quantitative or logical check, one serious counterargument, and one criterion that would falsify your initial conclusion.

§3.3 Distance protection and apparent impedance

Unit 3, Lesson 3: Distance protection and apparent impedance

Course position: Protection principles and fault studies. Study time: approximately 90–120 minutes including quantitative checks. Model domain: a synthetic, simplified calculation requiring interpretation. Safety classification: non-operational.

Physical idea and engineering context

Distance relays infer an apparent impedance from locally measured voltage and current. In a simplified uniform line, measured impedance can correlate with distance to a fault, but real errors arise from arc resistance, mutual coupling, remote infeeds, fault loops and load encroachment. Zones are designed as overlapping protection responsibilities rather than literal distance rulers. Their operation must be coordinated with peer substations and communications.

Governing model, boundaries and assumptions

Z_app = V_loop/I_loop for the appropriate fault-type loop; for a uniform ideal line Z_to_fault≈m Z_line

Worked quantitative example

A single-phase equivalent has Z_line=6+j24 Ω over 100 km. An ideal bolted fault at 60 km presents 0.6×(6+j24)=3.6+j14.4 Ω from the near terminal, provided the chosen loop and sources permit the simplification. A remote source feeding an arc or a resistive fault can change Z_app without changing physical location.

Interpretation and limiting cases

A distance relay does not measure kilometres directly; the apparent impedance maps to a reach only through line parameters and fault-loop design. Phase-to-ground loops require zero-sequence compensation.

What could make this answer wrong?

Using a phase-to-phase loop for a ground fault or using a wrong residual compensation factor can misplace the inferred fault. The lesson examples must not be treated as reach settings.

Applied comparison and design reasoning

An added series-compensated capacitor changes the impedance trajectory during a fault. Describe which assumptions behind a constant-Z-per-kilometre map no longer apply.

Problem B — alternate assumption. Name two physical effects that distort the simple proportionality between impedance and distance. State which terms in the equation change and which remain fixed. If the problem lacks a unique numerical answer, identify the missing measurements rather than guessing.

Problem C — critical reading. What observations would distinguish a remote infeed effect from a data-entry error in line impedance when a measured reach appears too short? §3.4 Breaker duty and protection validation

Unit 3, Lesson 4: Breaker duty and protection validation

Course position: Protection principles and fault studies. Study time: approximately 90–120 minutes including quantitative checks. Model domain: a synthetic, simplified calculation requiring interpretation. Safety classification: non-operational.

Physical idea and engineering context

A relay can request interruption only if a breaker can withstand and interrupt the expected duty at the correct voltage and recovery characteristics. The first cycle can contain a DC offset and asymmetrical peaks even when the symmetrical rms fault current is modest. A protection study therefore uses more than a single rms current result. A commissioned scheme also needs end-to-end testing, logic verification and documented coordination.

Governing model, boundaries and assumptions

Illustrative RL fault current includes periodic term plus decaying offset: i(t) = i_ac(t) + I_DC,0 exp(−t/tau), where tau=L/R

Worked quantitative example

If a test network has L=20 mH and R=2 Ω, tau=L/R=0.010 s. An initial DC offset of 1000 A falls to approximately 368 A at 10 ms and 135 A at 20 ms. Real three-phase breaker duties and making currents depend on additional system and standard-specific quantities; the exponential alone does not determine a breaker rating.

Interpretation and limiting cases

The asymmetrical fault current depends on point-on-wave and network X/R. Never infer an interrupting rating from a toy first-order current offset.

What could make this answer wrong?

A breaker with an adequate nominal symmetrical-current marking can still require verification of duty, voltage, tripping supply, operations count and coordination.

Applied comparison and design reasoning

Compare a high-X/R transmission terminal with a lower-X/R distribution bus; discuss how offset decay and measurement saturation could differ.

Guided problems

Problem A — first-principles check. Compute an exponential offset at t=τ and 3τ as fractions of initial value. Show the relationship used, a fully labeled unit conversion if needed, the computed result and an interpretive sentence.

Problem B — alternate assumption. Explain why these fractions alone do not determine the breaker interruption duty. State which terms in the equation change and which remain fixed. If the problem lacks a unique numerical answer, identify the missing measurements rather than guessing.

Problem C — critical reading. Why does a relay trip signal not prove that fault current has ceased? Outline the separate roles of trip circuit, breaker contacts, arc interruption and confirmation. Write a short technical response containing one quantitative or logical check, one serious counterargument, and one criterion that would falsify your initial conclusion.

§3.7 System Dynamics and Grid Modeling: Unsymmetrical Fault Analysis: Line-to-Ground, Line-to-Line, and Double Line-to-Ground

Unit 3: Protection principles and fault studies

Fault studies estimate the currents and voltages that protection systems must recognize, but protection is about selective decisions, not just peak currents. Relay decisions interact with circuit-breaker interrupting capability, instrument-transformer behavior, grounding, sequence networks, communications and changing network topology. The mathematical examples are safe low-dimensional study cases; no example is an approved relay setting or field commissioning instruction. The unit distinguishes fault detection, discrimination, clearing and restoration and explains why changing inverter fault current challenges traditional assumptions.

Deep reading — interacting limitations

Sequence components allow one unbalanced event to be decomposed into three balanced mathematical sequences, but the impedances still describe physical winding and grounding arrangements. Zero-sequence does not flow through a transformer merely because a positive-sequence impedance was supplied. Relay protection is a communication and measurement chain with failure modes that include incorrect instrument polarity, transformer saturation, lost auxiliary power and misplaced protection zones. A correct short-circuit current number is only one input to an adequate study. Differential restraint, directionality, fault-loop selection, breaker clearing duty and backup coordination are separate concerns. For a new distributed generator, an apparently harmless increase in output may reshape the fault currents seen by existing devices. The appropriate classroom conclusion is to define the required studies, not to recommend relay settings.

Case synthesis: why a correct fault current can still support a wrong action

An analyst receives an 11 kV bus short-circuit report with three numerical entries: a three-phase symmetrical fault current, a single-line-to-ground fault current and an X/R ratio. Even if every current was computed correctly, this is not enough to approve a new protection scheme. The sequence-network connections depend on transformer vector groups, grounding impedance and source contributions. The point at which the report measures a current, the waveform interval and the CT configuration matter to how a relay interprets it. A remote source can feed the same fault from a direction not represented in a one-source radial equivalent.

Take a deliberately simple ground fault with phase voltage 6.35 kV, Z1=j1 Ω, Z2=j1 Ω and Z0=j4 Ω. If fault impedance is zero, the current magnitude is approximately 3×6.35/6=3.175 kA. Add a purely resistive fault impedance of 1 Ω and the sequence denominator becomes 3+j6 Ω; the magnitude falls to about 2.84 kA. A transformer connection that removes a zero-sequence path can change the equivalent dramatically. These calculations demonstrate model sensitivity; they do not provide a pickup setting, trip delay or interrupting rating. In practice, the current at a relay location may differ from total current at a fault if multiple sources contribute.

Selectivity adds a second problem. A downstream device might clear a local fault while an upstream device serves as delayed backup. A non-directional element installed when power always flowed outward may react unexpectedly when a distributed generator introduces reverse fault-current contributions. A differential element may be sensitive to current-transformer polarity, compensation and saturation during an external through-fault. Distance elements may measure a different apparent impedance because fault resistance and remote infeed alter the voltage-current ratio. Circuit breakers have their own interrupting and operating-duty limits separate from relay detection.

A useful verification package contains a one-line diagram with protection zones, grounding details, instrument ratios and classes, short-circuit currents at several fault locations, breaker duty data, manufacturer logic and a documented coordination study. Proposed operating changes also need witnessed testing and approval under the responsible authority’s rules. When any of these are missing, the honest conclusion is that the simple classroom fault study is informative but not an authorization to energize, switch or set a protective device.
