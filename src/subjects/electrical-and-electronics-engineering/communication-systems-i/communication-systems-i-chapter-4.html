§4.1 Theoretical Overview: Superheterodyne Receiver Architectures, Mixing, and Selectivity

Unit 4: Angle modulation, FM and phase modulation

Learning objective: Explain instantaneous phase and frequency and why constant-envelope signals can tolerate some amplitude variation.

Physical perspective

Frequency and phase modulation encode a message as an angle trajectory rather than a direct amplitude fluctuation. This changes the receiver architecture: discriminators and phase-locked loops estimate derivatives or phase errors, while amplitude normalization can reduce some types of envelope interference. FM is not immune to noise; threshold effects, oscillator instability and bandwidth constraints remain important.

Extended engineering case: how to reason from requirements

Angle modulation is a model of a changing phase trajectory. The instantaneous frequency formula is a derivative, and therefore exposes why high-frequency phase noise can be dangerous in a discriminator. The link between a sinusoidal modulating tone, frequency deviation and modulation index explains why larger deviation produces a greater number of significant FM sidebands. Constant transmitted amplitude is attractive for an efficient nonlinear power amplifier, but nonlinear hardware can still create spurious spectra if the phase trajectory or switching waveform is distorted. Amplitude limiting removes part of the received amplitude noise, not all the noise that affects the estimated phase.

A simulation uses an FM carrier as if it were perfectly generated. A real radio also has oscillator phase noise, Doppler shift and time-varying attenuation. If the receiver is operated near the sensitivity limit, phase unwrapping may occasionally make a full-cycle mistake; an FM discriminator then produces a severe impulse. Such threshold behavior contradicts naive linearized noise calculations. Receiver filtering can reduce those impulses at the expense of rejecting abrupt legitimate message variation. Pre-emphasis and deemphasis operate on the message frequency weighting, not on free-space path loss.

The central engineering question is not 'Is FM always better than AM?' but 'Which disturbance, bandwidth limit and circuit complexity matter in this link?' Compare same received power, same message bandwidth and same nominal transmitter restrictions. Under a primarily multiplicative amplitude disturbance, FM with limiting often has a clear advantage; under frequency instability or narrow spectrum allocation that advantage can shrink. Give the comparison at least one explicit assumption in each sentence rather than relying on broadcasting folklore.

Connections and study order

The next four lessons move from the simplest tractable model to a broader engineering treatment of this unit. Solve their worked cases separately first, then assemble the assumptions in a single coherent link design. For every step, distinguish data representing the actual message from a signal generated by the transceiver and from noise introduced by the environment or receiver. Use the model's limiting cases to decide whether parameters can be safely neglected. Finally, specify how any simulation could be validated through an independent analytic calculation and, if qualified laboratory access exists, through a supervised physical measurement.

§4.2 Instantaneous frequency and phase

Lesson 4.1 Instantaneous frequency and phase

Unit: 4 — Angle modulation, FM and phase modulation Core model: fi(t)=(1/2π)dθ(t)/dt; θ(t)=2πfct+2πkf∫m(t)dt Suggested study: derive the model, solve its worked case, compare one limiting case, then attempt the open questions.

What is being described?

A constant-amplitude angle-modulated waveform has instantaneous phase whose derivative gives instantaneous frequency. For FM the message controls that derivative; for PM the message controls a phase increment directly. A step in FM message generates a change in instantaneous frequency, whereas the same step in PM introduces a phase discontinuity in the ideal model. Smooth transitions in physical circuits constrain both. Instantaneous-frequency interpretations can be misleading near zero envelope or in multi-component signals because a single analytic phase may not represent an intuitive local tone.

With frequency sensitivity 5 kHz per volt and a +0.4 V constant message, an FM carrier shifts by +2 kHz while the message remains constant. For sinusoidal message, peak frequency deviation is kf times peak message voltage.

§4.3 FM modulation index and Carson bandwidth

Lesson 4.2 FM modulation index and Carson bandwidth

Unit: 4 — Angle modulation, FM and phase modulation Core model: β=Δf/fm; approximate B≈2(Δf+fm) for a single-tone FM planning estimate Suggested study: derive the model, solve its worked case, compare one limiting case, then attempt the open questions.

What is being described?

A sinusoidal frequency-modulating tone produces theoretically infinitely many sidebands with Bessel-function weights. For sufficiently small modulation index, first-order sidebands dominate; for larger index, many components matter. Carson’s rule is a practical approximate occupancy measure, not a sharp spectral band limit or a legal emission mask. Frequency deviation and message bandwidth must be defined independently: β for a single tone does not determine multitone occupancy without the message spectrum.

§4.4 FM demodulation and limiter-discriminator chains

Lesson 4.3 FM demodulation and limiter-discriminator chains

Unit: 4 — Angle modulation, FM and phase modulation Core model: demodulated message ∝ d/dt unwrap(arg(z(t))) after appropriate filtering Suggested study: derive the model, solve its worked case, compare one limiting case, then attempt the open questions.

What is being described?

An ideal FM receiver estimates the rate of change of phase, which is the message scaled by frequency sensitivity. Practical implementations may use slope detectors, quadrature detectors, frequency-to-voltage converters or phase-locked loops. A limiter suppresses some amplitude disturbance before frequency estimation, but abrupt amplitude fades can still cause large phase errors. Differentiation boosts high-frequency noise, motivating deemphasis and appropriate detection bandwidth. Digital quadrature receivers commonly compute phase differences between adjacent complex samples rather than unwrapping an entire noisy phase time series.

§4.5 FM noise, pre-emphasis and practical tradeoffs

Lesson 4.4 FM noise, pre-emphasis and practical tradeoffs

Unit: 4 — Angle modulation, FM and phase modulation Core model: post-detection FM noise increases with baseband frequency under ideal limiting assumptions Suggested study: derive the model, solve its worked case, compare one limiting case, then attempt the open questions.

What is being described?

Above the FM threshold, a high-SNR approximation can make demodulated noise grow with message frequency because frequency detection differentiates phase. Pre-emphasis boosts selected high-frequency message components before FM, while a complementary deemphasis network at the receiver reduces their relative noise. The network shapes must be paired and must not drive the transmitter beyond allowed peak deviation. Near weak-signal threshold, capture effects and rapidly changing phase lead to behavior not predicted by a simple high-SNR linear approximation.
