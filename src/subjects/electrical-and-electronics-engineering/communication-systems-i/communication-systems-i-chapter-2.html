§2.1 Theoretical Overview: Amplitude Modulation: DSB-FC, DSB-SC, SSB, and VSB Schemes

Unit 2: Signals, spectra, filters and equivalent baseband

Learning objective: Understand waveform descriptions in both time and frequency, and define a consistent one-sided or two-sided spectral convention.

Physical perspective

For every signal, distinguish instantaneous amplitude, average power and energy. Sinusoidal examples may be power signals with infinite total energy, while finite pulses are finite-energy waveforms. The Fourier transform is a coordinate change that exposes filtering and channel occupancy. A filter that changes waveform shape also changes interference and noise, so there is no universal bandwidth independent of the measurement and the desired error rate.

Extended engineering case: how to reason from requirements

The foundation of spectral reasoning is to ask what a display actually computes. A laboratory spectrum analyzer can report power in a resolution bandwidth, a digital FFT routine can report a complex DFT coefficient, and a communications simulation may report a continuous density estimate. Those three views are not interchangeable until window energy, reference impedance, sample rate and calibration are specified. A sinusoid between FFT bins leaks across nearby estimates even if the underlying signal contains only one frequency. Increasing FFT size by zero-padding interpolates the same finite-record spectrum; it does not create additional observed information or improve fundamental resolution without longer data. Window tapering lowers distant sidelobes while changing the main-lobe width and noise bandwidth.

The LTI viewpoint unifies several devices: a copper cable, an acoustic room, a front-end anti-alias filter and a matched receiver filter. Each can be described locally as a convolution if its parameters do not change during the measurement. Apply a known test pulse and compare the observed shape with the impulse-response prediction. Then apply two tones with different phase. If a high-frequency tone is attenuated, the receiver may boost it but cannot undo noise that has already been added after attenuation. If two delayed components cancel exactly at a frequency, inverting the channel is ill-conditioned. A robust design often accepts some residual distortion to avoid extreme noise enhancement. Complex baseband is most useful when translating this reasoning from a 100 MHz carrier to a slowly varying envelope, but the sign convention and units must remain consistent.

As a quality audit, write a three-column notebook with time-domain prediction, spectral prediction and simulation observation. A correct simulation should satisfy each independent description within numerical tolerance. For example, a time delay of 2 ms appears as a linearly increasing negative phase with frequency, but preserves magnitude for an ideal delay-only channel. An amplitude notch implies a cancellation mechanism and cannot be inferred from phase slope alone.

Connections and study order

The next four lessons move from the simplest tractable model to a broader engineering treatment of this unit. Solve their worked cases separately first, then assemble the assumptions in a single coherent link design. For every step, distinguish data representing the actual message from a signal generated by the transceiver and from noise introduced by the environment or receiver. Use the model's limiting cases to decide whether parameters can be safely neglected. Finally, specify how any simulation could be validated through an independent analytic calculation and, if qualified laboratory access exists, through a supervised physical measurement.

§2.2 Fourier transforms and spectral lines

Lesson 2.1 Fourier transforms and spectral lines

Unit: 2 — Signals, spectra, filters and equivalent baseband Core model: x(t)=A cos(2 pi f0 t) ↔ (A/2)[δ(f-f0)+δ(f+f0)] Suggested study: derive the model, solve its worked case, compare one limiting case, then attempt the open questions.

What is being described?

A single real sinusoid has conjugate-symmetric positive and negative Fourier components in the mathematical two-sided spectrum. Multiplying by a cosine produces shifted spectra; this is the mechanism behind upconversion and downconversion. Engineers sometimes show only a positive-frequency spectrum for a real radio signal, but the axis convention must be declared. For finite observation windows, tones appear as broadened peaks because multiplication by a time window convolves their ideal line spectrum with the window transform.

Derivation and interpretation

Begin by writing the input, output and channel reference planes for fourier transforms and spectral lines. The governing relationship in this lesson is x(t)=A cos(2 pi f0 t) ↔ (A/2)[δ(f-f0)+δ(f+f0)]. Read each symbol according to its definition in the accompanying discussion, not as a context-free calculator instruction. Where the formula uses decibels, convert back to linear power before adding contributions. Where it describes a spectrum, identify whether positive and negative frequencies, peak or RMS amplitudes and window normalization are being used. If a formula describes probability, specify the random variable and statistical model. These conditions are substantive mathematical requirements: using the right arithmetic on the wrong reference conditions does not produce a correct engineering result.

Worked engineering case

For a 1 kHz sine observed over 10 ms, exactly ten cycles fit in the window. With a coherent sampled record, an FFT bin may align with the tone. Moving to 1.05 kHz while keeping a 10 ms record creates spectral leakage across bins.

Failure mode and model boundary

FFT-bin amplitude is not a calibrated power spectral density unless normalization, sample rate and window equivalent noise bandwidth are handled.

Investigation and design transfer

Generate tones whose frequencies do and do not fit an integer number of cycles into a record. Compare rectangular and Hann window frequency responses.

Connection to the rest of the chain

A communication receiver does not experience this mechanism in isolation. Source statistics influence coding; waveform choice influences occupied bandwidth; filtering changes the noise entering the decision statistic; and synchronization transforms signal geometry into errors. When using the result from fourier transforms and spectral lines inside an end-to-end budget, name its upstream inputs and downstream consequences. Avoid adding an improvement twice: reducing noise bandwidth and improving SNR through a filter may describe the same physical change. Conversely, an unmodeled impairment can create a gap between a correct local calculation and actual delivered payload. A full design review therefore asks for both local correctness and end-to-end error or outage evidence.

§2.3 Sampling, aliasing and reconstruction

Lesson 2.2 Sampling, aliasing and reconstruction

Unit: 2 — Signals, spectra, filters and equivalent baseband Core model: fs>2B for a strictly bandlimited baseband signal with highest positive frequency B Suggested study: derive the model, solve its worked case, compare one limiting case, then attempt the open questions.

What is being described?

Sampling repeats a continuous-time spectrum every sampling-frequency interval. If the replicas overlap, distinct analog inputs produce identical sample sequences and cannot be uniquely reconstructed. In practice, analog anti-alias filters limit the energy before the ADC, while finite aperture and clock jitter cause other impairments. The Nyquist statement is a sufficient sampling condition for a precisely bandlimited baseband waveform; bandpass sampling has a different set of feasibility constraints.

Worked engineering case

A 7 kHz tone sampled at 10 ksample/s produces the same discrete-time samples as a suitably phased 3 kHz alias. The observed folded frequency is |7-10|=3 kHz. Merely interpolating samples cannot reveal which original analog tone was present.

Failure mode and model boundary

Do not confuse the Nyquist frequency fs/2 with the filter transition width. Real filters need a guard band, and high-amplitude out-of-band signals may overload the front end.

§2.4 Linear time-invariant channel and group delay

Lesson 2.3 Linear time-invariant channel and group delay

Unit: 2 — Signals, spectra, filters and equivalent baseband Core model: y(t)=h(t)*x(t); H(f)=∫h(t)e^(-j2πft)dt Suggested study: derive the model, solve its worked case, compare one limiting case, then attempt the open questions.

What is being described?

An LTI channel can attenuate, delay and disperse the constituent frequencies of an input. Its impulse response contains the same input–output information as its transfer function, provided the model assumptions hold. Constant delay corresponds to a phase slope linear in frequency. If phase is nonlinear, spectral components travel with different group delays and the waveform can spread, increasing intersymbol interference even when the amplitude response seems flat. A causal system cannot depend on future inputs, and a physically stable impulse response should be compatible with the assumed energy or input–output stability definition.

Worked engineering case

Two tones at 1 and 4 kHz pass through a channel with gains of 1 and 0.4 respectively. Even with zero added noise the output shape changes because high-frequency content is weaker. A subsequent equalizer can restore amplitude approximately while amplifying noise in the attenuated region.

Failure mode and model boundary

Zero-forcing equalization is not always optimal: a spectral null makes inversion arbitrarily noise-sensitive. A perfectly rectangular channel frequency response also implies a noncausal or infinite-duration idealization.

§2.5 Complex envelopes and I/Q representation

Lesson 2.4 Complex envelopes and I/Q representation

Unit: 2 — Signals, spectra, filters and equivalent baseband Core model: s_RF(t)=Re{u(t)e^(j2πfct)} with complex envelope u(t) Suggested study: derive the model, solve its worked case, compare one limiting case, then attempt the open questions.

What is being described?

A real passband radio signal can be represented through a complex envelope containing in-phase and quadrature parts. The complex representation is a mathematical convenience for analyzing amplitude and phase without numerically resolving every carrier cycle. If u=I+jQ, multiplying by a complex exponential rotates phase; time variation in phase creates frequency offsets. Practical I/Q modulators can have gain imbalance, quadrature error and DC offsets. Those impairments create image components, carrier leakage and biased constellation measurements.

These conditions are substantive mathematical requirements: using the right arithmetic on the wrong reference conditions does not produce a correct engineering result.

Worked engineering case

If u=0.6+j0.8, the magnitude is one and the envelope phase is atan2(0.8,0.6)≈53.13 degrees. A 90 degree rotation gives -0.8+j0.6. The physical passband waveform remains real after taking the real part of the modulated complex exponential.

Failure mode and model boundary

The sign convention for Q depends on whether an upconverter uses cos minus Q sin or cos plus Q sin. Never merge formulas with inconsistent conventions.
