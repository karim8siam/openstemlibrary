§3.1 Theoretical Overview: Angle Modulation: Frequency Modulation (FM), Phase Modulation (PM), and Carson Rule

Unit 3: Amplitude modulation and synchronous detection

Learning objective: Analyze conventional AM, suppressed-carrier DSB and SSB by tracking the spectrum and detector assumptions.

Physical perspective

Analog amplitude modulation illustrates the cost of carrying information over a bandpass channel. A carrier makes envelope detection simpler but consumes transmitted power that carries no independent message samples. Suppressing the carrier improves power allocation yet requires phase-coherent downconversion. A single-sideband signal saves occupied bandwidth at the cost of more elaborate generation and receiver tuning.

Extended engineering case: how to reason from requirements

Amplitude modulation is easiest to understand through conservation of spectral content. Multiplying a baseband message by a carrier produces upper and lower translated copies. Including an unmodulated carrier changes the power distribution and creates an envelope that a simple circuit can track. These alternatives involve different receiver assumptions. DSB-SC and SSB save carrier power but a coherent receiver must know the oscillator reference. Conventional AM is tolerant of phase uncertainty at the envelope detector but transmits a carrier even when the message is silent. In SSB the result of an oscillator frequency error is an audible pitch shift rather than merely a constant gain loss.

A field-service engineer might use an amplitude-modulated test beacon to probe a narrowband receiver. Before attributing a distorted recovered waveform to the air path, verify that the source modulation index did not exceed unity, the envelope detector RC time constant was appropriate and the measurement input did not overload. A detector with a capacitor too small follows high-frequency ripple, whereas a time constant too large cannot follow a rapidly decreasing envelope and causes diagonal clipping. The correct compromise depends on both carrier and maximum message frequency. A product detector has a different failure mode: carrier-phase error reduces the in-phase branch even if the envelope itself is perfectly formed.

The simulator in this unit deliberately isolates those failures. Its AM sweep should show an envelope crossing zero when the normalized single-tone modulation index exceeds one. Do not call a clipped trace a measurement of over-the-air broadcast quality. Add finite filter settling and a carrier-offset thought experiment to the lab record. When comparing occupied bandwidth, identify whether the message bandwidth refers to a one-sided positive-frequency interval or a two-sided baseband spectrum.

Connections and study order

The next four lessons move from the simplest tractable model to a broader engineering treatment of this unit. Solve their worked cases separately first, then assemble the assumptions in a single coherent link design. For every step, distinguish data representing the actual message from a signal generated by the transceiver and from noise introduced by the environment or receiver. Use the model's limiting cases to decide whether parameters can be safely neglected. Finally, specify how any simulation could be validated through an independent analytic calculation and, if qualified laboratory access exists, through a supervised physical measurement.

§3.2 Double-sideband suppressed-carrier modulation

Lesson 3.1 Double-sideband suppressed-carrier modulation

Unit: 3 — Amplitude modulation and synchronous detection Core model: s(t)=m(t)cos(2πfct) shifts M(f) to ±fc, each scaled by 1/2 Suggested study: derive the model, solve its worked case, compare one limiting case, then attempt the open questions.

What is being described?

Multiplication in time is convolution in frequency, so a baseband spectrum creates mirrored copies around positive and negative carrier centers. A coherent receiver multiplies by the correct local oscillator and filters away the doubled carrier terms. For this convention a gain factor of two may be required to recover the original message amplitude, depending on transmitter and receiver scaling. If transmitter and receiver oscillators differ, the recovered message undergoes a residual low-frequency rotation or beating.

Worked engineering case

For m(t)=cos(2π·1 kHz·t) and fc=100 kHz, the transmitted real signal contains components at 99 and 101 kHz. A 100 kHz product detector creates the original 1 kHz term plus high-frequency terms that the low-pass filter rejects.

Failure mode and model boundary

A passive low-pass filter cannot fix a phase error of exactly 90 degrees in a one-branch synchronous detector; the useful component can vanish.

§3.3 Conventional AM depth and envelope detection

Lesson 3.2 Conventional AM depth and envelope detection

Unit: 3 — Amplitude modulation and synchronous detection Core model: s(t)=Ac[1+μ m_norm(t)]cos(2πfct), |m_norm|≤1 Suggested study: derive the model, solve its worked case, compare one limiting case, then attempt the open questions.

What is being described?

When the normalized message peaks between minus one and plus one, a modulation index no greater than one keeps the envelope nonnegative. An envelope detector can then track the intended amplitude without knowing carrier phase, assuming its RC response is fast relative to message changes but slow relative to carrier cycles. Overmodulation reverses envelope sign and produces severe nonlinear distortion for a diode detector. For single-tone AM the sideband powers sum to a fraction μ²/2 of carrier power; this is a statement about the conventional ideal single-tone waveform.

Worked engineering case

At μ=0.8, total transmitted power relative to carrier-only power is 1+0.8²/2=1.32; the ideal sideband fraction of total power is 0.32/1.32≈24.24%.

§3.4 Single-sideband and analytic signals

Lesson 3.3 Single-sideband and analytic signals

Unit: 3 — Amplitude modulation and synchronous detection Core model: analytic m_a(t)=m(t)+j H{m(t)}; SSB=Re{m_a(t)e^(j2πfct)} Suggested study: derive the model, solve its worked case, compare one limiting case, then attempt the open questions.

What is being described?

A Hilbert-transform pair allows one sideband of a real modulation to cancel while the other is retained. In the frequency domain this is the analytic-signal construction: negative-frequency content of the complex baseband is suppressed and positive-frequency components are doubled under a standard convention. The final physical waveform remains real. A practical SSB receiver uses a beat-frequency oscillator and narrowband filtering; small frequency mismatch shifts every recovered audio component and may make a voice unnatural.

§3.5 Carrier recovery and phase-sensitive detector error

Lesson 3.4 Carrier recovery and phase-sensitive detector error

Unit: 3 — Amplitude modulation and synchronous detection Core model: low-pass{2m(t)cos(ωct)cos(ωct+φ)}=m(t)cosφ Suggested study: derive the model, solve its worked case, compare one limiting case, then attempt the open questions.

What is being described?

Coherent demodulation works by aligning a receiver-generated carrier with the transmitted signal. A constant phase difference reduces the recovered amplitude for a scalar DSB detector; time-varying phase introduces modulation artifacts and spectral spreading. An I/Q receiver retains two components and can estimate phase without depending on a single cosine branch. A phase-locked loop uses error feedback to track oscillator frequency and phase over a limited range and dynamic response.

Worked engineering case

At a fixed 60 degree phase error, the recovered amplitude of an ideal coherent DSB branch is cos60°=0.5 of its phase-aligned value. At 90 degrees it is ideally zero. A time-varying 1 Hz frequency mismatch causes the relative phase to drift by one cycle each second.

Failure mode and model boundary

A PLL is not an arbitrary repair tool: acquisition range, noise bandwidth, cycle slips and pull-in time matter, especially at weak SNR.
