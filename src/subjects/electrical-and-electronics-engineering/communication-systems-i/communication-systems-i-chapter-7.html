§7.1 Theoretical Overview: Pulse Code Modulation (PCM), Uniform and Non-Uniform Quantization (A-Law, Mu-Law)

Unit 7: Information limits, line coding and multiplexing

Learning objective: Apply Shannon capacity as a bound, not as a plug-in prediction of a real modem data rate.

Physical perspective

A communication link trades among rate, bandwidth, power, complexity, delay and error probability. Coding can approach ideal information limits under suitable blocklength and channel assumptions, but a finite modem has framing and implementation losses. Frequency-division and time-division sharing alter the resource scheduling problem rather than creating extra physical capacity.

Extended engineering case: how to reason from requirements

Information theory separates what is mathematically possible from what a particular modem can deliver. A source code exploits probability structure and correlation; its average lossless rate is bounded by entropy, not by the number of characters in the alphabet alone. A channel code deliberately adds redundancy to make noisy transmission more reliable. These directions seem opposite but solve different tasks, and a practical chain often compresses first and then adds protection. Compressing encrypted or already efficiently compressed data seldom yields meaningful savings. In contrast, protecting information with parity or block codes trades data rate for error resilience.

A classroom exercise may calculate AWGN capacity using bandwidth and received SNR. Do not mistake this for the achieved link rate. Finite block length, synchronization, pilots, modulation constraints, oscillator drift and fixed maximum delay prevent the theoretical limit from being reached exactly. In low-SNR operation, spreading signal energy over more bandwidth can reduce required energy per bit toward an information-theoretic limit, but receiver power consumption and occupied spectrum may be unacceptable. In high-SNR operation, capacity grows only logarithmically with transmit power: multiplying power by ten does not multiply data rate by ten.

Multiplexing adds scheduling and ownership. For TDM, a slot is useful only when user data, preambles and recovery overhead fit. FDM needs filters and oscillator spacing sufficiently good to control adjacent-channel interference. When users are bursty, an average utilization below one does not guarantee low delay: queues grow during activity peaks. Design a channel-use accounting sheet with separate columns for theoretical capacity, raw physical bit rate, coding overhead, framing overhead, retransmissions and user payload. The numerical gap between columns explains more than a single 'speed' label.

Connections and study order

The next four lessons move from the simplest tractable model to a broader engineering treatment of this unit. Solve their worked cases separately first, then assemble the assumptions in a single coherent link design. For every step, distinguish data representing the actual message from a signal generated by the transceiver and from noise introduced by the environment or receiver. Use the model's limiting cases to decide whether parameters can be safely neglected. Finally, specify how any simulation could be validated through an independent analytic calculation and, if qualified laboratory access exists, through a supervised physical measurement.

§7.2 Entropy, source redundancy and compression

Lesson 7.1 Entropy, source redundancy and compression

Unit: 7 — Information limits, line coding and multiplexing Core model: H(X)=-∑p(x)log2 p(x) Suggested study: derive the model, solve its worked case, compare one limiting case, then attempt the open questions.

What is being described?

Entropy measures the average uncertainty of a random source symbol under a specified distribution. A source with nonuniform probabilities may require fewer average coded bits than a fixed-length representation, at the expense of codebooks and sometimes delay. Lossless compression cannot reduce every possible input; some sequences become longer if no information is discarded. For correlated sources, the relevant compression opportunity depends on conditional entropy and the model of previous samples.

Derivation and interpretation

Begin by writing the input, output and channel reference planes for entropy, source redundancy and compression. The governing relationship in this lesson is H(X)=-∑p(x)log2 p(x). Read each symbol according to its definition in the accompanying discussion, not as a context-free calculator instruction. Where the formula uses decibels, convert back to linear power before adding contributions. Where it describes a spectrum, identify whether positive and negative frequencies, peak or RMS amplitudes and window normalization are being used. If a formula describes probability, specify the random variable and statistical model. These conditions are substantive mathematical requirements: using the right arithmetic on the wrong reference conditions does not produce a correct engineering result.

Worked engineering case

For independent symbols A and B with probabilities 0.75 and 0.25, H=-0.75log2(0.75)-0.25log2(0.25)≈0.811 bits per symbol. This is an asymptotic lower bound on average lossless rate under the model, not necessarily the length of an individual symbol codeword.

Failure mode and model boundary

An encoder achieving low average length for its training distribution may perform poorly when actual symbol probabilities change. The decoded source still needs exact symbol boundaries and context.

Investigation and design transfer

Create a four-symbol distribution and compute entropy. Propose a prefix code and compare average length with the entropy lower bound.

4. Engineering design: Create a four-symbol distribution and compute entropy. Propose a prefix code and compare average length with the entropy lower bound. Guidance: specify achievable measurements, independent variables, expected plots and a criterion for accepting or rejecting the design.

Connection to the rest of the chain

A communication receiver does not experience this mechanism in isolation. Source statistics influence coding; waveform choice influences occupied bandwidth; filtering changes the noise entering the decision statistic; and synchronization transforms signal geometry into errors. When using the result from entropy, source redundancy and compression inside an end-to-end budget, name its upstream inputs and downstream consequences. Avoid adding an improvement twice: reducing noise bandwidth and improving SNR through a filter may describe the same physical change. Conversely, an unmodeled impairment can create a gap between a correct local calculation and actual delivered payload. A full design review therefore asks for both local correctness and end-to-end error or outage evidence.

§7.3 Shannon–Hartley capacity and spectral efficiency

Lesson 7.2 Shannon–Hartley capacity and spectral efficiency

Unit: 7 — Information limits, line coding and multiplexing Core model: C=B log2(1+S/N) for ideal bandlimited AWGN model Suggested study: derive the model, solve its worked case, compare one limiting case, then attempt the open questions.

What is being described?

Capacity is the supremum of reliable communication rates under a specific channel and allowable coding assumptions. For an ideal AWGN channel with bandwidth B and signal-to-noise power ratio S/N, the Shannon–Hartley expression establishes a benchmark. A practical link operating below that result may still require substantial coding, long blocks, accurate channel estimates and efficient modulation. The formula does not account for all forms of impulsive interference, fading correlation, spectrum-mask rules or traffic overhead. Spectral efficiency is useful as bits per second per hertz at a named interface.

Worked engineering case

At B=1 MHz and SNR=15 dB (linear 31.62), the theoretical AWGN capacity is 1e6·log2(32.62)≈5.03 Mb/s. A 4 Mb/s payload may be feasible in principle, but cannot be guaranteed by this upper bound alone.

Capacity does not predict latency, packet error probability at short block lengths, or practical battery life. Those need separate models and measurement.

§7.4 Line coding, clock recovery and DC balance

Lesson 7.3 Line coding, clock recovery and DC balance

Unit: 7 — Information limits, line coding and multiplexing Core model: R_line=Rb times any coding overhead ratio; transition density affects timing Suggested study: derive the model, solve its worked case, compare one limiting case, then attempt the open questions.

What is being described?

NRZ signaling, Manchester coding, alternate-mark inversion and block codes map bits to physical line levels with different transition and spectral properties. A code with deliberate transitions can simplify clock recovery while increasing occupied signaling bandwidth. A DC-balanced code may be important over transformer-coupled channels. Scramblers randomize long patterns statistically but do not guarantee a transition in every fixed number of symbols unless combined with constraints. Physical link design must include symbol timing tolerance, eye opening and baseline wander.

Worked engineering case

If an 8b/10b line code maps every eight data bits to ten transmitted bits, a 1 Gb/s payload requires at least 1.25 Gbaud binary line signaling before other overhead. This is not a claim about a particular standard implementation.

An apparent eye pattern can look open at low noise yet fail when long run-length patterns move the receiver baseline. Test stressed data patterns, not only random ones.

§7.5 Multiplexing and separation of users

Lesson 7.4 Multiplexing and separation of users

Unit: 7 — Information limits, line coding and multiplexing Core model: TDM frame duration relates slot count, guard intervals and per-user allocation Suggested study: derive the model, solve its worked case, compare one limiting case, then attempt the open questions.

What is being described?

Time-division multiplexing assigns different slots to users while frequency-division multiplexing assigns distinct subbands. Both require boundaries that do not leak too much energy into adjacent allocations. Guard intervals, pulse-shaping filters, frequency stability and synchronization consume real resources. In statistical multiplexing, user activity and queueing become part of quality-of-service calculations. The useful aggregate throughput is generally smaller than the raw sum of nominal bit-clock rates once pilots, guard time and addressing are counted.

Worked engineering case

Four users each need 100 kb/s, and a synchronous TDM frame devotes 80% to data and 20% to guard and overhead. The physical link must carry at least 500 kb/s aggregate symbols or bits under an appropriate one-bit-per-symbol assumption.

Failure mode and model boundary
