§8.1 Foundational Principles & Curriculum Scope: Magnetic Field Measurements: Search Coils, Fluxmeters, and Hall-Effect Sensors

Unit 8 — Noise, selective detection and automated measurements

Why this unit matters. Distinguish interference from random noise; define a measurement protocol that can be repeated.

Application setting. Reproducible software-only studies of detection, averaging, command sequencing and reporting. The examples are synthetic, deliberately low-energy, and do not establish safe procedures for handling energized high-power systems.

Concept map

Broadband Johnson noise, 1/f noise, clock interference and coherent pickup have different signatures and countermeasures. Repeated observations can characterize short-term repeatability, but if noise is correlated the usual √N improvement from averaging is optimistic. Analyze a time series in both time and frequency domains before deciding whether to filter, shield or redesign the circuit.

A lock-in identifies the part of a signal coherent with a known reference; this is not the same as reconstructing every component in a waveform. Its bandwidth is set by filtering and integration time. Automation can consistently repeat commands, but state checks, metadata, calibration and error handling are needed before results are scientifically reproducible.

Five essential terms

  • PSD: Power spectral density per unit bandwidth.
  • Autocorrelation: Similarity of signal with delayed versions.
  • Lock-in: Synchronous measurement against reference.
  • SCPI: Common structured instrument-command language.
  • Provenance: Recorded origin and processing history of data.

Cross-lesson example: competing explanations

Assume two engineers report conflicting results for the same notional specimen. One engineer repeats a measurement ten times and obtains a very narrow cluster. The second uses another range, reference, sample timing or wiring topology and obtains a systematic difference. The first engineer's repeatability does not prove that the second engineer is wrong. In this unit, list the physical differences between instrument chains and predict which could create a reproducible offset or scale change. Identify one controlled experiment that would separate the causes without relying on a higher number of decimal places.

§8.2 Thermal noise and interference coupling

8.1 — Thermal noise and interference coupling

Unit 8: Noise, selective detection and automated measurements Level: undergraduate, with prerequisite circuit theory, calculus and introductory probability. Estimated study: 70–100 minutes, including the numeric investigation. Tools: calculator, spreadsheet or the original offline simulator in this book. Evidence: examples are synthetic unless explicitly identified otherwise.

A problem that a measurement must answer

Johnson–Nyquist noise is a thermal fluctuation whose mean-square voltage grows with resistance, temperature and noise bandwidth. Interference from mains fields or digital clocks is not the same phenomenon: it may be coherent, correlated and removable with topology or shielding. Filtering always trades bandwidth for response time.

The application for this unit is: Reproducible software-only studies of detection, averaging, command sequencing and reporting. The meaningful output is not necessarily the number displayed by a meter. First decide whether it is the unperturbed circuit voltage, a time average, a reference-scale resistance, a physical temperature or a confidence-qualified estimate. That decision determines the topology, calibration approach and acceptable model. In an instrumentation report it is better to leave a result undecided than to replace the unmeasured quantity with a convenient proxy without disclosure.

Mathematical model and physical assumptions

Central relation: For ideal resistor in a bandwidth B, v_noise,rms=√(4 k_B T R B).

Derivation strategy

Start by writing the quantity and its reference state as a sentence. Identify every input that is actually measured and every value that is assumed. If a parameter came from a manufacturer specification, label it as a specification rather than calling it an independent observation. Use the equation to map those inputs onto the desired output. For a laboratory record, store instrument mode and environmental conditions alongside the number. In this case the key input evidence is the reading or physical parameter introduced above, not the final calculated answer. An experimenter should separate constants taken from a definition, instrument parameters taken from a datasheet and conditions measured on the day of use. If an assumed parameter is poorly characterized, a small model residual does not make the resulting prediction precise. The uncertainty of a quantity calculated from a product, quotient or nonlinear relation must follow its sensitivity to each contributing input, not a simple count of display digits.

For instance, ideal resistance or sampling calculations disregard temperature-dependent and frequency-dependent behavior unless those are included explicitly.

Fully worked numerical case

At T=300 K, R=10 kΩ and B=1 kHz, v_noise,rms≈0.407 μV using k_B=1.380649×10⁻²³ J/K. This is a theoretical resistor-noise estimate, not total amplifier input noise.

Uncertainty, nonidealities and instrumentation bias

Amplifier current noise through source impedance and flicker noise can exceed this white-noise floor. A strong periodic 50/60 Hz pickup is not best modeled as independent Gaussian thermal noise. The first concern affects the credibility of the numerical parameter; the second suggests a rival explanation of the observed behavior. A useful uncertainty analysis therefore goes beyond listing a collection of percentages. It defines the output measurand, classifies major sources as additive or multiplicative, evaluates correlations when necessary, and identifies the sensitivity of the output to each one. An uncertainty budget should preserve documentary support for Type B inputs and raw observations for Type A estimates. If the model is an approximation, give a bounded validity statement in addition to any statistical interval.

For a data-analysis problem, change sample rate, observation length or independent reference while holding the physical input constant. §8.3 Averaging, autocorrelation and effective sample size

8.2 — Averaging, autocorrelation and effective sample size

Unit 8: Noise, selective detection and automated measurements Level: undergraduate, with prerequisite circuit theory, calculus and introductory probability. Estimated study: 70–100 minutes, including the numeric investigation. Tools: calculator, spreadsheet or the original offline simulator in this book. Evidence: examples are synthetic unless explicitly identified otherwise.

A problem that a measurement must answer

For independent identically distributed zero-mean noise, averaging N observations reduces standard deviation of their mean by √N. Correlated observations do not offer that reduction; their effective sample size can be much smaller than the count displayed in software. Drift is another form of time-dependent bias.

The application for this unit is: Reproducible software-only studies of detection, averaging, command sequencing and reporting. The meaningful output is not necessarily the number displayed by a meter. First decide whether it is the unperturbed circuit voltage, a time average, a reference-scale resistance, a physical temperature or a confidence-qualified estimate. That decision determines the topology, calibration approach and acceptable model. In an instrumentation report it is better to leave a result undecided than to replace the unmeasured quantity with a convenient proxy without disclosure.

Central relation: u(mean)=σ/√N only for independent observations; for lag-one AR(1), N_eff≈N(1−ρ)/(1+ρ) for large N.

For a loaded circuit, preserve current paths and terminal definitions; for a sampled signal, preserve sample timing and analog bandwidth; for a statistical model, preserve independence assumptions. 8.3 — Lock-in detection and synchronous demodulation

A problem that a measurement must answer

The in-phase and quadrature terms identify a component coherent with the reference while reducing broadband noise over a chosen bandwidth. Uncertainty, nonidealities and instrumentation bias

A passed automated test does not verify electrical safety, calibration validity or software scientific assumptions. A useful uncertainty analysis therefore goes beyond listing a collection of percentages. It defines the output measurand, classifies major sources as additive or multiplicative, evaluates correlations when necessary, and identifies the sensitivity of the output to each one.
