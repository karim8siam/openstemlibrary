§7.1 Foundational Principles & Curriculum Scope: Instrument Transformers: Current Transformers (CT), Potential Transformers (PT), and Phase Errors

Unit 7 — Sensors, signal conditioning and calibration

Why this unit matters. Translate strain, temperature, current and displacement into electrically measurable quantities.

Application setting. Build mathematical calibration curves before relying on a sensor as evidence. The examples are synthetic, deliberately low-energy, and do not establish safe procedures for handling energized high-power systems.

Concept map

A sensor is not identical to its electrical output. It is a chain from physical measurand to material response, transducer, excitation, wiring, amplifier and digitizer. Temperature, stress direction, spatial averaging and mounting create biases that cannot be removed by digital arithmetic alone. Calibration establishes a relation over a specified range rather than proving every physical mechanism understood.

A small sum of squared residuals is not enough. Test independent inputs, rising and falling trajectories, different environmental conditions and repeated runs. Compare interpolations against extrapolations. Hysteresis or creep means that the same instantaneous input can produce multiple outputs depending on history, defeating an unconditional one-to-one calibration curve.

Five essential terms

  • Gauge factor: Strain sensitivity of a resistive element.
  • RTD: Resistance temperature detector.
  • Hysteresis: History-dependent difference for same input.
  • Holdout: Independent calibration-validation sample.
  • Self-heating: Sensor temperature change from excitation power.

Cross-lesson example: competing explanations

Assume two engineers report conflicting results for the same notional specimen. One engineer repeats a measurement ten times and obtains a very narrow cluster. The second uses another range, reference, sample timing or wiring topology and obtains a systematic difference. The first engineer's repeatability does not prove that the second engineer is wrong. In this unit, list the physical differences between instrument chains and predict which could create a reproducible offset or scale change. Identify one controlled experiment that would separate the causes without relying on a higher number of decimal places.

§7.2 Strain gauges and bridge conditioning

7.1 — Strain gauges and bridge conditioning

Unit 7: Sensors, signal conditioning and calibration Level: undergraduate, with prerequisite circuit theory, calculus and introductory probability. Estimated study: 70–100 minutes, including the numeric investigation. Tools: calculator, spreadsheet or the original offline simulator in this book. Evidence: examples are synthetic unless explicitly identified otherwise.

A problem that a measurement must answer

A bonded strain gauge changes its resistance under deformation; gauge factor converts fractional length strain to fractional resistance. A Wheatstone quarter-bridge detects the small change but is sensitive to temperature, lead effects and excitation drift. Amplifier input offset may exceed the signal if the gain is chosen casually.

The application for this unit is: Build mathematical calibration curves before relying on a sensor as evidence. The meaningful output is not necessarily the number displayed by a meter. First decide whether it is the unperturbed circuit voltage, a time average, a reference-scale resistance, a physical temperature or a confidence-qualified estimate. That decision determines the topology, calibration approach and acceptable model. In an instrumentation report it is better to leave a result undecided than to replace the unmeasured quantity with a convenient proxy without disclosure.

Mathematical model and physical assumptions

Central relation: GF=(ΔR/R)/ε; for a small-change quarter-bridge |Vo|≈(Vs/4)GF|ε|.

Derivation strategy

Start by writing the quantity and its reference state as a sentence. Identify every input that is actually measured and every value that is assumed. If a parameter came from a manufacturer specification, label it as a specification rather than calling it an independent observation. Use the equation to map those inputs onto the desired output. For a laboratory record, store instrument mode and environmental conditions alongside the number. In this case the key input evidence is the reading or physical parameter introduced above, not the final calculated answer. An experimenter should separate constants taken from a definition, instrument parameters taken from a datasheet and conditions measured on the day of use. If an assumed parameter is poorly characterized, a small model residual does not make the resulting prediction precise. The uncertainty of a quantity calculated from a product, quotient or nonlinear relation must follow its sensitivity to each contributing input, not a simple count of display digits.

Why the relation is useful

Transfer functions of sensors A sensor is not identical to its electrical output. It is a chain from physical measurand to material response, transducer, excitation, wiring, amplifier and digitizer. Temperature, stress direction, spatial averaging and mounting create biases that cannot be removed by digital arithmetic alone. Calibration establishes a relation over a specified range rather than proving every physical mechanism understood. The formula above is one of the operational links between a physical system and a numerical record. It is not an endorsement of any particular manufacturer's ratings, which must be checked independently. For instance, ideal resistance or sampling calculations disregard temperature-dependent and frequency-dependent behavior unless those are included explicitly.

Fully worked numerical case

For Vs=5 V, GF=2 and ε=500 μstrain, output magnitude ≈5×2×500e−6/4=1.25 mV. A 2 mV amplifier offset would overshadow the intended signal without calibration.

Uncertainty, nonidealities and instrumentation bias

Nonuniform strain, adhesive creep, transverse sensitivity and thermal expansion affect physical interpretation. A stable output voltage can conceal mechanical creep if the calibration reference is unchanged. The first concern affects the credibility of the numerical parameter; the second suggests a rival explanation of the observed behavior. A useful uncertainty analysis therefore goes beyond listing a collection of percentages. It defines the output measurand, classifies major sources as additive or multiplicative, evaluates correlations when necessary, and identifies the sensitivity of the output to each one. An uncertainty budget should preserve documentary support for Type B inputs and raw observations for Type A estimates. If the model is an approximation, give a bounded validity statement in addition to any statistical interval.

A counterexample to overconfidence

Validation beyond regression A small sum of squared residuals is not enough. Test independent inputs, rising and falling trajectories, different environmental conditions and repeated runs. Compare interpolations against extrapolations. Hysteresis or creep means that the same instantaneous input can produce multiple outputs depending on history, defeating an unconditional one-to-one calibration curve. This contextual distinction provides a way to design a discriminating check. For an instrument-chain problem, change input range, source impedance, frequency or wiring to see whether the measurement follows the predicted dependence. For a data-analysis problem, change sample rate, observation length or independent reference while holding the physical input constant. If a conclusion persists only because the same assumption is reused in every run, that persistence supplies little additional evidence.

Engineering use and what would be required to trust it

Choose compensation gauges or full-bridge patterns when temperature and bending modes matter. Calibration records, environmental limits and independent checks belong in the method, not just in appendices. Choose an experiment that isolates the main influence while staying within authorized, properly rated equipment and low-energy pedagogical conditions. A formula computed with fictional values is evidence that arithmetic was performed, not that a real device passed a standard. When the output feeds a safety, certification or financial decision, the appropriate reviewer and agreed acceptance rule must be identified before collecting measurements.

§7.3 Temperature sensors: RTD and thermistor

7.2 — Temperature sensors: RTD and thermistor

Unit 7: Sensors, signal conditioning and calibration Level: undergraduate, with prerequisite circuit theory, calculus and introductory probability. Estimated study: 70–100 minutes, including the numeric investigation. Tools: calculator, spreadsheet or the original offline simulator in this book. A problem that a measurement must answer

A platinum RTD offers a relatively predictable resistance-versus-temperature relation over a specified range; a thermistor is more nonlinear but highly sensitive. Lead resistance is significant for RTDs, especially in two-wire arrangements. Self-heating produces a bias that depends on measurement current and thermal contact.

§7.5 Least-squares calibration, hysteresis and validation

7.4 — Least-squares calibration, hysteresis and validation

Unit 7: Sensors, signal conditioning and calibration Level: undergraduate, with prerequisite circuit theory, calculus and introductory probability. A problem that a measurement must answer

A calibration line should be fitted to a documented set of reference inputs and holdout measurements, not just two convenient endpoints. Least squares minimizes squared vertical residuals under its assumed error model; uncertainty in the reference axis may require errors-in-variables methods. Hysteresis reveals path dependence.

Uncertainty, nonidealities and instrumentation bias

A perfect in-sample fit can arise from too few points; calibration and validation must be separated. Correlation between x and y does not establish that all operating hysteresis or nonlinearities are absent. The first concern affects the credibility of the numerical parameter; the second suggests a rival explanation of the observed behavior. A useful uncertainty analysis therefore goes beyond listing a collection of percentages. It defines the output measurand, classifies major sources as additive or multiplicative, evaluates correlations when necessary, and identifies the sensitivity of the output to each one. An uncertainty budget should preserve documentary support for Type B inputs and raw observations for Type A estimates. If the model is an approximation, give a bounded validity statement in addition to any statistical interval.

A counterexample to overconfidence

Validation beyond regression A small sum of squared residuals is not enough. Test independent inputs, rising and falling trajectories, different environmental conditions and repeated runs. Compare interpolations against extrapolations. Hysteresis or creep means that the same instantaneous input can produce multiple outputs depending on history, defeating an unconditional one-to-one calibration curve. This contextual distinction provides a way to design a discriminating check. For an instrument-chain problem, change input range, source impedance, frequency or wiring to see whether the measurement follows the predicted dependence. For a data-analysis problem, change sample rate, observation length or independent reference while holding the physical input constant. If a conclusion persists only because the same assumption is reused in every run, that persistence supplies little additional evidence.
