§5.1 Foundational Principles & Curriculum Scope: Alternating-Current Impedance Bridges: Maxwell, Hay, Anderson, and Schering

Unit 5 — Oscilloscopes and waveform analysis

Why this unit matters. Separate probe behavior, instrument response, sample rate and trigger conditions from the underlying waveform.

Application setting. A recorded-waveform analysis; never attach ordinary grounded probes to a mains circuit. The examples are synthetic, deliberately low-energy, and do not establish safe procedures for handling energized high-power systems.

Concept map

A recorded waveform is the source filtered by the complete chain: probe, cable, coupling circuit, amplifier, anti-alias filter and digital display process. Bandwidth and gain flatness determine amplitudes; timing response determines edge shapes. The difference between sampled data and a rendered smooth trace is pedagogically important; interpolation is not a direct observation.

The DFT is a mathematical decomposition of a finite set of samples. It reports bin frequencies tied to the assumed sample rate. Windows alter amplitude scales and noise-floor interpretation, and short records obscure close tones. In frequency analysis, the correct metadata include sample rate, record duration, units, window type, normalization and whether the signal was already filtered.

Five essential terms

  • Bandwidth: Frequency interval meeting a transfer-response criterion.
  • Aliasing: Ambiguity introduced by insufficient sample rate.
  • Trigger: Condition aligning repeated records.
  • Leakage: Spectral spreading due to finite record.
  • Timebase: Clock governing sample timestamps.

Cross-lesson example: competing explanations

Assume two engineers report conflicting results for the same notional specimen. One engineer repeats a measurement ten times and obtains a very narrow cluster. The second uses another range, reference, sample timing or wiring topology and obtains a systematic difference. The first engineer's repeatability does not prove that the second engineer is wrong. In this unit, list the physical differences between instrument chains and predict which could create a reproducible offset or scale change. Identify one controlled experiment that would separate the causes without relying on a higher number of decimal places.

§5.2 Oscilloscope input bandwidth and rise time

5.1 — Oscilloscope input bandwidth and rise time

Unit 5: Oscilloscopes and waveform analysis Level: undergraduate, with prerequisite circuit theory, calculus and introductory probability. Estimated study: 70–100 minutes, including the numeric investigation. Tools: calculator, spreadsheet or the original offline simulator in this book. Evidence: examples are synthetic unless explicitly identified otherwise.

A problem that a measurement must answer

An oscilloscope measures a bandwidth-limited version of the signal. A first-order front-end approximation relates bandwidth and 10–90% rise time through a constant near 0.35. Probe and scope responses combine, so a fast input edge can look slow on an otherwise high-resolution screen.

The application for this unit is: A recorded-waveform analysis; never attach ordinary grounded probes to a mains circuit. The meaningful output is not necessarily the number displayed by a meter. First decide whether it is the unperturbed circuit voltage, a time average, a reference-scale resistance, a physical temperature or a confidence-qualified estimate. That decision determines the topology, calibration approach and acceptable model. In an instrumentation report it is better to leave a result undecided than to replace the unmeasured quantity with a convenient proxy without disclosure.

Mathematical model and physical assumptions

Central relation: For a single-pole approximation t_r≈0.35/BW; if independent Gaussian-like rises, t_meas²≈t_signal²+t_system².

Derivation strategy

Start by writing the quantity and its reference state as a sentence. Identify every input that is actually measured and every value that is assumed. If a parameter came from a manufacturer specification, label it as a specification rather than calling it an independent observation. Use the equation to map those inputs onto the desired output. For a laboratory record, store instrument mode and environmental conditions alongside the number. In this case the key input evidence is the reading or physical parameter introduced above, not the final calculated answer. An experimenter should separate constants taken from a definition, instrument parameters taken from a datasheet and conditions measured on the day of use. If an assumed parameter is poorly characterized, a small model residual does not make the resulting prediction precise. The uncertainty of a quantity calculated from a product, quotient or nonlinear relation must follow its sensitivity to each contributing input, not a simple count of display digits.

Signal chain bandwidth A recorded waveform is the source filtered by the complete chain: probe, cable, coupling circuit, amplifier, anti-alias filter and digital display process. Bandwidth and gain flatness determine amplitudes; timing response determines edge shapes. The difference between sampled data and a rendered smooth trace is pedagogically important; interpolation is not a direct observation. The formula above is one of the operational links between a physical system and a numerical record. It is not an endorsement of any particular manufacturer's ratings, which must be checked independently. For instance, ideal resistance or sampling calculations disregard temperature-dependent and frequency-dependent behavior unless those are included explicitly.

Fully worked numerical case

A nominal 100 MHz single-pole instrument has rise time about 3.5 ns; a 4.0 ns signal would appear at roughly sqrt(4²+3.5²)=5.32 ns under the quadratic approximation. Different filter shapes require different constants.

Uncertainty, nonidealities and instrumentation bias

Bandwidth is not sample rate; response flatness, ringing and probe compensation affect edge shapes. A slow observed rise can be a front-end artifact rather than a property of the source. The first concern affects the credibility of the numerical parameter; the second suggests a rival explanation of the observed behavior. A useful uncertainty analysis therefore goes beyond listing a collection of percentages. It defines the output measurand, classifies major sources as additive or multiplicative, evaluates correlations when necessary, and identifies the sensitivity of the output to each one. An uncertainty budget should preserve documentary support for Type B inputs and raw observations for Type A estimates. If the model is an approximation, give a bounded validity statement in addition to any statistical interval.

Windows alter amplitude scales and noise-floor interpretation, and short records obscure close tones. In frequency analysis, the correct metadata include sample rate, record duration, units, window type, normalization and whether the signal was already filtered. This contextual distinction provides a way to design a discriminating check. For an instrument-chain problem, change input range, source impedance, frequency or wiring to see whether the measurement follows the predicted dependence. For a data-analysis problem, change sample rate, observation length or independent reference while holding the physical input constant. §5.3 Sampling, aliasing and timebase

5.2 — Sampling, aliasing and timebase

Unit 5: Oscilloscopes and waveform analysis Level: undergraduate, with prerequisite circuit theory, calculus and introductory probability. Estimated study: 70–100 minutes, including the numeric investigation. Tools: calculator, spreadsheet or the original offline simulator in this book. Evidence: examples are synthetic unless explicitly identified otherwise.

A problem that a measurement must answer

Sampling replaces a continuous signal by values at distinct times. Frequencies above half the sample rate can alias into a lower apparent frequency, and a visually smooth interpolated trace can be deceptive. Coherent acquisition and record length affect the apparent spectral resolution.

The application for this unit is: A recorded-waveform analysis; never attach ordinary grounded probes to a mains circuit. The meaningful output is not necessarily the number displayed by a meter. First decide whether it is the unperturbed circuit voltage, a time average, a reference-scale resistance, a physical temperature or a confidence-qualified estimate. §5.4 Probes, grounding and differential signals

5.3 — Probes, grounding and differential signals

Unit 5: Oscilloscopes and waveform analysis Level: undergraduate, with prerequisite circuit theory, calculus and introductory probability. Estimated study: 70–100 minutes, including the numeric investigation. Tools: calculator, spreadsheet or the original offline simulator in this book. Evidence: examples are synthetic unless explicitly identified otherwise.

A problem that a measurement must answer

The probe can add capacitance that loads fast nodes, and ordinary bench scopes commonly bond the probe ground lead to protective earth. Attaching such a lead to an unisolated energized circuit can create a dangerous short. A differential probe is engineered to reject common-mode voltage within specified limits.

The application for this unit is: A recorded-waveform analysis; never attach ordinary grounded probes to a mains circuit. The meaningful output is not necessarily the number displayed by a meter. First decide whether it is the unperturbed circuit voltage, a time average, a reference-scale resistance, a physical temperature or a confidence-qualified estimate. That decision determines the topology, calibration approach and acceptable model. In an instrumentation report it is better to leave a result undecided than to replace the unmeasured quantity with a convenient proxy without disclosure.

Central relation: For a simple RC source, f_c≈1/(2π R_source C_probe); common-mode rejection ratio CMRR=20log10(A_diff/A_cm).

A standard uncertainty is not necessarily equal to a specified maximum tolerance; a rectangular bounded-error model, for example, converts half-width a into u=a/√3 only if that probability model is justified. In a multivariable equation, derivatives and covariance decide how input uncertainty propagates. §5.5 FFT windows, spectral leakage and noise floors

5.4 — FFT windows, spectral leakage and noise floors

Unit 5: Oscilloscopes and waveform analysis Level: undergraduate, with prerequisite circuit theory, calculus and introductory probability. Estimated study: 70–100 minutes, including the numeric investigation. Tools: calculator, spreadsheet or the original offline simulator in this book. Evidence: examples are synthetic unless explicitly identified otherwise.

A problem that a measurement must answer

A discrete Fourier transform assumes a periodically repeated record. A sinusoid that does not complete an integer number of cycles within the record spreads its energy across bins. Window functions trade main-lobe width against sidelobe suppression, and displayed noise floors depend on bin width.

The application for this unit is: A recorded-waveform analysis; never attach ordinary grounded probes to a mains circuit. The meaningful output is not necessarily the number displayed by a meter. First decide whether it is the unperturbed circuit voltage, a time average, a reference-scale resistance, a physical temperature or a confidence-qualified estimate. Central relation: Frequency-bin spacing Δf=f_s/N; frequency resolution also depends on window and observation duration.

Use both a numerical consistency check and a counterexample. Change one input to challenge sensitivity; change the topology or data-generating assumption to challenge validity. A result is only as robust as the least defensible assumption. When discussing acceptance, state a decision rule, uncertainty and consequences for an incorrect classification. Uncertainty, nonidealities and instrumentation bias

Zero-padding interpolates the spectrum but does not supply longer real-time observation or independent resolution. A large-looking spike near a bin edge may reflect window leakage rather than a second physical tone. The first concern affects the credibility of the numerical parameter; the second suggests a rival explanation of the observed behavior. A useful uncertainty analysis therefore goes beyond listing a collection of percentages. It defines the output measurand, classifies major sources as additive or multiplicative, evaluates correlations when necessary, and identifies the sensitivity of the output to each one.
