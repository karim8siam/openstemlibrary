§7.1 Feedback loop gain and sensitivity

Unit 7, Lesson 1: Feedback loop gain and sensitivity

Model: Acl=A/(1+Aβ); S=1/(1+Aβ) Outcome: Analyze the operating principle, calculate a reference case, identify the limit of the approximation, and plan an independent check.

Physical mechanism

Negative feedback makes closed-loop gain less sensitive to changes in forward gain when loop gain is large and the system is stable. A is the open-loop forward gain and β the feedback factor, with sign conventions chosen for negative feedback. Sensitivity describes fractional change and is not the same as noise suppression at all frequencies.

Draw the relevant circuit or band diagram and name the source of energy, the signal that controls the result, the return path and the reference directions. A model of an individual element must be used with conservation of current and voltage around the complete circuit. This distinction is critical because a device characteristic measured under one set of bias conditions may not hold with a different load. Describe which parameters are external controls and which are intrinsic or empirical device parameters. In feedback, bandwidth, stability and noise, an especially important issue is stability and uncertainty; make it explicit before calculating anything.

Derivation and calculation

Start from Acl=A/(1+Aβ); S=1/(1+Aβ). Explain the assumptions required to use the expression and distinguish DC current, incremental gain, instantaneous waveform, average power and RMS quantities wherever they arise. Write the associated Kirchhoff equations and translate all input data to compatible SI units. Verify that the final expression has the dimensions of the required output. If the expression is nonlinear, first identify the valid region; if it is an incremental result, identify the linearization point. An elegant equation is not proof that the circuit's bias or signal swing permits the result.

Worked example. For A=1000 and β=0.1, Acl≈9.901, versus the ideal high-loop-gain estimate 1/β=10.

Limitations and expected departures

This DC scalar equation says nothing about frequency-dependent phase margin or nonlinear saturation.

§7.2 Poles, Bode plots and phase margin

Unit 7, Lesson 2: Poles, Bode plots and phase margin

Model: H(s)=A0/(1+s/ωp) Outcome: Analyze the operating principle, calculate a reference case, identify the limit of the approximation, and plan an independent check.

Physical mechanism

A pole adds gain rolloff and phase lag. A real loop may have several poles and zeros, each changing phase around its characteristic frequency. The unity-loop-gain crossing is especially important because negative feedback becomes susceptible to oscillation if phase approaches −180 degrees. Distinguish open-loop response from signal-path closed-loop response.

§7.3 Compensation and step-response tradeoffs

Unit 7, Lesson 3: Compensation and step-response tradeoffs

Model: tr≈0.35/BW for a single-pole system Outcome: Analyze the operating principle, calculate a reference case, identify the limit of the approximation, and plan an independent check.

Physical mechanism

Compensation can deliberately reduce high-frequency open-loop gain to obtain a more stable closed-loop response. A dominant pole may yield smoother steps at the cost of speed. Overshoot, ringing and settling time depend on damping and additional poles, so rise-time heuristics have specific assumptions. Large-signal slew rate is a separate constraint from small-signal bandwidth.

§7.4 Resistor, shot and flicker noise

Unit 7, Lesson 4: Resistor, shot and flicker noise

Model: vn,rms=√(4kTRB) Outcome: Analyze the operating principle, calculate a reference case, identify the limit of the approximation, and plan an independent check.

Physical mechanism

A resistor at nonzero temperature contributes thermal voltage noise over bandwidth. Shot noise reflects discrete carrier transport through suitable junction currents, while flicker noise grows toward low frequencies in many devices. Noise is statistically fluctuating; a computed RMS value is not an absolute peak. Filtering decreases integrated noise but may also remove information.

§7.5 Precision analog measurement and grounding

Unit 7, Lesson 5: Precision analog measurement and grounding

Model: Verror≈Vos+Ib Rin (approximate) Outcome: Analyze the operating principle, calculate a reference case, identify the limit of the approximation, and plan an independent check.

Physical mechanism

Low-level sensing requires attention to offset, resistor mismatch, common-mode noise, cable pickup and ground loops. A tiny voltage error can dominate a sensor signal, and repeated measurements reduce random uncertainty differently from systematic drift. Differential measurement can reject some interference but only within the equipment input range and the common-mode rejection limits.
