§2.1 Half-wave rectification

Unit 2, Lesson 1: Half-wave rectification

Model: vo(t)≈max(0,Vp sin(ωt)-Vf) Outcome: Analyze the operating principle, calculate a reference case, identify the limit of the approximation, and plan an independent check.

Physical mechanism

A half-wave rectifier allows current through a load during one polarity of the input. In the constant-drop model it blocks the opposite half cycle; the mean output is positive, but the ripple is large. With a resistor load, current follows output voltage. Transformer isolation and source impedance influence peak current in the physical system.

Draw the relevant circuit or band diagram and name the source of energy, the signal that controls the result, the return path and the reference directions. A model of an individual element must be used with conservation of current and voltage around the complete circuit. This distinction is critical because a device characteristic measured under one set of bias conditions may not hold with a different load. Describe which parameters are external controls and which are intrinsic or empirical device parameters. In diodes, rectifiers and supply conditioning, an especially important issue is conduction interval; make it explicit before calculating anything.

Derivation and calculation

Start from vo(t)≈max(0,Vp sin(ωt)-Vf). Explain the assumptions required to use the expression and distinguish DC current, incremental gain, instantaneous waveform, average power and RMS quantities wherever they arise. Write the associated Kirchhoff equations and translate all input data to compatible SI units. Verify that the final expression has the dimensions of the required output. If the expression is nonlinear, first identify the valid region; if it is an incremental result, identify the linearization point. An elegant equation is not proof that the circuit's bias or signal swing permits the result.

Worked example. For an ideal 10 V peak sine without a diode drop, average half-wave output is Vp/π≈3.18 V. This average is not the same as RMS output, Vp/2=5 V.

Limitations and expected departures

A diode voltage drop destroys the closed-form ideal average, and capacitive loads change conduction intervals.

This is the boundary of the teaching model, not evidence that all equations are unreliable. Record whether an observed deviation is likely to arise from a different bias condition, instrument impedance or a real phenomenon the simple expression omits.

§2.2 Bridge rectification and ripple frequency

Unit 2, Lesson 2: Bridge rectification and ripple frequency

Model: vo(t)≈max(0,|Vp sin(ωt)|-2Vf) Outcome: Analyze the operating principle, calculate a reference case, identify the limit of the approximation, and plan an independent check.

Physical mechanism

A bridge reverses the negative half-cycle so that load current keeps one direction. Two diodes conduct in a typical bridge path, doubling the constant-drop approximation compared with a single series diode. A full-wave rectifier without smoothing has ripple at twice the source frequency. Adding a capacitor changes the problem to pulse charging and discharge.

§2.3 Reservoir capacitors and load ripple

Unit 2, Lesson 3: Reservoir capacitors and load ripple

Model: ΔV≈Iload/(fripple C) Outcome: Analyze the operating principle, calculate a reference case, identify the limit of the approximation, and plan an independent check.

Physical mechanism

A reservoir capacitor accepts short charging pulses near rectified peaks and delivers current during the rest of each cycle. The approximation assumes relatively small ripple and nearly constant load current. Larger capacitance lowers voltage ripple but increases charging pulse stress and can worsen inrush current. Ripple frequency differs between half-wave and full-wave supplies.

§2.4 Zener shunt regulation

Unit 2, Lesson 4: Zener shunt regulation

Model: Is=(Vin−Vz)/Rs; Iz=Is−IL Outcome: Analyze the operating principle, calculate a reference case, identify the limit of the approximation, and plan an independent check.

Physical mechanism

A shunt regulator uses a series current-limiting resistor and a breakdown-operated zener or reference device. When reverse current remains in the regulated region, changing load current is partly absorbed by changing zener current. A nominal zener voltage alone does not fix dynamic resistance, knee current or temperature drift. Always test both maximum and minimum input and load conditions.

§2.5 Linear regulators and power budgeting

Unit 2, Lesson 5: Linear regulators and power budgeting

Model: Pdiss≈(Vin−Vout)Iout Outcome: Analyze the operating principle, calculate a reference case, identify the limit of the approximation, and plan an independent check.

Physical mechanism

A series regulator actively adjusts its pass element so that output remains near the setpoint despite moderate input and load variation. Regulation fails when dropout headroom is insufficient. Thermal loss scales with input-output voltage difference and delivered load current. Input capacitors, output capacitance and equivalent series resistance may affect stability in regulator-specific ways.
