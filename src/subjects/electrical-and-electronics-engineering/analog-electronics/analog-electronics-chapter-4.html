§4.1 Incremental transconductance and hybrid-pi

Unit 4, Lesson 1: Incremental transconductance and hybrid-pi

Model: gm≈Ic/Vt; rπ≈β/gm Outcome: Analyze the operating principle, calculate a reference case, identify the limit of the approximation, and plan an independent check.

Physical mechanism

The hybrid-pi model linearizes a bipolar device at a valid forward-active bias point. Small-signal transconductance is the slope of Ic versus VBE, so increasing DC collector current raises gm. The base input resistance rπ scales with beta and inversely with gm. Linearization assumes signal excursions small enough that higher-order curvature is negligible.

A model of an individual element must be used with conservation of current and voltage around the complete circuit. This distinction is critical because a device characteristic measured under one set of bias conditions may not hold with a different load. Describe which parameters are external controls and which are intrinsic or empirical device parameters. In bipolar small-signal amplifiers, an especially important issue is signal integrity; make it explicit before calculating anything.

Derivation and calculation

Start from gm≈Ic/Vt; rπ≈β/gm. Explain the assumptions required to use the expression and distinguish DC current, incremental gain, instantaneous waveform, average power and RMS quantities wherever they arise. Write the associated Kirchhoff equations and translate all input data to compatible SI units. Verify that the final expression has the dimensions of the required output. If the expression is nonlinear, first identify the valid region; if it is an incremental result, identify the linearization point. An elegant equation is not proof that the circuit's bias or signal swing permits the result.

Worked example. At Ic=1 mA, Vt≈25.85 mV, gm≈38.7 mS. With β=100, rπ≈2.585 kΩ. A tenfold increase in Ic raises gm tenfold in the ideal model.

§4.2 Common-emitter gain and inversion

Unit 4, Lesson 2: Common-emitter gain and inversion

Model: Av≈−gm(Rc∥RL) Outcome: Analyze the operating principle, calculate a reference case, identify the limit of the approximation, and plan an independent check.

Physical mechanism

Collector current variations produce voltage changes of opposite sign across the collector load, creating voltage inversion. The common-emitter stage can exhibit large voltage gain, but gain only applies when the Q point can accommodate the signal. A finite signal source resistance reduces actual input-to-output gain. Output resistance and transistor capacitances matter once frequency rises.

§4.3 Emitter degeneration and local feedback

Unit 4, Lesson 3: Emitter degeneration and local feedback

Model: Av≈−gm RL/(1+gm Re) Outcome: Analyze the operating principle, calculate a reference case, identify the limit of the approximation, and plan an independent check.

Physical mechanism

An unbypassed emitter resistor turns a rising emitter current into a rising emitter voltage, reducing incremental VBE for a given base drive. This sacrifices gain while improving linearity and making gain less dependent on gm. The same resistor raises effective input resistance. The model is approximate when base current and output resistance are important.

§4.4 Coupling, bypass capacitors and low cutoff

Unit 4, Lesson 4: Coupling, bypass capacitors and low cutoff

Model: fc≈1/(2πReq C) Outcome: Analyze the operating principle, calculate a reference case, identify the limit of the approximation, and plan an independent check.

Physical mechanism

A coupling capacitor transfers a varying signal while blocking different DC bias levels. Together with surrounding resistances, it creates a high-pass pole. A bypass capacitor changes emitter degeneration in a frequency-dependent way. In multistage circuits, several nearby poles can make the overall low-frequency attenuation steeper than a single first-order estimate.

§4.5 High-frequency response and Miller effect

Unit 4, Lesson 5: High-frequency response and Miller effect

Model: Cin≈Cπ+Cμ(1−Av) Outcome: Analyze the operating principle, calculate a reference case, identify the limit of the approximation, and plan an independent check.

Physical mechanism

Inverting gain can magnify the effect of a feedback capacitance between input and output. The Miller estimate refers the bridging capacitance to the input under a chosen gain assumption. As frequency changes, the gain itself changes, so applying a constant midband gain far beyond bandwidth can be misleading. Parasitic layout capacitance contributes even if the schematic omits it.
