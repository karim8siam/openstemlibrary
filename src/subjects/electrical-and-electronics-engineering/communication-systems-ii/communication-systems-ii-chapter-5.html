§5.1 Theoretical Overview: Information Measure, Source Entropy, Shannon-Fano, and Huffman Source Coding

Unit 5 — Fading, diversity and spatial communication

Why this unit exists

The unit centers on mobile vehicle-to-infrastructure channel, where a moving vehicle exchanges sensor messages while surrounding reflectors change. Its controlling difficulty is that coverage holes and packet deadlines matter more than average SNR alone. The four lessons isolate different parts of the mechanism, yet the delivered system must satisfy all of them at once. You will link equations to implementation, design measurements and deliberately test situations in which a convenient idealization becomes misleading.

Concept sequence

Lesson 5.1 — Rayleigh fading statistics and outage

Fast and slow fading describe variation relative to packet or symbol duration, not simply 'bad' and 'good' links. In a flat Rayleigh channel, the envelope has a Rayleigh distribution when many independent scattered contributions combine without a deterministic dominant component. Instantaneous SNR fluctuates with squared envelope, and rate adaptation may fail when fades are deeper than the coding/interleaving can handle. Average SNR alone cannot predict reliability; outage probability relates the supported rate to the distribution of channel state.

Key relation: For normalized Rayleigh flat fading, G=|h|² is exponential with mean 1; outage at threshold γ_th with mean SNR γ_bar is P_out=1-exp(-γ_th/γ_bar).

Design boundary: Shadow fading, spatial correlation and a line-of-sight component change the distribution. A Rayleigh formula cannot describe all propagation environments or a site-specific coverage map.

Lesson 5.2 — Time, frequency and spatial diversity

Diversity places multiple independently faded observations of the same information at the receiver. Time diversity needs a channel that changes sufficiently across repeats or interleaved symbols; frequency diversity needs independently varying spectral regions; antenna diversity needs sufficiently independent spatial channels. Maximum-ratio combining weights each branch by its channel conjugate and noise level, producing combined SNR equal to the sum of branch SNRs in the independent-noise idealization. Selection combining is simpler and often less effective than coherent combining.

Key relation: For independent exponentially distributed branch SNRs, selection combining of M equal-mean branches yields P_out=(1-exp(-γ_th/γ_bar))^M.

Design boundary: Antenna spacing does not guarantee zero correlation, especially with narrow angular spread or nearby coupling. Noise covariance matters when forming optimal linear combiners.

Lesson 5.3 — Space-time block coding and Alamouti structure

The Alamouti construction provides transmit diversity without requiring two independent data streams at the same instant. Its two complex symbols occupy two time intervals, so the orthogonal design has code rate one symbol per use for the two-antenna idealization. The receiver combines conjugated observations using channel estimates, separating the two symbols when the channel is sufficiently constant across both intervals. Training and receiver noise affect detection. Space-time coding should not be confused with spatial multiplexing, which transmits independent information streams and demands sufficient channel rank.

Key relation: A two-transmit one-receive Alamouti block sends [[s1,s2],[-s2,s1]] across two time slots under a channel constant over the block, enabling linear orthogonal combining.

Design boundary: If the channel changes between time slots, the textbook orthogonality cancellation no longer holds exactly. Antenna power normalization must also be stated when comparing with one-transmit operation.

Lesson 5.4 — MIMO capacity and rank limitations

Multiple antennas can create spatial diversity, multiplexing gain or a beamforming advantage depending on channel rank, CSI and power allocation. A large antenna count does not guarantee equally many independent streams: channels aligned in spatial response can be nearly rank deficient. Singular-value decomposition exposes orthogonal spatial modes with different strengths. The log determinant capacity formula assumes Gaussian signaling and known channel at the receiver; practical coding, channel estimation and control traffic reduce deliverable throughput.

Key relation: For Gaussian MIMO with channel H and receiver noise covariance I, C=log2 det(I+(ρ/Nt)HHᴴ) bits/s/Hz when transmit power is equally allocated over Nt antennas and ρ is total linear SNR.

Design boundary: Capacity is not the modulation order or an achievable payload rate without coding and overhead. A nearly singular channel can make naive spatial multiplexing extremely error-prone.

Integrated case study

An engineer reviews the architecture for mobile vehicle-to-infrastructure channel. The first review identifies the performance or integrity quantity at the receiver. The second asks which subsystem can change that quantity. The third checks the impact on user payload, energy consumption, delay and operating cost. A favorable conclusion requires comparable conditions; changing both the channel model and signal power while comparing decoders makes it impossible to attribute improvement to one change.

Three-stage assessment plan

1. Analytical baseline. Derive a transparent relation using one of the four listed key models. Describe the allowed parameter region, units and what was held constant in the comparison. For mobile vehicle-to-infrastructure channel, decide which model variable is realistically measurable and which must be inferred.

2. Synthetic controlled test. Isolate one impairment, vary its magnitude and compare to the baseline. Re-run with a different seed and a second numerical implementation. Build a plot with labeled axes and, for error probabilities, uncertainty intervals.

3. Engineering hand-off. Replace the idealized assumption with a more plausible impairment relevant to a moving vehicle exchanges sensor messages while surrounding reflectors change. State which earlier conclusion remains reliable and which needs further data. Record what must be checked before a prototype is trusted outside the laboratory.

  • Identify a numerical sanity check, an implementation test, and a separate real-world validation requirement.

The purpose of this lesson is to connect mimo capacity and rank limitations with observable decisions in this chain.

Physical or algorithmic explanation

Multiple antennas can create spatial diversity, multiplexing gain or a beamforming advantage depending on channel rank, CSI and power allocation. A large antenna count does not guarantee equally many independent streams: channels aligned in spatial response can be nearly rank deficient. Singular-value decomposition exposes orthogonal spatial modes with different strengths. The log determinant capacity formula assumes Gaussian signaling and known channel at the receiver; practical coding, channel estimation and control traffic reduce deliverable throughput.

Boundary conditions and common mistakes

Capacity is not the modulation order or an achievable payload rate without coding and overhead. A nearly singular channel can make naive spatial multiplexing extremely error-prone.

An additional failure mode in mobile vehicle-to-infrastructure channel arises when the local mechanism is interpreted as an end-to-end guarantee. For example, packets may be rejected by framing or replay checks even after an error-correcting decoder outputs a plausible bit sequence; conversely, a successful frame may be too late to serve a time-critical application.
