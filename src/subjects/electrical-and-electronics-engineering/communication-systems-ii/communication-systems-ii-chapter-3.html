§3.1 Theoretical Overview: M-ary Passband Modulation: QPSK, M-PSK, M-QAM, and Constellation Geometry

Unit 3 — Receiver estimation and equalization

Why this unit exists

The unit centers on urban wireless receiver, where a moving receiver crosses an area with multipath echoes and oscillator drift. Its controlling difficulty is that the receiver has limited pilot resources and cannot assume a stationary channel. The four lessons isolate different parts of the mechanism, yet the delivered system must satisfy all of them at once. You will link equations to implementation, design measurements and deliberately test situations in which a convenient idealization becomes misleading.

Concept sequence

Lesson 3.1 — Matched filters and ML/MAP decisions

The sufficient statistic for a known pulse in white Gaussian noise is the inner product of the received waveform with that pulse, scaled appropriately. Correlator and matched-filter receivers are mathematically equivalent under the same time-reversal convention. Maximum-likelihood detection selects the symbol maximizing the conditional observation density. Maximum a posteriori detection also incorporates prior symbols and may use a different boundary when symbols are unequally likely. Matched filtering is not automatically optimal when noise is colored or pulse timing is uncertain.

Key relation: For deterministic signal s in AWGN with covariance N0/2 per real dimension, a matched filter maximizes output sample SNR for a specified sampling instant.

Design boundary: A receiver can improve total decision accuracy by using unequal priors while becoming less fair to the rare event. State the cost function before calling the threshold 'optimal'.

Lesson 3.2 — Channels with memory and ISI equalizers

A channel with several resolvable delays spreads each transmitted symbol into neighboring symbol intervals. A zero-forcing equalizer reverses the channel response where possible but can dramatically amplify noise near spectral nulls. A minimum-mean-square-error equalizer trades residual ISI for reduced noise enhancement. Decision-feedback equalizers subtract reconstructed interference from previously detected symbols, often reducing noise amplification while risking error propagation. Tap delay and channel timing must align with the sample grid.

Key relation: A discrete-time channel y[k]=sum_l h[l]x[k-l]+n[k] creates intersymbol interference; a finite-tap linear equalizer attempts to invert or regularize this convolution.

Design boundary: A frequency zero within the signaling band makes exact stable inversion problematic. An equalizer cannot restore information lost below the noise floor without paying an SNR penalty.

Lesson 3.3 — Pilot-based channel estimates and LMS adaptation

Pilots provide a reference known to the receiver and let it infer gain, phase or frequency-selective channel response. More pilots can improve estimates but reduce net payload throughput. An adaptive least-mean-squares filter updates coefficients using the instantaneous error e[k] times the conjugated input vector, following the chosen complex-gradient convention. Its step size controls convergence speed against steady-state misadjustment. Training on detected data after the pilot period works only if decisions are sufficiently reliable.

Key relation: With observed y[k]≈h x[k]+n[k], a scalar pilot least-squares estimate is h_hat=sum x*[k]y[k] / sum |x[k]|^2.

Design boundary: A small training residual does not validate generalization across time, carrier frequency or device temperature. Using pilots that are not actually orthogonal can bias a multichannel estimate.

Lesson 3.4 — Carrier recovery and phase-locked estimation

Coherent demodulation must establish reference phase and often estimate frequency offset before timing and symbol decisions become reliable. A simple phase-tracking loop compares a received pilot or decision-directed symbol to an expected phase, filters its error and updates an oscillator estimate. The loop bandwidth trades tracking speed against noise sensitivity. Phase ambiguity in square-QAM or PSK can require known symbols or differential coding. The carrier loop may interact with equalizer adaptation and is not an isolated block.

Key relation: An oscillator mismatch introduces phase theta[k]=theta0+2piDelta_fkT_s, rotating complex baseband symbols as exp(j theta[k]).

Design boundary: Arctangent phase detectors wrap at ±π; direct unwrapping can fail near deep fades. A loop that tracks a noisy data decision may lock to a wrong stable phase.

Integrated case study

An engineer reviews the architecture for urban wireless receiver. The first review identifies the performance or integrity quantity at the receiver. The second asks which subsystem can change that quantity. The third checks the impact on user payload, energy consumption, delay and operating cost. A favorable conclusion requires comparable conditions; changing both the channel model and signal power while comparing decoders makes it impossible to attribute improvement to one change.

Three-stage assessment plan

1. Analytical baseline. Derive a transparent relation using one of the four listed key models. Describe the allowed parameter region, units and what was held constant in the comparison. For urban wireless receiver, decide which model variable is realistically measurable and which must be inferred.

2. Synthetic controlled test. Isolate one impairment, vary its magnitude and compare to the baseline. Re-run with a different seed and a second numerical implementation. Build a plot with labeled axes and, for error probabilities, uncertainty intervals.

3. Engineering hand-off. Replace the idealized assumption with a more plausible impairment relevant to a moving receiver crosses an area with multipath echoes and oscillator drift. State which earlier conclusion remains reliable and which needs further data. Record what must be checked before a prototype is trusted outside the laboratory.

Unit questions

  • Which of the four mechanisms is most likely to control acceptance for urban wireless receiver, and what measured evidence would establish that claim?
  • How would the design fail if the assumption “A receiver can improve total decision accuracy by using unequal priors while becoming less fair to the rare event. State the cost function before calling the threshold 'optimal'.” were ignored?
  • Identify a numerical sanity check, an implementation test, and a separate real-world validation requirement.
  • Where would additional redundancy help and where might it merely increase latency or occupy already-limited bandwidth?

Coherent demodulation must establish reference phase and often estimate frequency offset before timing and symbol decisions become reliable. A simple phase-tracking loop compares a received pilot or decision-directed symbol to an expected phase, filters its error and updates an oscillator estimate. The loop bandwidth trades tracking speed against noise sensitivity. Phase ambiguity in square-QAM or PSK can require known symbols or differential coding. The carrier loop may interact with equalizer adaptation and is not an isolated block.
