§4.1 Theoretical Overview: Non-Coherent Detection, Differential Phase Shift Keying (DPSK), and Continuous-Phase FSK

Unit 4 — Multicarrier and OFDM systems

Why this unit exists

The unit centers on multicarrier campus backhaul, where a local network uses multicarrier transmission between fixed rooftop endpoints. Its controlling difficulty is that amplifier efficiency, reflected paths and timing synchronization affect both capacity and interference. The four lessons isolate different parts of the mechanism, yet the delivered system must satisfy all of them at once. You will link equations to implementation, design measurements and deliberately test situations in which a convenient idealization becomes misleading.

Concept sequence

Lesson 4.1 — DFT subcarriers and useful symbol duration

Orthogonality comes from integrating distinct complex exponentials over a full useful OFDM symbol. With a synchronized FFT window and a channel that is approximately constant during the symbol, each subcarrier can be equalized with a complex coefficient. The useful duration and sampling rate determine the tone grid. Guard or unused subcarriers can shape emission spectra, but they reduce the fraction of digital bandwidth carrying payload. A transmitter must establish DFT normalization so that computed average power agrees with its implementation.

Key relation: For N-point OFDM over useful duration T_u, subcarrier spacing Δf=1/T_u; the time-domain symbol is an inverse DFT of complex subcarrier values.

Design boundary: Subcarrier orthogonality is destroyed by significant synchronization error, rapid time variation or inadequate guard interval. A finite FFT grid does not guarantee a compliant continuous-time emission mask.

Lesson 4.2 — Cyclic prefix, delay spread and equalization

An OFDM receiver discards the prefix and applies an FFT. If the guard interval covers the significant impulse response and the received window is placed correctly, a frequency-selective time-invariant channel becomes N approximately independent one-tap gains. The cyclic prefix does not add new information; it reduces useful symbol efficiency. Extending it can tolerate longer delay spread but makes fewer bits per second available at fixed sample rate. Extremely small subcarrier spacing can reduce multipath difficulty while increasing sensitivity to oscillator offset and time selectivity.

Key relation: Appending a cyclic prefix of L samples to an N-sample OFDM block converts a channel of length ≤L+1 taps into a circular convolution over the retained N samples when timing is correct.

Design boundary: A longer cyclic prefix cannot solve very rapid Doppler changes during a single FFT window. It also does not correct phase noise or interference from adjacent transmitters.

Lesson 4.3 — Frequency offset and intercarrier interference

Frequency mismatch between transmitter and receiver creates a linearly rotating phase across the FFT observation. A common phase error affects all subcarriers, while noninteger normalized offset also breaks their mutual orthogonality and creates intercarrier interference. Timing offsets within the usable cyclic prefix often introduce a deterministic phase ramp across frequency rather than immediately corrupting orthogonality. Pilot-aided compensation can estimate residual phase, but large offsets generally require coarse acquisition before fine tracking.

Key relation: For normalized carrier offset ε=Δf_off/Δf_sub, each received OFDM tone leaks energy into other bins; desired-tone magnitude is approximately |sin(π ε)/(N sin(π ε/N))| for an N-sample rectangular FFT window.

Design boundary: The sinc approximation is for the rectangular window and idealized single-offset model. Phase noise spreads energy differently and cannot be modeled by replacing it with a single constant frequency error.

Lesson 4.4 — PAPR, amplifier clipping and EVM

A multicarrier signal combines many subcarrier phases, sometimes producing peaks far above average power. A power amplifier needs enough linear operating range to preserve constellation geometry near those peaks. Backoff reduces efficiency, while aggressive clipping increases error vector magnitude and spectral spreading. Digital predistortion can compensate a characterized amplifier within its usable memory and bandwidth limits; it does not make an arbitrarily saturated stage linear. PAPR statistics depend on subcarrier assignments, oversampling and measurement interval.

Key relation: For discrete samples x[n], PAPR=max_n |x[n]|² / mean_n |x[n]|²; clipping a multicarrier waveform adds in-band distortion and out-of-band regrowth.

Design boundary: An FFT at the symbol sampling grid may miss continuous-time peaks. Compare oversampled PAPR estimates before claiming that a waveform meets amplifier specifications.

Integrated case study

An engineer reviews the architecture for multicarrier campus backhaul. The first review identifies the performance or integrity quantity at the receiver. The second asks which subsystem can change that quantity. The third checks the impact on user payload, energy consumption, delay and operating cost. A favorable conclusion requires comparable conditions; changing both the channel model and signal power while comparing decoders makes it impossible to attribute improvement to one change.

Three-stage assessment plan

1. Analytical baseline. Derive a transparent relation using one of the four listed key models. Describe the allowed parameter region, units and what was held constant in the comparison. For multicarrier campus backhaul, decide which model variable is realistically measurable and which must be inferred.

2. Synthetic controlled test. Isolate one impairment, vary its magnitude and compare to the baseline. Re-run with a different seed and a second numerical implementation. Build a plot with labeled axes and, for error probabilities, uncertainty intervals.

3. Engineering hand-off. Replace the idealized assumption with a more plausible impairment relevant to a local network uses multicarrier transmission between fixed rooftop endpoints. State which earlier conclusion remains reliable and which needs further data. Record what must be checked before a prototype is trusted outside the laboratory.

Unit questions

  • Which of the four mechanisms is most likely to control acceptance for multicarrier campus backhaul, and what measured evidence would establish that claim?
  • How would the design fail if the assumption “Subcarrier orthogonality is destroyed by significant synchronization error, rapid time variation or inadequate guard interval. A finite FFT grid does not guarantee a compliant continuous-time emission mask.” were ignored?
  • Identify a numerical sanity check, an implementation test, and a separate real-world validation requirement.
  • Where would additional redundancy help and where might it merely increase latency or occupy already-limited bandwidth?

A quantity measured at the transmitter cannot automatically be used as if it were the receive-filter output, and uncoded symbol reliability cannot be silently treated as packet reliability. The purpose of this lesson is to connect papr, amplifier clipping and evm with observable decisions in this chain.

Physical or algorithmic explanation

A multicarrier signal combines many subcarrier phases, sometimes producing peaks far above average power. A power amplifier needs enough linear operating range to preserve constellation geometry near those peaks. Backoff reduces efficiency, while aggressive clipping increases error vector magnitude and spectral spreading. Digital predistortion can compensate a characterized amplifier within its usable memory and bandwidth limits; it does not make an arbitrarily saturated stage linear. PAPR statistics depend on subcarrier assignments, oversampling and measurement interval.

Core mathematical relationship

For discrete samples x[n], PAPR=max_n |x[n]|² / mean_n |x[n]|²; clipping a multicarrier waveform adds in-band distortion and out-of-band regrowth.
