§2.1 Phasor Kirchhoff laws

Unit 2, Lesson 1: Phasor Kirchhoff laws

Primary relation: ∑ Ĩnode=0; ∑ Ṽloop=0 Unit outcome: Use impedance networks to solve currents, power, and amplitude response with consistent complex arithmetic.

What the relation means

KCL and KVL remain valid in linear lumped networks at a common sinusoidal frequency because summation commutes with differentiation. Current reference arrows and passive signs still matter. The expression ∑ Ĩnode=0; ∑ Ṽloop=0 is meaningful only when the input, output, reference arrows, and component idealizations are stated. Draw one simple circuit or signal diagram, label every referenced terminal, and identify which terms can be measured and which are inferred from the model. Distinguish an algebraic sign from a physical polarity; a sign reversal after changing an arrow is usually a coordinate choice, not a violation of energy conservation.

Detailed engineering reasoning

Frequency-domain network solving resembles DC solving, but every branch quantity is complex. An AC nodal analysis must use a single frequency at a time; if sources differ in frequency, solve each tone separately. A complex answer represents two real facts—amplitude and phase—not a mysterious imaginary current. Check KCL by adding rectangular phasors, not their magnitudes.

For phasor kirchhoff laws, begin with a simplest-case prediction before substituting values. If an excitation vanishes, a passive zero-initial-state model must not invent an output. If one parameter becomes very large or small, interpret the corresponding limit with the relevant source and load held fixed. For an AC circuit, a magnitude-only answer is incomplete whenever phase matters, and for a transient circuit the initial state changes the waveform even if the final DC steady state is the same.

Worked example and interpretation

A node with 2∠0° A entering and 1∠90° A leaving requires a third leaving current 2−j1 A. Report the numerical quantity with physical units. Then explain what the sign or phase of the result tells an observer. Use another representation—for example rectangular versus polar complex notation or time versus frequency response—to check the result without simply repeating the first arithmetic.

The model predicts a conditional answer. It does not predict tolerance drift, insulation integrity, wiring parasitics, thermal runaway, bandwidth of an instrument, or safe operation unless those effects have been explicitly included. A nominal value should therefore be reported alongside its assumed operating conditions.

Failure mode to detect

Do not add phasor magnitudes as if they were signed scalar currents. To diagnose this error, ask which assumption was silently changed. Trace the computation back to the definition of the source, the selected terminals, and the units of the constitutive law. A useful check is to repeat the calculation with a parameter changed by a factor of two and decide whether the direction of the output change is consistent with physical intuition. Where the network has several branches, apply KCL, KVL, and a power balance rather than trusting a single number.

Primary relation: Y Ṽ=Ĩ Unit outcome: Use impedance networks to solve currents, power, and amplitude response with consistent complex arithmetic.

What the relation means

Complex admittance transforms connected impedance into linear equations. Formulate node equations with conductances and susceptances, or mesh equations with impedances. The expression Y Ṽ=Ĩ is meaningful only when the input, output, reference arrows, and component idealizations are stated. Detailed engineering reasoning

A current-phasor power calculation should document the positive terminal and current reference of the load. For a capacitor-dominated network, source-current phase leads voltage and Q is negative under passive convention. Apparent power magnitude is |S|=Vrms Irms, whereas P can be far smaller if reactance dominates.

§2.3 Real, reactive, and apparent power

Unit 2, Lesson 3: Real, reactive, and apparent power

Primary relation: S=Ṽ Ĩ*=P+jQ Unit outcome: Use impedance networks to solve currents, power, and amplitude response with consistent complex arithmetic.

What the relation means

The conjugate is essential for RMS complex power under passive-sign convention. Positive Q indicates net inductive reactive absorption; negative Q indicates net capacitive behavior. The expression S=Ṽ Ĩ*=P+jQ is meaningful only when the input, output, reference arrows, and component idealizations are stated. Draw one simple circuit or signal diagram, label every referenced terminal, and identify which terms can be measured and which are inferred from the model. Distinguish an algebraic sign from a physical polarity; a sign reversal after changing an arrow is usually a coordinate choice, not a violation of energy conservation.

Detailed engineering reasoning

Resonance creates a useful but dangerous tradeoff: cancellation of external reactances permits high current, while individual reactive voltages may exceed the source voltage by Q. Ideal lossless formulas miss coil resistance, capacitor ESR, and source impedance. Always test a resonance formula above, below, and exactly at its nominal frequency and state how a shifted component value alters the peak.

For real, reactive, and apparent power, begin with a simplest-case prediction before substituting values. If an excitation vanishes, a passive zero-initial-state model must not invent an output.
