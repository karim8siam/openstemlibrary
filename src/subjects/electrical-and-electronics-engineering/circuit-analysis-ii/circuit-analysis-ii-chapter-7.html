§7.1 Fourier series representation

Unit 7, Lesson 1: Fourier series representation

Primary relation: x(t)=a0+∑[an cos(nω0t)+bn sin(nω0t)] Unit outcome: Connect periodic time waveforms to their spectra and test LTI approximations under non-sinusoidal excitation.

What the relation means

A periodic waveform can be decomposed into harmonics under suitable convergence conditions. Linear circuits process each sinusoidal harmonic through the transfer function at its own frequency. The expression x(t)=a0+∑[an cos(nω0t)+bn sin(nω0t)] is meaningful only when the input, output, reference arrows, and component idealizations are stated. Draw one simple circuit or signal diagram, label every referenced terminal, and identify which terms can be measured and which are inferred from the model. Distinguish an algebraic sign from a physical polarity; a sign reversal after changing an arrow is usually a coordinate choice, not a violation of energy conservation.

Detailed engineering reasoning

Fourier analysis gives a language for waveform shape. A square pulse is not merely a high-frequency sinusoid: its harmonics are essential for sharp transitions. When harmonic components pass through a low-pass circuit, each frequency is attenuated and shifted differently, changing the reconstructed waveform. Plotting only the magnitude spectrum hides the phase needed for time-domain reconstruction.

For fourier series representation, begin with a simplest-case prediction before substituting values. If an excitation vanishes, a passive zero-initial-state model must not invent an output. If one parameter becomes very large or small, interpret the corresponding limit with the relevant source and load held fixed. For an AC circuit, a magnitude-only answer is incomplete whenever phase matters, and for a transient circuit the initial state changes the waveform even if the final DC steady state is the same.

Worked example and interpretation

An ideal 1 V-amplitude symmetric square wave has first sine harmonic amplitude 4/π≈1.273 V. Start from the stated equation, convert any milli-, micro- or kilo- prefixes to SI units, and substitute only quantities that share a consistent peak/RMS convention. Report the numerical quantity with physical units. Then explain what the sign or phase of the result tells an observer. Use another representation—for example rectangular versus polar complex notation or time versus frequency response—to check the result without simply repeating the first arithmetic.

The model predicts a conditional answer. It does not predict tolerance drift, insulation integrity, wiring parasitics, thermal runaway, bandwidth of an instrument, or safe operation unless those effects have been explicitly included. A nominal value should therefore be reported alongside its assumed operating conditions.

Failure mode to detect

A square wave cannot be represented by its fundamental alone when rise time or harmonics matter. To diagnose this error, ask which assumption was silently changed. Trace the computation back to the definition of the source, the selected terminals, and the units of the constitutive law. A useful check is to repeat the calculation with a parameter changed by a factor of two and decide whether the direction of the output change is consistent with physical intuition. Where the network has several branches, apply KCL, KVL, and a power balance rather than trusting a single number.

Primary relation: X(ω)=∫ x(t)e^(−jωt)dt Unit outcome: Connect periodic time waveforms to their spectra and test LTI approximations under non-sinusoidal excitation.

What the relation means

The Fourier transform addresses aperiodic signals and yields a continuous frequency spectrum. Distinguish the radian-frequency transform from the hertz-frequency convention because factors of 2π shift. The expression X(ω)=∫ x(t)e^(−jωt)dt is meaningful only when the input, output, reference arrows, and component idealizations are stated. Draw one simple circuit or signal diagram, label every referenced terminal, and identify which terms can be measured and which are inferred from the model. Detailed engineering reasoning

Aliasing is a loss of distinguishability introduced by sampling. For sinusoids, frequencies separated by an integer multiple of sample rate can create indistinguishable sequences. The Nyquist inequality requires the input be bandlimited before sampling; an FFT or interpolation after acquisition cannot repair an aliased signal. A true instrument design requires analog anti-alias filtering and clock-accuracy assessment.
