§6.1 Transfer functions and Bode plots

Unit 6, Lesson 1: Transfer functions and Bode plots

Primary relation: H(jω)=Vout/Vin Unit outcome: Design and evaluate networks by their gain, phase, loading, and frequency selectivity.

What the relation means

Magnitude in decibels is 20log10|H| for voltage ratios, while phase records time shift between same-frequency sinusoids. A pole ordinarily introduces a −20 dB/decade asymptote in a first-order minimum-phase low-pass. The expression H(jω)=Vout/Vin is meaningful only when the input, output, reference arrows, and component idealizations are stated. Draw one simple circuit or signal diagram, label every referenced terminal, and identify which terms can be measured and which are inferred from the model. Distinguish an algebraic sign from a physical polarity; a sign reversal after changing an arrow is usually a coordinate choice, not a violation of energy conservation.

Detailed engineering reasoning

Gain and phase must be specified with the same input and output reference terminals at all frequencies. In a first-order RC low-pass, |H| tends to one at very low frequencies and to zero at high frequencies. A high-pass complements that behavior, but the actual output impedance and load may shift the corner. Plotting gain in decibels makes multiplicative ratios add.

For transfer functions and bode plots, begin with a simplest-case prediction before substituting values. If an excitation vanishes, a passive zero-initial-state model must not invent an output. If one parameter becomes very large or small, interpret the corresponding limit with the relevant source and load held fixed. For an AC circuit, a magnitude-only answer is incomplete whenever phase matters, and for a transient circuit the initial state changes the waveform even if the final DC steady state is the same.

Worked example and interpretation

For a first-order RC low-pass, at ω=1/(RC) magnitude is 1/√2 or −3.01 dB. Start from the stated equation, convert any milli-, micro- or kilo- prefixes to SI units, and substitute only quantities that share a consistent peak/RMS convention. Report the numerical quantity with physical units. Then explain what the sign or phase of the result tells an observer. Use another representation—for example rectangular versus polar complex notation or time versus frequency response—to check the result without simply repeating the first arithmetic.

The model predicts a conditional answer. It does not predict tolerance drift, insulation integrity, wiring parasitics, thermal runaway, bandwidth of an instrument, or safe operation unless those effects have been explicitly included. A nominal value should therefore be reported alongside its assumed operating conditions.

Failure mode to detect

Using 10log10 for voltage gain without a squared quantity gives half the correct dB result. To diagnose this error, ask which assumption was silently changed. Trace the computation back to the definition of the source, the selected terminals, and the units of the constitutive law. A useful check is to repeat the calculation with a parameter changed by a factor of two and decide whether the direction of the output change is consistent with physical intuition. Where the network has several branches, apply KCL, KVL, and a power balance rather than trusting a single number.

1. Prediction. Before any calculator is used, describe the expected response for low, nominal, and high excitation. Write down the reference direction and the output to measure.

2. Calculation. Derive the governing expression from the ideal element relations. Recalculate a second operating point and show intermediate values, SI conversions, and the choice of complex argument branch if needed.

§6.2 RC and RL passive filters

Unit 6, Lesson 2: RC and RL passive filters

Primary relation: HLP(s)=1/(1+sRC) Unit outcome: Design and evaluate networks by their gain, phase, loading, and frequency selectivity.

What the relation means

Component placement and the selected output terminals determine whether a simple RC/RL network is low-pass or high-pass. The frequency response is derived from an impedance divider, not from component names alone. The expression HLP(s)=1/(1+sRC) is meaningful only when the input, output, reference arrows, and component idealizations are stated. Draw one simple circuit or signal diagram, label every referenced terminal, and identify which terms can be measured and which are inferred from the model. Distinguish an algebraic sign from a physical polarity; a sign reversal after changing an arrow is usually a coordinate choice, not a violation of energy conservation.

Detailed engineering reasoning

Two-port parameters become valuable when repeatedly combining blocks, but the assumed current sign convention matters. ABCD matrix multiplication follows the cascade order and often uses output current defined as leaving the second port. Under terminated loading, direct multiplication of isolated voltage gain numbers is generally invalid. For cascaded passive RC stages, interstage loading can shift both response and phase.

For rc and rl passive filters, begin with a simplest-case prediction before substituting values. If an excitation vanishes, a passive zero-initial-state model must not invent an output. If one parameter becomes very large or small, interpret the corresponding limit with the relevant source and load held fixed. For an AC circuit, a magnitude-only answer is incomplete whenever phase matters, and for a transient circuit the initial state changes the waveform even if the final DC steady state is the same.

§6.3 RLC band-pass and band-stop circuits

Unit 6, Lesson 3: RLC band-pass and band-stop circuits

Primary relation: BW=ω0/Q (series RLC) Unit outcome: Design and evaluate networks by their gain, phase, loading, and frequency selectivity.

What the relation means

RLC networks can favor or suppress a narrow frequency interval. Their quality factor and bandwidth follow the chosen topology; a formula from a series circuit should not be applied to a parallel circuit blindly. The expression BW=ω0/Q (series RLC) is meaningful only when the input, output, reference arrows, and component idealizations are stated. Draw one simple circuit or signal diagram, label every referenced terminal, and identify which terms can be measured and which are inferred from the model. Distinguish an algebraic sign from a physical polarity; a sign reversal after changing an arrow is usually a coordinate choice, not a violation of energy conservation.

Detailed engineering reasoning

Filter performance has several competing goals. A narrower passband may imply a longer impulse response. A sharper transition may increase component count, loss and sensitivity. Passive filters cannot deliver power gain, although a voltage resonance may increase some measured node voltages relative to the source. A responsible specification includes source/load impedances, tolerance, temperature, and input signal range.

For rlc band-pass and band-stop circuits, begin with a simplest-case prediction before substituting values. If an excitation vanishes, a passive zero-initial-state model must not invent an output. If one parameter becomes very large or small, interpret the corresponding limit with the relevant source and load held fixed. For an AC circuit, a magnitude-only answer is incomplete whenever phase matters, and for a transient circuit the initial state changes the waveform even if the final DC steady state is the same.
