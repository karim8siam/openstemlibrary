§5.1 Unilateral Laplace transform

Unit 5, Lesson 1: Unilateral Laplace transform

Primary relation: F(s)=∫[0,∞] f(t)e^(−st) dt Unit outcome: Represent arbitrary switching waveforms using initial conditions, algebraic impedances and pole-based responses.

What the relation means

The unilateral transform is adapted to switching circuits because it explicitly encodes the state immediately before t=0. It transforms time derivatives into algebraic expressions plus initial-state terms. The expression F(s)=∫[0,∞] f(t)e^(−st) dt is meaningful only when the input, output, reference arrows, and component idealizations are stated. Draw one simple circuit or signal diagram, label every referenced terminal, and identify which terms can be measured and which are inferred from the model. Distinguish an algebraic sign from a physical polarity; a sign reversal after changing an arrow is usually a coordinate choice, not a violation of energy conservation.

Detailed engineering reasoning

Laplace methods connect the time-domain state to an algebraic expression. A capacitor initially holding charge has memory; a new switch position does not erase that state. When transforming i=C dv/dt, record initial v(0−) explicitly. In an ideal network, finite inductor voltage cannot cause an instantaneous current jump, and finite capacitor current cannot cause an instantaneous voltage jump.

For unilateral laplace transform, begin with a simplest-case prediction before substituting values. If an excitation vanishes, a passive zero-initial-state model must not invent an output. If one parameter becomes very large or small, interpret the corresponding limit with the relevant source and load held fixed. For an AC circuit, a magnitude-only answer is incomplete whenever phase matters, and for a transient circuit the initial state changes the waveform even if the final DC steady state is the same.

Worked example and interpretation

For f(t)=e^(−2t)u(t), F(s)=1/(s+2), valid where Re(s)>−2. Start from the stated equation, convert any milli-, micro- or kilo- prefixes to SI units, and substitute only quantities that share a consistent peak/RMS convention. Report the numerical quantity with physical units. Then explain what the sign or phase of the result tells an observer. Use another representation—for example rectangular versus polar complex notation or time versus frequency response—to check the result without simply repeating the first arithmetic.

The model predicts a conditional answer. It does not predict tolerance drift, insulation integrity, wiring parasitics, thermal runaway, bandwidth of an instrument, or safe operation unless those effects have been explicitly included. A nominal value should therefore be reported alongside its assumed operating conditions.

Failure mode to detect

Forgetting the region of convergence may disguise a noncausal or unstable inverse transform. To diagnose this error, ask which assumption was silently changed. Trace the computation back to the definition of the source, the selected terminals, and the units of the constitutive law. A useful check is to repeat the calculation with a parameter changed by a factor of two and decide whether the direction of the output change is consistent with physical intuition. Where the network has several branches, apply KCL, KVL, and a power balance rather than trusting a single number.

Design exercise: a three-stage method

1. Prediction. Before any calculator is used, describe the expected response for low, nominal, and high excitation. Write down the reference direction and the output to measure.

2. Calculation. Derive the governing expression from the ideal element relations. Recalculate a second operating point and show intermediate values, SI conversions, and the choice of complex argument branch if needed.

3. Assessment. Identify a parasitic or nonideality absent from the calculation. Explain how an engineer would test it with a safe low-voltage setup, without assuming that software itself validates a built device.

§5.2 Initial conditions in the s domain

Unit 5, Lesson 2: Initial conditions in the s domain

Primary relation: L{dv/dt}=sV(s)−v(0−) Unit outcome: Represent arbitrary switching waveforms using initial conditions, algebraic impedances and pole-based responses.

What the relation means

Capacitor voltage and inductor current are state variables that cannot change instantaneously under finite ideal excitation. Their initial conditions act as independent terms in transformed circuit equations. The expression L{dv/dt}=sV(s)−v(0−) is meaningful only when the input, output, reference arrows, and component idealizations are stated. Draw one simple circuit or signal diagram, label every referenced terminal, and identify which terms can be measured and which are inferred from the model. Distinguish an algebraic sign from a physical polarity; a sign reversal after changing an arrow is usually a coordinate choice, not a violation of energy conservation.

Detailed engineering reasoning

Transfer-function poles reveal modes but are not the whole answer. A pole-zero cancellation may hide an internal unstable mode from one input-output pair. A right-half-plane zero affects phase and control response, though a passive RC ladder ordinarily exhibits stable poles in ordinary parameter ranges. For a first-order step, 63.2% at one τ follows directly from 1−e^(−1).

§5.3 s-domain impedances

Unit 5, Lesson 3: s-domain impedances

Primary relation: ZL(s)=sL; ZC(s)=1/(sC) Unit outcome: Represent arbitrary switching waveforms using initial conditions, algebraic impedances and pole-based responses.

What the relation means

Laplace impedances resemble phasor impedances but also require proper initial-condition sources. They permit algebraic network solving and transfer-function extraction for linear time-invariant circuits. The expression ZL(s)=sL; ZC(s)=1/(sC) is meaningful only when the input, output, reference arrows, and component idealizations are stated. Draw one simple circuit or signal diagram, label every referenced terminal, and identify which terms can be measured and which are inferred from the model. Distinguish an algebraic sign from a physical polarity; a sign reversal after changing an arrow is usually a coordinate choice, not a violation of energy conservation.

Detailed engineering reasoning

Convolution is the universal time-domain formula for causal linear time-invariant circuits. The impulse response h(t) gives the output to an ideal unit impulse; the response to an arbitrary input follows by weighting and shifting copies of h. In computation, use finite time steps and disclose numerical truncation, initial condition handling, and sampling error.

For s-domain impedances, begin with a simplest-case prediction before substituting values. If an excitation vanishes, a passive zero-initial-state model must not invent an output. If one parameter becomes very large or small, interpret the corresponding limit with the relevant source and load held fixed. For an AC circuit, a magnitude-only answer is incomplete whenever phase matters, and for a transient circuit the initial state changes the waveform even if the final DC steady state is the same.
