§4.1 Mutual inductance and flux linkage

Unit 4, Lesson 1: Mutual inductance and flux linkage

Primary relation: M=k√(L1 L2) Unit outcome: Understand coupling, sign conventions, equivalent impedances, and limits of the ideal-transformer model.

What the relation means

The coupling coefficient is bounded by |k|≤1 for passive lumped windings. Flux created by one winding can link another, introducing induced voltage proportional to rate of current change. The expression M=k√(L1 L2) is meaningful only when the input, output, reference arrows, and component idealizations are stated. Draw one simple circuit or signal diagram, label every referenced terminal, and identify which terms can be measured and which are inferred from the model. Distinguish an algebraic sign from a physical polarity; a sign reversal after changing an arrow is usually a coordinate choice, not a violation of energy conservation.

Detailed engineering reasoning

Mutual inductance is an energy-storage interaction. The self terms L1 and L2 account for voltage from each winding current, while M multiplies the derivative of the other winding current. In the passive model, energy positivity demands a bounded coupling factor; if |M| exceeds √(L1L2), the parameter set is not physically passive.

For mutual inductance and flux linkage, begin with a simplest-case prediction before substituting values. If an excitation vanishes, a passive zero-initial-state model must not invent an output. If one parameter becomes very large or small, interpret the corresponding limit with the relevant source and load held fixed. For an AC circuit, a magnitude-only answer is incomplete whenever phase matters, and for a transient circuit the initial state changes the waveform even if the final DC steady state is the same.

Worked example and interpretation

For L1=4 mH, L2=9 mH and k=0.5, M=3 mH. Start from the stated equation, convert any milli-, micro- or kilo- prefixes to SI units, and substitute only quantities that share a consistent peak/RMS convention. Report the numerical quantity with physical units. Then explain what the sign or phase of the result tells an observer. Use another representation—for example rectangular versus polar complex notation or time versus frequency response—to check the result without simply repeating the first arithmetic.

The model predicts a conditional answer. It does not predict tolerance drift, insulation integrity, wiring parasitics, thermal runaway, bandwidth of an instrument, or safe operation unless those effects have been explicitly included. A nominal value should therefore be reported alongside its assumed operating conditions.

Failure mode to detect

M must not be selected larger than √(L1L2) in a passive two-winding model. To diagnose this error, ask which assumption was silently changed. Trace the computation back to the definition of the source, the selected terminals, and the units of the constitutive law. A useful check is to repeat the calculation with a parameter changed by a factor of two and decide whether the direction of the output change is consistent with physical intuition. Where the network has several branches, apply KCL, KVL, and a power balance rather than trusting a single number.

1. Prediction. Before any calculator is used, describe the expected response for low, nominal, and high excitation. Write down the reference direction and the output to measure.

2. Calculation. Derive the governing expression from the ideal element relations. Recalculate a second operating point and show intermediate values, SI conversions, and the choice of complex argument branch if needed.

§4.2 Dot convention and voltage signs

Unit 4, Lesson 2: Dot convention and voltage signs

Primary relation: v1=L1 di1/dt ± M di2/dt Unit outcome: Understand coupling, sign conventions, equivalent impedances, and limits of the ideal-transformer model.

What the relation means

Dots mark corresponding instantaneous voltage polarity for current references. Changing a current reference reverses a mutual term without changing the physical coil. The expression v1=L1 di1/dt ± M di2/dt is meaningful only when the input, output, reference arrows, and component idealizations are stated. Draw one simple circuit or signal diagram, label every referenced terminal, and identify which terms can be measured and which are inferred from the model. Distinguish an algebraic sign from a physical polarity; a sign reversal after changing an arrow is usually a coordinate choice, not a violation of energy conservation.

Detailed engineering reasoning

Dot convention is best learned by a disciplined reference exercise. Draw reference arrows entering dotted terminals on both windings. Then the mutual terms enter with matching signs. Flip one arrow and update its entire current and voltage convention. Without this explicit book-keeping, the same coupled pair may appear to have contradictory equations.

For dot convention and voltage signs, begin with a simplest-case prediction before substituting values. If an excitation vanishes, a passive zero-initial-state model must not invent an output. If one parameter becomes very large or small, interpret the corresponding limit with the relevant source and load held fixed. For an AC circuit, a magnitude-only answer is incomplete whenever phase matters, and for a transient circuit the initial state changes the waveform even if the final DC steady state is the same.

§4.3 Coupled circuits at sinusoidal steady state

Unit 4, Lesson 3: Coupled circuits at sinusoidal steady state

Primary relation: V1=jωL1 I1+jωM I2 Unit outcome: Understand coupling, sign conventions, equivalent impedances, and limits of the ideal-transformer model.

What the relation means

Coupled phasor equations are a 2×2 impedance matrix. The off-diagonal signs depend on reference directions, and the matrix must represent physically admissible stored magnetic energy. The expression V1=jωL1 I1+jωM I2 is meaningful only when the input, output, reference arrows, and component idealizations are stated. Draw one simple circuit or signal diagram, label every referenced terminal, and identify which terms can be measured and which are inferred from the model. Distinguish an algebraic sign from a physical polarity; a sign reversal after changing an arrow is usually a coordinate choice, not a violation of energy conservation.

Detailed engineering reasoning

An ideal transformer is a constrained device model, not a universal model of real magnetic components. The effective impedance transformation arises from the voltage ratio squared and the inverse current ratio. At light load, finite magnetizing current can dominate; at heavy load, winding resistance can reduce output voltage. For any hardware build, insulation class, creepage distance, fault protection, thermal performance and local standards require qualified design.

For coupled circuits at sinusoidal steady state, begin with a simplest-case prediction before substituting values. If an excitation vanishes, a passive zero-initial-state model must not invent an output. If one parameter becomes very large or small, interpret the corresponding limit with the relevant source and load held fixed. For an AC circuit, a magnitude-only answer is incomplete whenever phase matters, and for a transient circuit the initial state changes the waveform even if the final DC steady state is the same.
