Macroevolution and deep-time diversity

> Radiations, innovations, extinction and limitations of diversification-rate models.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Evolutionary Biology study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§6.1 Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, the reader should be able to investigate macroevolution and deep-time diversity, interpret a relevant quantitative model, design a realistic test that controls for alternative explanations, and state the principal limits of the inference. An intellectually satisfactory answer can remain uncertain when the data do not discriminate among mechanisms.

§6.2 Integrative foundation

Diversity has several currencies

Species richness counts currently recognized units, morphological disparity measures how different their forms or functions are, and phylogenetic diversity reflects the lengths of evolutionary branches represented in an assemblage. These measures can move in different directions. A lineage might evolve several distinctive ecologies without producing many species; conversely, a rapid burst of closely related species can increase richness while occupying similar positions in trait space. Extinction removes historical branches, and the pattern remaining among survivors can misrepresent earlier ecological experiments.

When researchers propose adaptive radiation, they should establish both shared history and ecological diversification and, where appropriate, examine changes in the tempo of lineage splitting. A richly diversified clade does not automatically document 'ecological opportunity'; that causal explanation needs information about niche availability, colonization time, competition and alternative histories. Extinct species are especially important because living taxa are a survivorship-filtered sample of what existed.

The fossil record and rates

Fossil occurrence records are uneven. Marine sediments and hard-bodied taxa often preserve better than delicate terrestrial organisms; geological formations are not equally exposed or sampled; stratigraphic intervals can mix remains from different moments. A fossil taxon's first appearance is later than or equal to its actual origin, and its last documented occurrence is earlier than or equal to its true extinction, in the usual direction of the fossil-record uncertainties. A claim that lineages changed 'instantaneously' from adjacent layers is generally not justified without discussing the layers' temporal resolution.

Patterns described as stasis can involve little visible change in measured morphology while substantial genetic, behavioral or ecological evolution continues. Punctuated equilibrium concerns episodes of relatively rapid morphological change separated by long intervals of comparative stasis, often interpreted in the context of speciation; it does not imply that mutation, selection or drift have stopped operating between episodes. Rate statements always require a measured character and a time denominator.

Models of diversification are conditional

Simple birth–death models characterize speciation and extinction with parameters usually written λ and μ, respectively; the net diversification rate is λ−μ under a specific model. Richness at one date cannot independently determine both rates. A clade with high speciation and high extinction could have the same net rate as one with both low. Fossils, sampling fractions and changes through time can improve analysis, though different models may remain nearly indistinguishable with available data. Trait-associated diversification models can be especially vulnerable when unsampled species or historical conditions correlate with the trait.

Innovations such as altered appendage form, biochemical pathways or developmental regulators can open ecological possibilities without guaranteeing adaptive radiation. To test a proposed innovation, map its evolutionary origin, assess its developmental mechanism and compare diversification or ecological performance with appropriately matched relatives. A good explanation connects levels without assuming that a single gene 'caused' the success of millions of years of subsequent lineages.

§6.3 Focused studies and worked problems

6.1 Adaptive radiation and ecological opportunity

Core idea and causal mechanism

Adaptive radiation describes diversification associated with ecological differences, often when available niches, novel resources or reduced competition permit divergent lineages to persist.

Colonization, habitat fragmentation, ecological interactions and developmental variation can provide opportunities. Radiation need not involve unusually high speciation rates at every moment; extinction can alter the surviving pattern.

Example in biological context

Lake fish or island lizards may diverge in jaw structure, habitat use and mating signals. Testing radiation requires evidence of common ancestry, ecological divergence and diversification rather than only striking shapes.

Evidence and a workable test

Estimate lineage relationships and ecology with comparable sampling, then ask whether speciation or morphological disparity shifted after inferred habitat change. Include extinct or unsampled groups where evidence allows.

Investigation prompt. Sketch a lineage history with an extinct branch that is absent from modern sampling. Ask how the missing branch would alter apparent diversity, trait transitions or inferred ecological specialization.

Where the inference could fail

Modern diversity is the result of both speciation and extinction. A clade with many species is not automatically more successful, and a set of similar ecological forms may represent convergence among distant relatives.

Specify the fossil preservation and stratigraphic resolution that would be needed to compare alternate histories. A simple numerical rate without its denominator and uncertainty cannot establish the cause of diversification.

Apply and question

Compare a hypothesis of shared ancestral ecological specialization with repeated adaptation following dispersal. Identify which phylogenetic mapping predicts different outcomes.

Adaptive-radiation research helps explain biodiversity hotspots and how introduced species can alter ecological opportunity for native lineages.

What if morphological diversification is rapid but the number of species stays nearly constant? Explain how those observations can coexist. Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

6.2 The fossil record, stasis and tempo of evolution

Core idea and causal mechanism

Evolutionary change need not occur at a constant visible rate. Fossil sequences can show long-lasting morphologies, abrupt appearance within sampling resolution and variable change among lineages.

Environmental stability, stabilizing selection, developmental constraints and shifting population structure are possible explanations for stasis. Punctuated-equilibrium patterns describe aspects of fossil tempo but are not a separate mechanism replacing selection or drift.

Example in biological context

Marine invertebrate shells may preserve diagnostic features across many strata, while brief intervals can record relatively fast shifts. Time averaging and preservational gaps complicate the apparent pace.

Evidence and a workable test

Quantify morphology at repeated dated horizons, estimate measurement error and compare the resolution of strata to plausible generation times before claiming an instantaneous transformation.

Where the inference could fail

Absence of visible change in one shell trait does not imply genetic stasis in every locus. Gaps can mimic jumps; taxonomic reclassification can also create apparent abrupt boundaries.

Apply and question

Construct two hypothetical time series, one continuous but poorly sampled and one with genuinely concentrated change. Ask which fossil sampling scheme would discriminate them.

Fossil tempos contextualize current biodiversity change, but ancient geological averages should not be converted into simplistic forecasts for contemporary ecosystems.

If two species occupy the same deposit but share similar shell form, why is chronological coexistence alone insufficient to establish stasis or direct ancestry? Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

Quantitative case 6.2: Morphological rate in a time series

Assumed data (synthetic). A standardized shell feature changes from 3.2 to 3.8 units over 1.5 million years.

Calculation. Compute change per million years. Substituting the stipulated values into (3.8-3.2)/1.5 gives 0.4 trait units per million years. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

Interpretation. The average rate of 0.4 conceals potential periods of stasis and rapid change.

Model limitation. Preservational time averaging can smooth abrupt shifts.

6.3 Extinction, background turnover and mass extinctions

Core idea and causal mechanism

Extinction permanently removes lineages, shaping the tree of surviving biodiversity. Background extinction and intervals of unusually elevated loss can have different ecological and evolutionary consequences.

Global environmental change, habitat collapse, climate shifts and interacting ecological dependencies can raise extinction risk; the fossil record records appearances and last occurrences under strong sampling limitations.

Example in biological context

The end-Cretaceous event eliminated non-avian dinosaurs while birds persisted; the distinction matters because birds are dinosaurs phylogenetically. Surviving lineages radiated into altered ecosystems afterward.

Evidence and a workable test

Use fossil occurrence counts corrected for sampled rock volume or collection intensity where possible. Compare extinction timing with independent geological and geochemical records.

Where the inference could fail

A last observed fossil is not necessarily the true extinction moment. Poorly sampled groups can disappear from a regional record and later reappear elsewhere; extrapolation must track those uncertainties.

Apply and question

Distinguish species loss, phylogenetic diversity loss and ecological function loss in a synthetic community. Explain why a single percent extinction number may be insufficient.

Conservation triage benefits from evolutionary diversity and ecological function, yet modern rates should not be compared naively against fossil rates measured over radically different timescales.

Could a mass extinction increase diversification among survivors? Describe resource release without framing catastrophe as biologically desirable. Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

Quantitative case 6.3: Lineage survival fraction

Assumed data (synthetic). Twenty lineages are confidently identified before an extinction interval and five are sampled afterward.

Calculation. Compute sampled lineage survival percentage. Substituting the stipulated values into 100*5/20 gives 25 percent. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

Interpretation. A 25-percent sampled survival fraction must not be mistaken for a global extinction estimate without sampling corrections.

6.4 Evolutionary innovations and developmental changes

Core idea and causal mechanism

New ecological capabilities often result from modifications of existing genetic and anatomical systems, not the sudden appearance of fully assembled organs independent of ancestry.

Gene duplication can relax constraints on one copy, changes in gene regulation shift where or when structures form, and altered developmental interactions produce new combinations. Not every novelty depends on a new gene.

Example in biological context

Changes in vertebrate appendages and flower structures involve regulatory deployment of longstanding developmental programs. Similar developmental genes can be reused in distinct origins of superficially similar traits.

Evidence and a workable test

Compare expression patterns, functional perturbations and fossil/phylogenetic distributions. Trace which subcomponents predate the full innovation rather than treating the modern endpoint as the starting cause.

Where the inference could fail

Loss-of-function evidence can reveal necessity in development without proving the historical order of origin. Deep conservation of a regulator should not be mistaken for complete homology of the final organ.

Apply and question

Explain how gene duplication and later functional divergence could change a biochemical pathway. Propose separate tests for gene history and phenotypic function.

Evolutionary developmental biology clarifies congenital variation and morphological diversity, with limits when transferring across distant species.

What would count as evidence for co-option of an existing regulatory network rather than de novo invention? Specify comparative genomic and developmental predictions. Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

Quantitative case 6.4: Gene duplication copies

Assumed data (synthetic). A single ancestral gene is duplicated twice in series without losses along the same lineage.

Calculation. Compute copies by repeated doubling. Substituting the stipulated values into 22 gives 4 gene copies. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

Interpretation. Four copies are a didactic branching maximum if each copy duplicates in each event; real duplicate events usually affect particular loci, not all genes synchronously.

Model limitation. A duplication followed by loss changes copy number and may obscure historical events.

6.5 Diversification rates and the problem of survivorship

Core idea and causal mechanism

Observed species richness reflects speciation, extinction, sampling effort and time since origins. Apparent explosive diversification may partly reflect which branches survive to the present.

Birth–death models represent splitting and extinction as statistical rates under assumptions, while time-dependent sampling and incomplete fossils can bias their estimates. Richness and disparity are different dimensions of diversity.

Example in biological context

Two sister clades of similar age may differ dramatically in living species counts even if historical diversification fluctuated through time. Missing extinct branches can conceal past richness.

Evidence and a workable test

Use an explicitly simulated phylogeny with known speciation and extinction parameters, remove some tips and compare inferred rates. Repeat across replicate histories before claiming a distinctive process.

Where the inference could fail

Rate heterogeneity may be confounded with incompletely sampled lineages or taxonomic practices. Correlation of richness with a trait does not demonstrate that the trait caused diversification.

Apply and question

Compute net diversification under a simplified birth–death approximation and compare two clades of equal age. Explain why an estimated net rate does not reveal extinction and speciation separately.

Biogeographic and conservation planning should avoid treating surviving species count as a complete index of evolutionary uniqueness.

If a region holds many closely related endemic species and another holds few deeply divergent lineages, which priorities depend on your conservation objective? Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

Quantitative case 6.5: Net diversification approximation

Assumed data (synthetic). Stipulated instantaneous speciation rate is 0.18 per lineage-million-year, with extinction rate 0.07.

Calculation. Net rate r=lambda-mu. Substituting the stipulated values into 0.18-0.07 gives 0.11 per lineage-million-year. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

Interpretation. Net 0.11 does not separate historical diversification paths from a single current richness observation.

Model limitation. Fossil sampling and time-variable rates can make constant-rate models unrealistic.

§6.4 Integrated investigation 6: Diversification and sampling in simulated fossils

Research aim. Evaluate how preservational incompleteness changes inferred extinction or diversification signals in a simple simulated clade. The investigation is designed for an introductory undergraduate seminar or computational laboratory; its specimens, dates, counts and measurements are hypothetical. Do not represent any result as a published empirical discovery.

Materials, assumptions and ethical boundaries

A time-stamped set of 30 simulated lineages, true origination/extinction events, stratigraphic occurrence probabilities and a visible sample of preserved occurrences. Use a spreadsheet or local script to create tables and plots, recording pseudorandom seeds if stochastic simulations are used. Collect no personal genetic data. Do not culture pathogens, collect protected wildlife, disturb habitats or undertake medical tests. Where the real-world analog requires permits, trained staff or ethical approval, use simulation or openly available teaching data instead.

Step-by-step procedure

  1. For diversification and sampling in simulated fossils, define biological units, sampling frame, time coordinate and outcome variables. Write the competing hypotheses as distinct predictions: Evaluate how preservational incompleteness changes inferred extinction or diversification signals in a simple simulated clade. A generic statement that 'evolution occurs' is not a testable outcome.
  2. Assemble the synthetic input table and verify totals. For counts, check whether each observation represents an individual, gene copy, fossil occurrence or independent lineage. For traits, specify scale and units. Leave missing observations explicitly missing rather than silently substituting zero.
  3. Produce a first visualization with individual values visible when practical. Include replicates, not only a pooled mean. Label assumptions, treatment groups and what uncertainty is shown; error bars without a stated definition are ambiguous.
  4. Recalculate the outcome after changing one defensible assumption: sampling effort, effective size, migration, branch rate or measurement error, as appropriate. The change in conclusion is a sensitivity result, not a failure of the exercise.
  5. Write a short report separating observation, model, interpretation and limitation. Include a figure caption, a reproducibility note and one proposed independent test.

Expected reasoning, not prescribed results

True last occurrence and sampled last occurrence need not coincide, and missing branches can make survivor lineages seem unusually successful. In this synthetic scenario, a sudden decrease in sampled fossils occurs when preservation probability drops although true species counts do not. Resist presenting a single calculated value as the only outcome consistent with the mechanism. Explain what a failed prediction would require you to reconsider: an incorrect method, an unrealistic assumption or a genuinely unsupported biological hypothesis. Negative results are informative when sampling was capable of detecting the predicted difference.

Troubleshooting the inference

Rate calculations depend on temporal bin size and the entity counted, while observed first and last fossil occurrences need not equal actual origination and extinction events. Pseudoreplication is a major threat when multiple measurements from one biological unit are treated as independent organisms or populations. Similarly, sampling several fossils from one well-exposed outcrop may not represent equal coverage across geological intervals. Identify the true replication unit and justify the comparator. If simulations use the same seed or parameter configuration, discuss whether the apparently repeated outcome is truly independent.

Submission and further questions

Submit a hypothesis table, methods summary, results figure with labeled axes, numerical worked example, explanation of assumptions and a 150-word limitations statement. Which external geological observations can help separate a preservation artifact from genuine extinction? For extension, describe one observation that would distinguish the leading hypothesis from a realistic competing mechanism, and state how much uncertainty would remain even if that observation agreed with the prediction.

§6.5 End-of-unit integrative assessment

  1. Construct a concept map linking Adaptive radiation and ecological opportunity and Extinction, background turnover and mass extinctions. Each connecting arrow must be labeled with a measurable or historically constrained mechanism, not a vague phrase such as 'related to'. Include at least one causal hypothesis and one statement of historical relationship.
  2. Contrast The fossil record, stasis and tempo of evolution with Diversification rates and the problem of survivorship. Describe a plausible observational pattern compatible with both, then design a new measurement that would make their predictions diverge. State your relevant biological unit and justify how many independent replicates you would need for a preliminary test.
  3. Reproduce all five solved quantitative cases from this unit without viewing their answers. Change one parameter in each case, label units and explain whether the biological conclusion is sensitive to the change. A model calculation is not a field observation.
  4. Evaluate a hypothetical paper claiming that evolutionary innovations and developmental changes has been conclusively demonstrated from a small unreplicated dataset. Draft a respectful scientific response explaining the most serious limitation, a rival mechanism, and a feasible next step.
  5. Prepare a 600-word mini-review integrating chronology or inheritance, mechanism and evidence for two topics of your choice. Define your terminology precisely, distinguish observed data from inferred history and cite a credible external scientific source. Close with a statement of what remains unknown.

Self-check rubric for this unit. When writing about adaptive radiation and ecological opportunity or evolutionary innovations and developmental changes, identify a defined population or lineage, give a defensible observation, show appropriate units or character coding and distinguish the leading hypothesis from a competitor. Evaluation rewards testable reasoning and candid limitations rather than memorization of a favored interpretation.
