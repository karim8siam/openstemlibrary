Chapter 8 • Theory & Derivations

Evolution in medicine, conservation and society

> Testing applied evolutionary hypotheses while respecting uncertainty, biosafety and human dignity.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university evolutionary biology courses. Eight units with five solved questions per unit and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fossil preservation probabilities, pairwise sequence mismatch, selection coefficients, genetic drift decay, and allopatric reproductive isolation. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§8.1 Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, the reader should be able to investigate evolution in medicine, conservation and society, interpret a relevant quantitative model, design a realistic test that controls for alternative explanations, and state the principal limits of the inference. An intellectually satisfactory answer can remain uncertain when the data do not discriminate among mechanisms.

§8.2 Integrative foundation

Applying evolution without converting theory into a slogan

Evolutionary biology is useful in public health, agriculture and conservation because inherited variants interact with changing environments. That usefulness comes with obligations: quantify uncertainty, distinguish selection from confounding and recognize that management changes the selective landscape. Antimicrobial resistance, for example, may reflect inherited bacterial mutations, horizontal acquisition of resistance determinants, mixed infections or diagnostic and dosing problems. A simplified selection equation can teach how relative survival changes allele frequencies, but it cannot prescribe therapy or substitute for susceptibility testing and clinical guidelines.

In agriculture, repeatedly relying on the same pest-control mechanism can favor resistance variants. Yet specific management outcomes depend on pest life history, movement, cross-resistance, product properties, host crop and regulation. The safe classroom task is to compare transparent toy models or published surveillance data, not to recommend field chemical schedules without expert knowledge. Resistance can remain common even when a chemical is reduced because compensatory evolution, low fitness cost or movement from untreated populations may prevent easy reversal.

Conservation is more than maximizing heterozygosity

Genetic diversity matters because it reflects histories of drift, inbreeding and connectivity and can affect capacity for evolutionary responses. However, an apparent gain in heterozygosity does not guarantee a viable population. Habitat quality, disease, mortality and breeding structure determine whether introduced genetic variation translates into sustained reproduction. Assisted movement can improve outcomes in some circumstances and cause harm in others, especially when ecological differences are large or translocations move pathogens. Such decisions require legal authorization, species-specific risk assessment and monitoring across generations.

Effective population size is a useful concept but not a replacement for demographic forecasting. Census trends and genomic trends can point in different directions, particularly immediately after bottlenecks or intensive management. The best conservation analyses state whether the aim is immediate population persistence, retention of unique lineages, maintenance of ecosystem function or a combination. Trade-offs among those aims should be explicit.

Human history and medical inference

Human evolution provides a powerful case of shared ancestry, dispersal and contact among lineages. It is not a ranking of living populations, and phylogenetic categories must never be turned into unsupported claims of human hierarchy or ability. Ancient DNA is vulnerable to contamination, damage and uneven geographic sampling; one ancient genome is evidence about an individual and its relationships, not an exhaustive portrait of all people at that time. Branching among ancestors and episodes of gene flow are compatible rather than mutually exclusive interpretations.

Evolutionary medicine generates hypotheses about pathogen adaptation, immune trade-offs and environmental change. Its limitation is that a plausible ancestral narrative can sound explanatory without making unique, testable predictions. Epidemiology, mechanistic biology and properly designed intervention studies remain necessary. Students should practice asking what observation would falsify a mismatch hypothesis and which confounders would explain the same correlation. The discipline contributes most when it raises sharper research questions rather than replacing evidence with stories.

Looking forward

The rate of environmental change can exceed the capacity for evolutionary rescue, especially when populations are small, fragmented or slow to reproduce. A population that initially increases after a disturbance may have benefited from immigration, altered competition or short-term plasticity; only appropriate genetic and demographic evidence can support an inherited adaptive response. The practical closing skill of this book is to design a study that can distinguish those mechanisms while keeping ethical and social consequences in view.

§8.3 Focused studies and worked problems

8.1 Evolution of drug and pesticide resistance

Core idea and causal mechanism

Resistance is a population-level evolutionary response when heritable variants improve survival or reproduction under a chemical pressure. Its speed depends on existing variation, new mutation, gene flow and treatment regime.

Selection increases resistant genotypes under exposure; target modifications, drug efflux, metabolic detoxification and horizontal transfer can confer different mechanisms. Resistance may carry a cost in other conditions but compensation can reduce it.

Example in biological context

A pest population repeatedly exposed to one insecticide may show rising resistance, while alternative pest-management practices can change selection and population dynamics. Resistance outcomes vary by compound, organism and context.

Evidence and a workable test

Track standardized susceptibility measures across seasons with properly randomized sampling and laboratory quality control. Pair phenotype assays with candidate mechanism tests and look for cross-resistance.

Investigation prompt. State which variables a manager, clinician or conservation scientist can actually observe before deciding on an intervention. Use controlled or published data where practical and avoid biological interventions without authorization.

Where the inference could fail

A rise in treatment failure can also reflect dosage, access, environmental persistence or diagnostic problems. Do not extrapolate a teaching model to medical dosing or field chemical recommendations.

Check whether the evolutionary mechanism is supported independently of the immediate outcome. A treatment failure, an abundance shift or a correlated disease risk can each arise through several different pathways.

Apply and question

Compare two management scenarios in a transparent simulation with an assumed resistance cost; list which biological assumptions could reverse the model ranking.

Resistance monitoring connects evolutionary theory with public health and agriculture, but practical clinical decisions require authoritative protocols and specialists.

Can reducing treatment pressure always restore susceptibility? Discuss compensation, linkage, incomplete return migration and survival of resistant reservoirs. Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

Quantitative case 8.1: Selection under pesticide exposure

Assumed data (synthetic). Resistant individuals survive an assay with probability 0.90 and susceptible individuals with probability 0.15.

Calculation. Compute their survival ratio in that particular exposure. Substituting the stipulated values into 0.9/0.15 gives 6 survival ratio. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

Interpretation. The sixfold advantage in survival need not equal relative lifetime reproductive fitness and may disappear without treatment.

Model limitation. Assay dose, stage and inherited variation must be standardized.

8.2 Conservation genetics and genetic rescue

Core idea and causal mechanism

Conservation genetics uses allelic diversity, inbreeding, population structure and adaptive differentiation to inform population viability; genetic rescue can improve fitness in some small, inbred populations but is context-dependent.

Small effective size increases drift and inbreeding, possibly exposing deleterious recessive alleles. Managed gene flow may restore variation, while introducing poorly matched populations can disrupt ecological adaptations or spread disease.

Example in biological context

Isolated carnivore populations may show genomic evidence of inbreeding. However, moving individuals without habitat security or demographic planning can leave the original conservation problem unresolved.

Evidence and a workable test

Combine genomic diversity, pedigree information, demography and habitat conditions. Monitor survival and reproduction after any authorized conservation intervention and compare with an appropriate baseline.

Where the inference could fail

High heterozygosity is not a complete proxy for population health, and low diversity does not automatically predict immediate extinction. Translocation requires ecological, disease and social evaluation.

Apply and question

Explain how a short-lived demographic increase could mislead an assessment of genetic rescue. Propose indicators to monitor for multiple generations.

Genomics can guide reintroductions but does not replace land protection, governance and species-specific ecology.

How might mixing two populations increase short-term heterozygosity but reduce performance in a particular habitat? Distinguish a plausible risk from a prediction of inevitable outbreeding depression. Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

Quantitative case 8.2: Genetic heterozygosity response

Assumed data (synthetic). A population's heterozygosity statistic changes from 0.21 to 0.27 after a managed conservation period.

Calculation. Calculate the absolute increase in heterozygosity. Substituting the stipulated values into 0.27-0.21 gives 0.06 heterozygosity units. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

Interpretation. A 0.06 increase is not sufficient evidence that long-term demographic risk has fallen; fitness and habitat must also be assessed.

Model limitation. Sampling a different family mix can alter heterozygosity estimates.

8.3 Human evolution and shared ancestry

Core idea and causal mechanism

Human evolutionary history is a branching history among primates, not a linear march from modern monkeys to modern humans. Fossils, comparative anatomy, genetics and archaeology contribute complementary evidence.

Population splits, migration, gene flow and environmental change shaped different hominin lineages. Anatomical mosaics mean traits such as habitual bipedalism, tooth form and brain size need not change together.

Example in biological context

Living chimpanzees are not human ancestors; both lineages share extinct ancestors. Some archaic human populations exchanged genes with ancestors of many living people, showing that branching and admixture can coexist.

Evidence and a workable test

Assess fossil characters within age ranges and independent trees. When reading ancient-DNA results, check contamination controls, sampling representativeness and uncertainty.

Where the inference could fail

A single fossil seldom tells a species-wide story, and ancient genomes reflect individuals rather than exhaustive populations. Modern human population labels should not be treated as pure ancestral biological types.

Apply and question

Draw a branching timeline with possible contact among lineages. Mark direct fossils, dated archaeological evidence and genetic inferences using visually distinct symbols.

Evolutionary evidence can illuminate shared human biology while guarding against essentialist claims about abilities, identities or hierarchy.

How would an older fossil with a mosaic of traits affect one model of human origins without forcing all other evidence to be rejected? Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

Quantitative case 8.3: Ancient lineage admixture

Assumed data (synthetic). An illustrative 2400-base sequence shows 36 sites carrying variants assigned by a statistical ancestry method.

Calculation. Calculate the fraction of sites classified into the category. Substituting the stipulated values into 100*36/2400 gives 1.5 percent. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

Interpretation. A simple site percentage is not an accepted substitute for genome-wide admixture estimation; this exercise highlights the denominator.

Model limitation. Shared ancestral variation and technical artifacts can generate candidate sites.

8.4 Evolutionary medicine and mismatch

Core idea and causal mechanism

Evolutionary medicine asks why vulnerabilities or trade-offs may persist; it complements, rather than replaces, proximate physiology and evidence-based clinical research.

Selection favors reproductive contributions in historical conditions, not guaranteed lifetime health. Pathogen evolution, host immune trade-offs and environmental mismatch can influence risk, but causal explanations require testable mechanisms.

Example in biological context

Some pathogens evolve under drug and immune pressures while host defenses impose inflammation costs. A mismatch hypothesis might predict altered risk under rapid dietary or environmental change, but is not proven by storytelling.

Evidence and a workable test

Formulate predictions contrasting an evolutionary hypothesis with a non-evolutionary mechanism and evaluate them with controlled epidemiology or experimental data where ethical.

Where the inference could fail

An attractive just-so story is not medical evidence. No evolutionary explanation alone determines an individual's diagnosis or medication choice; avoid using speculative ancestral narratives to promote therapies.

Apply and question

Pick a proposed mismatch explanation and specify a measured exposure, comparator group, plausible confounders and an outcome that could falsify the story.

This framework is useful for questions about resistance and susceptibility when integrated with modern clinical guidelines and public-health surveillance.

Why can a trait that was advantageous under one historic environment become harmful now? Give a mechanistic explanation and identify a critical observational test. Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

Quantitative case 8.4: Risk ratio and evolutionary explanation

Assumed data (synthetic). Disease prevalence is 8 per 100 in a study group and 5 per 100 in a comparator group.

Calculation. Calculate the crude prevalence ratio 0.08/0.05. Substituting the stipulated values into 0.08/0.05 gives 1.6 prevalence ratio. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

Interpretation. A crude 1.6 association cannot establish an evolutionary mismatch mechanism or causality.

Model limitation. Exposure, age, socioeconomic status and measurement practices may confound groups.

8.5 Evolution under rapid environmental change

Core idea and causal mechanism

Contemporary evolution occurs when inherited variant frequencies shift across generations during habitat fragmentation, warming, pollution, harvesting or species introductions. Demographic change and plasticity can occur at the same time.

Standing genetic variation, mutation, gene flow, generation time and the rate of environmental shift determine the potential response; selection can fail to keep pace with change despite being directionally favorable.

Example in biological context

Urban plants may shift flowering times and animals may alter behavior near roads. Determining whether the change is genetic requires common-garden or pedigree evidence instead of treating any visible trend as evolution.

Evidence and a workable test

Combine repeated phenotyping with genomic sampling, control for age and environmental effects, and compare affected sites to matched references. Record extinction and local immigration so the surviving sample is not misinterpreted.

Where the inference could fail

Short time series, spatial autocorrelation and selective sampling can produce spurious trends. An allele associated with stress tolerance may be linked to another causal variant or reflect ancestry differences.

Apply and question

Design a before-after-control-impact observational study and indicate why it cannot always isolate evolutionary change. Suggest one common-garden test to strengthen inference.

Realistic forecasting of biodiversity responses needs both population demography and evolutionary potential; neither alone guarantees persistence.

If a population increases in abundance after a heat wave, does that demonstrate genetic adaptation? Describe the alternative role of immigration or ecological release. Your answer should include a prediction that differs between hypotheses and state which assumptions would remain untested after the observation.

Quantitative case 8.5: Population change after a stress event

Assumed data (synthetic). Counts rise from 240 to 276 in a standardized monitoring interval.

Calculation. Calculate proportional count change percentage. Substituting the stipulated values into 100*(276-240)/240 gives 15 percent. Confirm the result independently and retain consistent units.

Interpretation. A 15-percent demographic increase is not evidence by itself of genetic adaptation; survival, immigration and detectability can all contribute.

Model limitation. Survey conditions and sample boundaries need consistent definitions.

Try next. What genomic and common-garden evidence could connect the demographic trend to inherited adaptation? Show the derivation and distinguish the numerical result from any proposed biological cause.

§8.4 Integrated investigation 8: Rapid-change monitoring with ethical limits

Research aim. Assess whether a simulated increase in frequency of an urban-associated trait reflects genetic evolution, immigration or phenotypic plasticity. The investigation is designed for an introductory undergraduate seminar or computational laboratory; its specimens, dates, counts and measurements are hypothetical. Do not represent any result as a published empirical discovery.

Materials, assumptions and ethical boundaries

Time-series allele counts, migration estimates, repeated phenotypes at paired urban and rural sites, and a short common-garden exercise performed on fictitious observations. Use a spreadsheet or local script to create tables and plots, recording pseudorandom seeds if stochastic simulations are used. Collect no personal genetic data. Do not culture pathogens, collect protected wildlife, disturb habitats or undertake medical tests. Where the real-world analog requires permits, trained staff or ethical approval, use simulation or openly available teaching data instead.

Step-by-step procedure

  1. For rapid-change monitoring with ethical limits, define biological units, sampling frame, time coordinate and outcome variables. Write the competing hypotheses as distinct predictions: Assess whether a simulated increase in frequency of an urban-associated trait reflects genetic evolution, immigration or phenotypic plasticity. A generic statement that 'evolution occurs' is not a testable outcome.
  2. Assemble the synthetic input table and verify totals. For counts, check whether each observation represents an individual, gene copy, fossil occurrence or independent lineage. For traits, specify scale and units. Leave missing observations explicitly missing rather than silently substituting zero.
  3. Produce a first visualization with individual values visible when practical. Include replicates, not only a pooled mean. Label assumptions, treatment groups and what uncertainty is shown; error bars without a stated definition are ambiguous.
  4. Recalculate the outcome after changing one defensible assumption: sampling effort, effective size, migration, branch rate or measurement error, as appropriate. The change in conclusion is a sensitivity result, not a failure of the exercise.
  5. Write a short report separating observation, model, interpretation and limitation. Include a figure caption, a reproducibility note and one proposed independent test.

Expected reasoning, not prescribed results

Population growth, allele-frequency change and phenotypic change are different quantities; none should be used as a proxy for another without justification. In this synthetic scenario, both trait values and census counts increase, yet the frequency of the focal genetic variant changes little once immigration is considered. Resist presenting a single calculated value as the only outcome consistent with the mechanism. Explain what a failed prediction would require you to reconsider: an incorrect method, an unrealistic assumption or a genuinely unsupported biological hypothesis. Negative results are informative when sampling was capable of detecting the predicted difference.

Troubleshooting the inference

Separate numerical evidence of an allele shift from the ethical or practical decision being considered. If a proposed intervention could cause harm, use synthetic data, published observations or supervised specialists instead of independent field experimentation. Pseudoreplication is a major threat when multiple measurements from one biological unit are treated as independent organisms or populations. Similarly, sampling several fossils from one well-exposed outcrop may not represent equal coverage across geological intervals. Identify the true replication unit and justify the comparator. If simulations use the same seed or parameter configuration, discuss whether the apparently repeated outcome is truly independent.

Submission and further questions

Submit a hypothesis table, methods summary, results figure with labeled axes, numerical worked example, explanation of assumptions and a 150-word limitations statement. What additional longitudinal evidence could justify a stronger claim of rapid local adaptation? For extension, describe one observation that would distinguish the leading hypothesis from a realistic competing mechanism, and state how much uncertainty would remain even if that observation agreed with the prediction.

§8.5 End-of-unit integrative assessment

  1. Construct a concept map linking Evolution of drug and pesticide resistance and Human evolution and shared ancestry. Each connecting arrow must be labeled with a measurable or historically constrained mechanism, not a vague phrase such as 'related to'. Include at least one causal hypothesis and one statement of historical relationship.
  2. Contrast Conservation genetics and genetic rescue with Evolution under rapid environmental change. Describe a plausible observational pattern compatible with both, then design a new measurement that would make their predictions diverge. State your relevant biological unit and justify how many independent replicates you would need for a preliminary test.
  3. Reproduce all five solved quantitative cases from this unit without viewing their answers. Change one parameter in each case, label units and explain whether the biological conclusion is sensitive to the change. A model calculation is not a field observation.
  4. Evaluate a hypothetical paper claiming that evolutionary medicine and mismatch has been conclusively demonstrated from a small unreplicated dataset. Draft a respectful scientific response explaining the most serious limitation, a rival mechanism, and a feasible next step.
  5. Prepare a 600-word mini-review integrating chronology or inheritance, mechanism and evidence for two topics of your choice. Define your terminology precisely, distinguish observed data from inferred history and cite a credible external scientific source. Close with a statement of what remains unknown.

Self-check rubric for this unit. When writing about evolution of drug and pesticide resistance or evolutionary medicine and mismatch, identify a defined population or lineage, give a defensible observation, show appropriate units or character coding and distinguish the leading hypothesis from a competitor. Evaluation rewards testable reasoning and candid limitations rather than memorization of a favored interpretation.
