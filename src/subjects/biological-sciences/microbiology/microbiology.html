§1.1 Unit overview

Unit 1: Microbial Diversity

Microbiology studies organisms and biological entities that are usually too small to characterize unaided, but scale is not a sound definition of an evolutionary group. Bacteria, archaea, microscopic eukaryotes and fungi differ profoundly in cellular architecture and descent. Viruses, which are acellular and require host machinery to replicate, are relevant to microbial ecosystems but deserve separate treatment. A reliable introduction distinguishes resemblance from homology, morphology from evolutionary identity, and the abundance of molecular markers from the abundance of living organisms. A single drop of natural water can contain countless interactions among organisms with very different metabolic abilities and ecological histories. Even a named species may contain physiologically diverse populations. Describing diversity therefore means specifying what is being compared, how the observation was made, and which conclusions the observation licenses.

§1.2 1.1 Bacteria: phylogeny, form and ecological diversity

Bacteria are a cellular domain whose members share a common ancestry but not one universal cell shape or lifestyle. Many bacteria have ribosomes, a cytoplasmic membrane, and a wall containing peptidoglycan, yet some lineages depart from the textbook rod-or-sphere image. Cocci, rods, curved cells, filaments and highly variable shapes show how cellular morphology responds to wall synthesis, division machinery, mechanical conditions and ecological selection. Shape can influence the relation between surface area and volume, but it is not a sufficient species diagnostic: unrelated lineages may converge on similar morphology. A shape seen under a light microscope is an observation; assigning that shape to a taxon usually requires additional evidence.

Modern bacterial classification combines conserved sequence comparisons, genomic similarity and phylogenetic inference with observations of phenotypes. A ribosomal RNA gene offers a useful historical marker because homologous sequences can be aligned across many lineages. It cannot, however, guarantee species-level separation: copy-number variation, intragenomic differences and nearly identical markers across ecologically distinct populations all complicate interpretation. Whole-genome comparisons can add resolution while introducing new assumptions about assembly quality, contamination and the threshold chosen for relatedness. The concept of a bacterial species remains a pragmatic combination of genomic coherence, evolutionary history and biological properties rather than a single mating-based definition copied from animals.

The ecological range of bacteria is wider than the familiar disease-associated examples. Oxygen-producing cyanobacteria transformed global element cycles; nitrogen-transforming bacteria contribute to soil fertility; heterotrophs mineralize organic matter; and numerous symbionts participate in nutrient acquisition. Calling every bacterium either beneficial or harmful misses context. A gut-associated population may contribute useful metabolites under one diet and expand opportunistically under another host condition. Ecological function is often distributed among strains and communities rather than uniformly shared by every member of a named genus. A functional gene detected in DNA also does not demonstrate that the gene was expressed or that a relevant biogeochemical flux occurred.

Consider a freshwater sample whose sequencing report assigns 260 reads to a putative cyanobacterial group and 740 reads to other groups. The relative assigned fraction is 260/(260+740)=0.26. That statement describes the sequencing library. It is not evidence that 26% of cells are cyanobacteria or that they account for 26% of photosynthesis: marker copy number, extraction efficiency, primer preference and cell size can all distort that conversion. Direct microscopy, pigment measurements and controlled rates of oxygen production could address different pieces of the ecological question. The discipline requires agreement among independent observations before a strong process claim is made.

A useful conceptual map separates identity, potential and activity. Identity asks which lineage a marker or genome resembles. Potential asks which metabolic pathways its genes could encode. Activity asks whether transcripts, proteins or reaction fluxes provide evidence that the pathways were operating at a specified time. A culture-independent sequence survey is especially powerful for identifying candidates for further investigation, but it cannot replace the later steps. Good scientific writing labels these layers rather than treating a taxonomic profile as a complete description of a microbial ecosystem.

§1.3 1.2 Archaea and the meaning of cellular domains

Archaea are not unusual bacteria, even though many archaeal cells are similarly small and may lack nuclei. Their information-processing systems, membrane lipid chemistry and evolutionary relationships justify treatment as a distinct domain. Archaeal membranes typically contain ether-linked isoprenoid chains rather than the ester-linked fatty-acid chains prominent in many bacterial and eukaryotic membranes. This is a broad comparative pattern, not a universal rule suitable for identifying every cell from a single lipid measurement. Some archaeal lipids span the membrane as tetraethers, which is relevant to membrane organization under certain environmental stresses. One should connect molecular structure to function without concluding that every archaeon is adapted to heat or extremes.

Research history initially emphasized conspicuous archaea from hot, saline or anaerobic environments. That sampling history created a misleading public picture in which archaea were simply specialists in extreme habitats. Marine planktonic archaea, soil-associated archaeal lineages and organisms involved in ammonia oxidation demonstrate a much wider distribution. Some archaea participate in methane production, a metabolism strongly associated with particular anaerobic lineages. Others oxidize ammonia or engage in diverse symbiotic relationships. There is no single archaeal metabolism any more than there is a single bacterial metabolism. Genetic and biochemical diversity must be mapped at the appropriate taxonomic scale.

Evolutionary comparisons reveal connections between archaea and the origin of eukaryotic cellular complexity. Many archaeal proteins involved in information processing resemble eukaryotic homologues more closely than corresponding bacterial machinery. Models of eukaryogenesis place archaeal and bacterial ancestors in a history of endosymbiosis and major cellular innovation. Specific branching relationships and the details of the transition remain subjects of continuing research. In a textbook argument it is appropriate to distinguish consensus-level conclusions, such as the bacterial ancestry of mitochondria, from particular hypotheses about the precise lineages and sequence of events involved.

Suppose a survey of 1,000 aligned homologous positions finds 60 mismatches between two archaeal marker sequences. The observed p-distance is 60/1000=0.06, or 6%. That is a legitimate descriptive quantity, not automatically 6% of evolutionary time. The observed fraction undercounts historical substitutions when sites have changed more than once. Gaps, uneven rates and poorly aligned positions add uncertainty. Evolutionary distance models attempt to account for these issues, but every model also has assumptions. The interactive distance plot in this unit deliberately shows the uncorrected calculation, so students can separate arithmetic from phylogenetic inference.

The domain framework is also an exercise in scientific humility. Many microbial branches were discovered from environmental DNA without an isolate grown in a laboratory. Such data provide strong evidence of genetic lineages while often leaving cellular ultrastructure and growth physiology poorly characterized. Genome-based predictions can identify plausible pathways, but validation of actual transformations requires biochemical or ecological measurements. A domain-level tree gives evolutionary structure; it does not supply a fully observed physiology for every branch represented in the tree.

§1.4 1.3 Fungi, yeasts and ecological decomposers

Fungi form a diverse eukaryotic lineage and include microscopic yeasts, filamentous molds and organisms with large multicellular reproductive structures. Their cells have nuclei and a membrane-bound compartment system. Many possess walls rich in chitin and other polysaccharides, features distinct from bacterial peptidoglycan. Some fungi grow as single cells, while others elaborate hyphae that extend through substrates and collectively form mycelial networks. The difference between a yeast and a mold can refer to growth form rather than a clean evolutionary division; a single species may exhibit different forms under different conditions.

Hyphal architecture helps explain why filamentous fungi are effective decomposers. A network of narrow filaments explores patches of material, secretes extracellular enzymes, absorbs soluble digestion products and reallocates resources. The process is not equivalent to an animal ingesting a food particle. Fungi transform carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus availability in ecosystems by breaking down plant tissue and other polymers. The detailed balance depends on oxygen availability, moisture, pH, substrate chemistry and interactions with competitors. A fungus visible on a food item is evidence of growth under those conditions, not a complete measure of food safety or spoilage chemistry.

Symbiosis adds another dimension. Mycorrhizal fungi associate with plant roots, often exchanging access to soil nutrients for plant-derived carbon. Lichens are persistent biological associations involving a fungal partner and one or more photosynthetic partners, frequently including algae or cyanobacteria. The outcomes are dynamic and depend on partners, habitat and resource constraints. Avoid the simplistic statement that a particular symbiosis is always mutually beneficial: costs of carbon supply or nutrient exchange may shift with environmental conditions. In ecology, the measured direction and magnitude of benefit matter more than a category label.

Fungal growth can be observed through several imperfect proxies: colony radius, dry mass, optical density of dispersed cells, substrate consumption or CO2 production. These quantities are not interchangeable. A filamentous colony may expand in radius while its biomass density changes; dry mass integrates living and nonliving material; gas production measures a metabolic flux instead of cell number. An experiment testing a nutrient hypothesis should specify a response variable and a control that isolates the candidate cause. Repeated independent biological samples are needed because one growing colony is not a population-level estimate.

Fungi also illustrate limits of human-centered taxonomies. Some yeasts are valuable in fermentation and genetic research; some molds make industrial enzymes; others interact with hosts or cause plant disease. A label such as 'microbe' does not say whether an organism is a prokaryote or eukaryote, pathogenic or nonpathogenic, aerobic or anaerobic. A careful case study names the organism and the experimental conditions before applying an ecological or biomedical conclusion. Microscopic diversity becomes intellectually useful when it is tied to mechanisms rather than lists of unfamiliar names.

§1.5 1.4 Protists, classification and the unseen diversity of water

Protists is a historical convenience term for many eukaryotes outside the groups traditionally called animals, plants and fungi. It does not describe one monophyletic lineage, so modern teaching should avoid presenting protists as an evolutionary kingdom with a single common defining feature. Photosynthetic microalgae, predatory flagellates, ciliates and numerous parasitic or symbiotic lineages differ in life history and cellular architecture. Some combine photosynthesis with ingestion of other organisms, a strategy often called mixotrophy. Others exist in morphological forms that change over the life cycle, complicating identification by any single microscopic snapshot.

A pond food web may feature photosynthetic eukaryotes capturing light, heterotrophic protists grazing on bacteria, and microbial recycling of nitrogen and phosphorus. The interactions operate across size scales and time scales. A grazing event transfers cellular carbon to a consumer, while incomplete digestion and excretion can return dissolved nutrients to the environment. Such recycling helps explain why the 'microbial loop' matters to aquatic production. Yet the direction and magnitude of its effects depend on temperature, predation, dissolved organic material and mixing. Describing one mechanism does not guarantee its ecological importance everywhere.

Microscopy remains essential to interpreting these systems. Bright-field imaging can show shape and movement, phase contrast can improve visibility of unstained structures, and fluorescence can identify particular cellular constituents if validated labels are available. Each image is a sample of a dynamic process. A flagellate that crosses a field of view might be responding to light, oxygen, prey or a mechanical boundary; movement alone does not reveal intent or mechanism. Identification by shape should be checked against reference images, life stages and, where appropriate, sequence data. An observer should document magnification, scale and the preparation method instead of relying on an attractive image without metadata.

A sequence alignment adds another kind of evidence. If two marker sequences differ at 30 of 1,000 positions, their observed mismatch fraction is 3%. The numerator records positions judged comparable after alignment; insertions and uncertain regions may have been omitted. The answer changes if the alignment strategy changes, which is why sequence preprocessing belongs in the methods section of a scientific report. Similar marker values do not prove ecological equivalence. Two near-identical organisms may differ in predator avoidance, nutrient requirements or associations with particular hosts.

The larger methodological principle is triangulation. Morphology informs cell design and visible behavior; molecular markers support lineage inference; biochemical or tracer assays reveal selected processes; and repeated environmental measurements test relevance in situ. Each observation has its own resolution and failure modes. The best identification is not necessarily the most technologically complex assay. It is the combination that answers the stated question with transparent uncertainty. This unit therefore ends with an operational distinction: a name is a hypothesis about identity, whereas evidence about metabolism, abundance and ecological role must be established separately.

§1.6 Guided investigation A: Designing a freshwater microbial diversity study

Imagine a university field project asking whether microbial diversity differs between two nearby ponds during the monsoon season. The question sounds simple, but 'diversity' could mean taxonomic richness, phylogenetic breadth, evenness, metabolic potential or direct functional activity. A project must choose its primary outcome before collecting data. Suppose it chooses marker-derived diversity and records water temperature, turbidity and nutrient concentrations as contextual variables. This choice deliberately limits the main claim to the groups represented by the marker and their recovered sequences. It does not promise a complete inventory of bacteria, archaea, fungi and protists.

Sampling design comes next. A single bottle from each pond cannot separate pond-level differences from local variation within the pond. Replication across sites, dates and sampling depths would allow some of that variability to be estimated. In a heterogeneous water body, surface blooms and bottom sediments may represent different environments. Mixing samples can be useful for a broad average but can also conceal habitat structure. The report should therefore preserve location, depth, date and environmental metadata for each sample and avoid claiming a pond-wide mean from an arbitrary collection point. A repeated sampling plan makes seasonal and spatial interpretation more defensible than one spectacular sequence profile.

Analytical controls are as important as field replication. Extraction blanks can reveal contamination introduced by reagents or handling; technical replicates can help identify analytical variability; a defined reference mixture can test aspects of classification performance. None replaces true biological replication because repeating an assay on the same material cannot show how much the ecological system varies. If a low-abundance organism appears only in one sample, the investigator should ask whether the observation is biologically credible, below detection consistency or associated with batch background. Read counts should be compared with suitable normalization and uncertainty, never treated as absolute numbers of cells without an independently validated conversion.

The hypothetical result might show a greater number of assigned lineages in pond A but a more even distribution in pond B. Which pond is 'more diverse' depends on the chosen metric. Species richness counts detected categories; Shannon entropy also responds to their proportions. Two communities can reverse rank under different metrics, so the author must report the method and biological question instead of selecting whichever result is more dramatic. If algal pigments differ, that complementary observation could indicate different photosynthetic communities, but it would not identify exactly which lineages caused the difference. Follow-up hypotheses could consider turbidity, light penetration and grazing without claiming these factors have been proved causal.

Finally, a well-written conclusion contains both what was learned and what remained unmeasured. It might report consistent seasonal variation in marker-derived diversity across replicated sites and a correlation with turbidity. It should not claim that one pond was healthier, carried fewer pathogens or had greater primary production unless those properties were directly studied. This case illustrates the discipline's central epistemic move: convert broad curiosity into a defined outcome, use observations suited to that outcome, and keep the strength of every conclusion within its evidential boundary.

§1.7 Guided investigation B: Building and criticizing a microbial phylogenetic hypothesis

Suppose an investigator has aligned homologous marker sequences from four environmental isolates and one carefully chosen outgroup. Before estimating a tree, the team must check that the selected sequence regions are comparable. An alignment with many low-confidence segments can create relationships determined by preprocessing choices rather than evolutionary history. The outgroup, when suitable, helps orient a rooted interpretation, but a distant outgroup may be difficult to align reliably. There is no automatic guarantee that the longest sequence or most familiar taxon makes the best reference.

One descriptive statistic is the proportion of aligned positions with mismatched nucleotides. If sequences A and B differ at 50 of 1,000 usable sites, their observed p-distance is 0.05. If B and C differ at 20 sites, p-distance is 0.02. Those values summarize differences, but pairwise distances alone do not reveal every branching relationship when evolutionary rates vary. A method for tree inference specifies assumptions about substitution processes, character independence and sampling. The researcher should document the method and quantify support for critical branches rather than present one drawing as historical fact.

Horizontal gene transfer creates additional complications. A marker can follow a history unlike that of the rest of a genome. A ribosomal marker often provides a useful broad frame, but a tree based on a metabolic gene may differ for biological reasons. Such disagreement may also reflect gene duplication, contamination or alignment error. A rigorous interpretation examines genomic context and independent evidence before claiming transfer. The tree is an inference from a particular set of characters; it is not a photograph of every ancestral event. These points are especially important in microbial evolution, where lineage divergence, extinction and gene movement can all leave partial traces.

To evaluate reliability, students can perturb assumptions conceptually. Would the topology change if the shortest sequences were excluded? Are the same groups recovered from an independent marker? Does one long branch attract a distant lineage under a simplistic method? Are likely contamination sources represented among the unexpectedly close relatives? These are not exercises in choosing the prettiest tree. They make visible the uncertainty that might otherwise be hidden by a branch diagram. A tree supported by multiple independent datasets and analyses is stronger than a tree drawn from one small alignment without quality control.

The final product should be a figure with taxon labels, a scale indicating sequence change, a statement of inference method and a clear distinction between supported groupings and uncertain nodes. It should avoid converting divergence directly into geological time without external calibration. When novel environmental DNA suggests an unusual lineage, the investigator can propose additional sampling and genome analysis while acknowledging that cell morphology and physiology may remain unknown. The honest outcome is a progressively tested evolutionary hypothesis rather than a premature species story.

§1.8 Research note: Resolving a conflict between taxonomy and metabolism

Imagine a sample in which a particular archaeal marker is abundant, while a measured metabolic process usually associated with some archaea is barely detectable. There is no necessary contradiction. The marker might occur in archaeal lineages without the relevant pathway; an organism may encode a pathway but not express it under the observed conditions; or the measurement may average an environment where only a minority of cells are active. Even if marker counts and process rates are measured with high technical precision, their relationship requires knowledge of lineage-specific function, biomass and environmental chemistry. A robust study would avoid calculating a per-cell transformation rate by dividing an ecological flux by an uncalibrated marker count.

There is also a distinction between historical classification and current function. Evolutionary descent constrains which molecular machinery is possible, but horizontal exchange, gene loss and regulatory changes can yield functional variation within close relatives. Assigning a metabolic property to an entire domain from one example is a taxonomic fallacy. A good teaching diagram might show three columns labeled lineage, genomic potential and measured activity, with arrows only where independent evidence connects them. Missing arrows express uncertainty, not a failure of science. This form of evidence mapping is especially useful when explaining the discovery of organisms known chiefly from environmental sequences.

§1.9 Research note: When a name is only a hypothesis

A genus assignment returned by computational software is not the same type of evidence as a cell observed performing a function. Reference databases contain gaps and taxonomic revisions; marker sequences may be shared by close relatives. An identifier should therefore be reported with confidence and method, while ecological claims require functional measurements. When two databases disagree, the defensible response is to investigate coverage, marker choice and classification criteria, not select the label that makes the narrative most impressive. The ability to leave a sequence unclassified is sometimes the most scientifically responsible result.

§1.10 Synthesis: from a marker to a biological explanation

A microbial taxonomic label is best treated as a claim supported by particular observations, not a synonym for metabolic function. Imagine that two water samples each yield one thousand classified sequence reads. The first contains a relatively large share assigned to a cyanobacterial group; the second has fewer reads from that group but a larger measured oxygen flux in the light. These statements are compatible because marker abundances, living biomass and metabolic activity are separate quantities. The first survey might reflect differences in gene-copy number, primer affinity or sampled cell sizes. The second measurement integrates contributions from organisms whose genetic markers were not recovered efficiently. To evaluate photosynthetic contributions, the researcher would connect simultaneous light-response measurements with biomass, composition and independent functional markers. A comparison that merely divides oxygen production by the fraction of classified reads would introduce an untested conversion factor.

A second example concerns archaeal sequence identification. Two markers that differ at sixty of a thousand aligned positions have an observed mismatch fraction of six percent, but a third marker might yield a different genetic distance. That discrepancy calls for examination of homology, recombination, sequencing error, alignment and evolutionary-rate assumptions. None of those issues are resolved by reporting extra decimal places. A rooted tree is an evidence-based historical hypothesis built from assumptions about sequence change. When a branch has weak support, the figure should communicate uncertainty rather than hide it in a single taxonomic name. Clear captions identify which sequences were used, whether the alignment removed ambiguous sites and how confident the investigator is in each grouping.

Morphology completes a complementary rather than subordinate picture. A microscope can show a dividing rod, a branched filament, a ciliated protist or a budding yeast, but it generally cannot reveal the whole genome or directly quantify a metabolic flux. A DNA extract can reveal marker sequences while losing spatial associations among cells. A rate measurement can show that a process occurs while leaving the responsible lineage unidentified. These instruments answer distinct questions. The most convincing microbial-diversity study plans how independent observations will constrain one another and predefines what pattern would contradict its favored ecological explanation.

§1.11 Quantitative investigations: interactive models

Sequencing read fraction

A controlled teaching model uses Reads classified in target group and Reads in other groups to predict %. Its stated relationship is 100 × a/(a+b). Read fraction is not the living-cell fraction; marker copy number and extraction bias are not represented. Compare the chart with the fractional count calculation; distinguish reads from cells. Use the paired reference curve only to compare the stated parameter choice; it represents a mathematical counterfactual, not biological replicate evidence.

Observed marker mismatch fraction

A controlled teaching model uses Mismatched aligned positions and Comparable positions to predict %. Its stated relationship is 100 × mismatches/sites. Uncorrected sequence p-distance; not elapsed evolutionary time and not a species threshold. Check why one additional mismatch has different influence at different alignment lengths. Use the paired reference curve only to compare the stated parameter choice; it represents a mathematical counterfactual, not biological replicate evidence.

Step-by-Step Microbiology Solutions

Three original questions connect calculations, definitions and interpretation. Open each solution to follow the reasoning.

Solved Problem Example 1.1: Read fractions versus cell fractions

A survey assigns 260 reads to group A and 740 to all other groups. Calculate its relative read fraction. A classmate calls it the percentage of living cells. Is that justified?

Step

Set the denominator to all assigned reads: 260+740=1000. This is the library total for the stated classification, not necessarily the total number of cells or genomes in the original sample.

Step

Compute 260/1000=0.26, which is 26%. The value is an estimate of the fraction of classified reads under the pipeline and sampling assumptions. It has no independent mass, biomass or cell-count units.

Step

Reject the unqualified living-cell claim. Extraction, marker copy number, primer bias, dead-cell DNA and classification error can all alter the translation from reads to organisms. A calibrated independent cell measurement would address that separate question.

Solved Problem Example 1.2: Observed sequence distance

Two homologous aligned markers differ at 60 of 1000 comparable sites. What is the observed p-distance and what inference would be invalid?

Step

The numerator is the number of mismatched aligned sites, 60. The denominator is the number of comparable aligned sites, 1000. These are sequence positions, not years of evolutionary history.

Step

Calculate 60/1000=0.060=6.0%. This is an uncorrected observed mismatch proportion. If 50 positions were excluded during alignment, those choices should be recorded before comparing results.

Step

Do not convert 6% directly to a speciation date. Multiple substitutions at one position, unequal rates and uncertain alignment can distort time inference. Use a specified evolutionary model and independent calibration for any stronger claim.

Solved Problem Example 1.3: Microscopy and identity

A pond micrograph shows a motile photosynthetic-looking cell. A DNA profile also contains many algal reads. Construct a defensible two-stage identification argument.

Step

List observations separately: cell shape, visible movement and apparent pigment under the chosen imaging conditions; algal marker reads from a pooled sample. The microscope and DNA data need not originate from exactly the same cell.

Step

Generate alternative hypotheses: a pigmented microalga, a mixotroph, a cell containing ingested chloroplast-bearing prey, or an optical artifact. Determine what each predicts in independent images and sequence observations.

Step

Conclude only that both microscopy and sequence data are consistent with algal activity in the sample. A direct cell-to-sequence connection, calibrated fluorescence and activity measurements would strengthen identification and metabolism claims.
