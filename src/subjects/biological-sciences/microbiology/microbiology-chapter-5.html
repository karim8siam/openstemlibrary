§5.1 Unit overview

Unit 5: Microbial Communities and Microbiomes

Microbial cells rarely function as isolated, identical units in natural environments. They share resources, compete for space, alter local chemistry and form relationships that can increase or decrease the performance of their neighbors. The word community describes co-occurring organisms, but a community profile does not automatically reveal which organisms interact. A microbiome study may characterize microorganisms, their genes and surrounding environmental conditions; its operational definition varies across projects. Sound conclusions therefore begin with explicit spatial boundaries, sampling design and measured variables. This unit connects surface-associated biofilms, mutualistic and antagonistic relationships, diversity statistics and the challenge of inferring causality from complex ecological data.

§5.2 Biofilm organization and gradients

A biofilm is a structured surface-associated microbial assemblage embedded in a matrix that can include extracellular polymers and material from the environment. It may contain one taxon or many. Attachment, growth, local production of matrix components and detachment can all contribute to community development, but the relative order and importance of these processes differ among systems. A cartoon showing a smooth sequence from single cells to a large biofilm is a teaching aid rather than a universally validated developmental program. Real surface communities form under variable flow, chemical gradients, physical disturbance and ecological interactions.

A central property is heterogeneity. Cells near a nutrient source may grow rapidly while cells deeper inside an aggregate experience depletion or accumulation of metabolic by-products. Oxygen can be consumed faster than it is replenished, allowing distinct chemical microenvironments to exist a short distance apart. This structure can sustain metabolisms that require different conditions. It can also invalidate measurements that treat all cells as being in the same physiological state. If a bulk sample displays an average growth rate, there is no guarantee that any individual cell is growing at exactly that rate.

Transport provides a physical framework. Diffusion redistributes material along concentration gradients; advection moves material with flowing fluid; chemical binding and biological consumption can alter both the rate and spatial pattern of penetration. Our model uses C(x)=C0 exp(−x/L) as a deliberately stylized attenuation relationship. With L=0.8 mm, the concentration fraction at x=0.8 mm is approximately e−1=0.368. This calculation does not separate reaction from diffusion or model clinical resistance. It illustrates why a depth-dependent measurement conveys information that a bulk concentration may conceal. A fitted attenuation length should always be interpreted for a specified solute and condition.

Quantifying a biofilm also requires care with operational definitions. Surface coverage estimates the area occupied in an image; biomass may be approximated by mass or optical staining; thickness is a spatial measurement; and viable-cell estimates are a separate biological quantity. A thick, optically dense matrix could contain fewer viable cells than a thinner community under different conditions. Imaging software can yield precise-looking percentages from threshold choices that are only weakly justified. Validation should include reference images, calibration and sensitivity checks on segmentation parameters. An attractive 3D reconstruction is not by itself a proof of a measured diffusion mechanism.

Biofilms matter in soil, rivers, engineered environments and host-associated surfaces. They help retain organisms in locations where nutrients arrive intermittently, and they can modify the chemistry encountered by later colonizers. The relationships are not uniformly cooperative. Some species consume shared resources faster than neighbors, while others release metabolites that neighbors can use. A description of 'the biofilm' should identify whether the claim concerns its matrix, a particular population, local chemistry or collective behavior. Different lines of evidence are needed for each of those claims.

§5.3 Symbiosis, cross-feeding and metabolic networks

Symbiosis describes close associations between different organisms, but its consequences range from mutual benefit to exploitation depending on the partners and conditions. In microbial communities, metabolic cross-feeding occurs when a substance produced or released by one population becomes a resource for another. This may arise through cooperative exchange or ordinary metabolic leakage; benefit to the recipient does not prove that the producer evolved to help it. Distinguishing intent-free biochemical consequence from an adapted exchange is essential to a rigorous evolutionary explanation.

Consider a simplified two-member community. Organism A transforms compound X and releases product Y; organism B consumes Y and may alter the concentration of another substance. Observing the disappearance of X and the appearance of B does not demonstrate the proposed pathway. A plausible mechanism requires a carbon or electron balance, independent identification of the product and evidence that the relevant population could perform the transformation. A community metagenome can reveal candidate enzymes, but gene presence alone is insufficient to assign measured flux. Isotope tracing or metabolite time series may provide stronger constraints when ethically and technically appropriate.

Mutualism must be measured relative to a comparison. If partner A grows faster in association with B than under a well-matched isolated condition, there is evidence of benefit to A in that environment. But the comparison can be confounded if B changes pH, oxygen levels or nutrient concentrations. Such environmental modification may be the mechanism or may reflect another variable. A broad ecological claim should distinguish what was directly observed from which interaction was hypothesized. An interaction can also shift across conditions: an organism providing a scarce vitamin may cease to be beneficial when the vitamin becomes abundant.

Competition is equally widespread. Populations can compete for limiting nutrients, light, electron acceptors or attachment sites. Some interactions involve secreted inhibitory substances, but competition need not require chemical antagonism. Occupying the same niche can reduce another population's access to resources. The strength of an interaction depends on population density, mixing and environmental renewal. Mathematical pairwise models often summarize effects with coefficients, yet those coefficients are context-dependent approximations. A coefficient measured in a simple system may be a poor predictor in a complex ecosystem where a third partner transforms the shared resource.

Ecological networks are models built from observations. A line connecting two taxa might mean co-occurrence, hypothesized metabolite exchange or a verified physical association; these meanings must not be conflated. Correlation networks from sequence profiles are particularly vulnerable to compositional constraints and shared environmental responses. If both populations prefer cool water, they may co-vary without interacting directly. Mechanistic inference becomes stronger when network predictions are compared with environmental covariates, temporal ordering and independent functional measurements.

§5.4 Microbiomes, sequencing profiles and diversity metrics

The term microbiome frequently refers to the community of microorganisms in a specified habitat together with their genetic repertoire or surrounding environment. Studies can differ on whether they include viruses, microbial eukaryotes, metabolites or only bacterial marker data. Because definitions vary, a report should state what was measured. Marker-gene surveys offer taxonomic profiles for selected lineages; shotgun metagenomics captures broader sequence content; metatranscriptomics samples expressed RNA under a particular moment; and metabolomics observes selected chemical products. None individually reports every relevant property of a living community.

Relative abundance is compositionally constrained: the fractions sum to one. If one group increases greatly in absolute abundance while others remain unchanged, their relative fractions can fall even without a true decrease in cell number. This is a major reason not to infer absolute population loss from a stacked bar chart alone. Additional measurements, such as calibrated cell estimates or spike-in approaches under validated assumptions, can help bridge that gap. Reporting only the percentage of reads also overlooks unequal marker copy numbers and biases introduced during collection and processing.

Alpha diversity summarizes variety within a sample, whereas beta diversity compares differences among samples. Shannon entropy H=−Σ p_i ln(p_i) incorporates both the number of detectable categories and the distribution of their proportions. For two groups at p=0.5, H=−0.5 ln0.5−0.5 ln0.5≈0.693 nats. At p=0.9, H≈0.325 nats, reflecting greater dominance. The statistic measures a particular feature of a defined categorical distribution; it is not a universal score of health, ecological stability or desirable function. The effect of rare organisms and detection thresholds must be discussed with any interpretation.

Beta diversity involves additional choices: whether to use presence-absence, abundance-weighted measures or phylogenetic relationships. Different metrics emphasize different biological questions and can yield differing impressions of separation between groups. An ordination diagram reduces complex distances to a few axes; the axes are visual summaries, not direct measurements of causal ecological variables. Visual clustering can also be affected by sequencing depth, batch effects and the choice of transformation. Robust analysis should check those sources before drawing ecological or clinical conclusions.

A single 'normal microbiome' is rarely a sufficient description across individuals, locations and seasons. Variation can reflect diet, environment, age, measurement technique and many other factors. A public claim that one microbial taxon indicates a particular health outcome requires substantially more evidence than a cross-sectional correlation. For scientific literacy, the critical skill is matching the breadth of a claim to the specificity and limitations of the sampled data.

§5.5 Community ecology, resilience and causal inference

Microbial communities change under disturbance, resource fluctuations and immigration. Resilience concerns aspects of recovery after change, while resistance concerns how much a community changes when challenged. These concepts require a defined response variable and time window. A return to a previous relative-abundance profile does not necessarily mean full restoration of ecosystem function. Different taxa may perform similar transformations, and an apparently stable community may experience large underlying turnover. Assessing resilience therefore often requires functional measurements alongside composition.

Ecological succession can occur as one population modifies conditions for another. For example, consumption of a readily available resource may enable later users of secondary metabolites, or an oxygen-consuming process may alter local conditions for other activities. Such sequences can create stable patterns without requiring purposeful cooperation. A time series showing one taxon rising after another is suggestive but not sufficient proof of facilitation. The timing could instead result from different growth responses to the same external factor. A mechanistic explanation compares competing hypotheses and identifies measurements that distinguish them.

Statistical power is particularly important in microbiome work. Thousands of genes or taxa can be tested for association, creating many opportunities for false positives. Appropriate multiple-testing corrections, predeclared outcomes and replication can reduce overinterpretation. Compositional transformations can improve particular analyses but introduce other assumptions, such as the handling of zero observations. Zero counts may indicate true absence, abundance below detection, extraction failure or insufficient sampling. Different explanations imply different statistical models and ecological interpretations.

Causal inference requires intervention evidence or natural experiments with defensible assumptions, not merely a network graph. If a microbial group is associated with a nutrient shift, it may consume the nutrient, respond to it or covary with another cause. A controlled experiment can sometimes isolate one of those possibilities, but a model organism or simplified community may not represent the full ecological setting. Real-world transfer requires attention to diversity, interaction complexity and environmental variability. It is scientifically sound to describe a reproducible association when the mechanism remains unresolved.

The unit's diversity simulation illustrates one measured property of a two-group composition. It allows a student to see how greater evenness changes Shannon entropy, but it cannot determine whether the community is productive, stable, harmful or beneficial. That distinction captures the broader principle of community microbiology: numerical summaries are useful precisely when their biological meaning and limitations remain explicit.

§5.6 Guided investigation A: A microbiome chart that seems to contradict itself

A study compares two river samples and reports that bacterial group A fell from 50% to 10% in the second sample. The investigator is tempted to conclude that A is disappearing because the environment became unfavorable. Consider the underlying counts in a hypothetical perfectly measured example: sample one has 100 A cells and 100 B cells; sample two has 100 A cells and 900 B cells. A's absolute abundance is unchanged while its fraction decreases substantially. This is not an unusual mathematical trick; it is a direct consequence of describing parts of a whole when the whole can change in size.

In amplicon sequencing, the problem is more complicated because read counts are not direct absolute cell counts. Each sample is generally represented by a finite number of reads, and the relative abundance of one taxon can change simply because another taxon yields more recoverable sequence. Marker copy number and extraction efficiency can differ by lineage, while library preparation can introduce preferential recovery. It is therefore incorrect to assume that dividing by total reads produces an unbiased estimate of true population fractions. Compositional constraints should guide which statistical tests and interpretations are defensible.

The Shannon diversity index illustrates another distinct question. At exactly two groups with proportions 0.5 and 0.5, H=0.693 nats. At proportions 0.1 and 0.9, H≈0.325 nats. Thus the evenness of the measured two-group composition decreases. This is compatible with A having the same absolute count as before. The index describes how the proportions are distributed, not why they changed or whether the ecosystem function improved. A system can maintain a metabolic transformation even while taxonomic composition changes if different organisms contribute similar functions, although that possibility must itself be tested.

To distinguish hypotheses, the study might incorporate an independently calibrated estimate of total cell concentration, measure microbial activity relevant to its ecological question and collect replicates across environmental conditions. If the river discharge increased, the amount of material carried through a section might change even when concentration did not. Sampling dates and water mixing must therefore be considered. A technical replicate of the same extracted DNA would not capture field variability, and more sequence reads would not correct an unrepresentative sample location. The uncertainty structure depends on the experimental design.

The final report should separate changes in relative composition, absolute abundance and ecosystem function in three different sentences with appropriate evidence. A graph that makes one category appear to shrink is not proof of population decline. This simple distinction protects against exaggerated interpretations of many public microbiome claims and gives a precise role to each additional measurement.

§5.7 Guided investigation B: What can an ecological network actually tell us?

Suppose a network diagram depicts two microbial taxa with a thick green line between them and a third taxon connected by a red line. It is natural to interpret green as cooperation and red as inhibition. Yet the meaning of the edge depends on how the network was constructed. If it derives from correlations among relative-abundance profiles, it demonstrates a statistical association under specified preprocessing choices. It does not independently show direct biochemical exchange, physical contact or causation. Color can make an uncertain association look more authoritative than the underlying data warrant.

A positive correlation may arise because both taxa respond to the same environmental feature. For example, two different populations could become abundant during periods of low oxygen without interacting directly. A negative correlation may arise from compositional closure: if one group consumes a larger fraction of a fixed sequencing library, other fractions necessarily become smaller. Different algorithms attempt to handle compositional effects but may return different networks. The investigator should report the method, threshold, sampling variation and robustness to reasonable preprocessing alternatives.

To test a proposed cross-feeding relation, the reasoning must move beyond correlation. Identify a candidate metabolite, a plausible producing pathway, a plausible consuming pathway and evidence of actual flux. A gene annotation alone supports pathway potential but not current activity. Chemical measurements showing a metabolite appears before a recipient grows are suggestive, yet both could respond to a third variable. Direct evidence of conversion or carefully interpreted tracer data would strengthen inference. The exact experimental form depends on biosafety, feasibility and study context; the important point is the independent chain of logic.

Network stability also needs a defined meaning. A robust pattern of co-occurrence across multiple seasons does not imply that the relationship is beneficial or even biologically direct. Conversely, a changing network may reflect shifting environmental conditions while key ecosystem functions remain stable. Replication across independent habitats can establish broader generality, but results from one environment should not be exported uncritically to another. Spatial scale is vital: a species pair sharing a whole pond may never occupy the same microscopic habitat.

A rigorous conclusion would describe which associations remained after environmental adjustment and explain which functional hypotheses they motivate. It would not label untested edges 'mutualism' or 'antagonism.' The primary achievement of a network analysis is to organize patterns for further study. Treating an exploratory visualization as a mechanistic map confuses the beginning of science with its endpoint.

§5.8 Research note: What does 'biofilm thickness' really measure?

A fluorescence image may suggest that a surface-associated community is fifty micrometres thick, but the estimate depends on the dye, optical resolution, segmentation boundary and whether extracellular material is visible. A different label might make the same community appear thinner by emphasizing living cells rather than the surrounding matrix. Thickness should therefore be treated as an operational measurement with method and uncertainty, not as a complete property of all components. A profile of oxygen or dissolved substrate can add functionally relevant information, but it does not automatically reveal which organism caused the chemical gradient.

An exponential attenuation model is useful for practicing quantitative interpretation because its characteristic length has a clear mathematical meaning. At distance L, the modeled concentration is e−1, about 36.8% of its surface value. At twice that distance it is about 13.5%. But a fitted exponential does not uniquely identify diffusion or reaction. Several physical and chemical processes can generate similar profiles, and measurements may not resolve narrow gradients. The correct scientific conclusion identifies the observed profile and its fitted parameter while leaving mechanism contingent on further tests.

§5.9 Research note: Higher diversity is not always higher function

A two-group Shannon index is maximal when the proportions are equal, yet a community with equal fractions could perform a desired transformation less effectively than one dominated by a specialist. A measure of evenness says nothing direct about reaction rates. The concept becomes particularly important when a diversity score is used as a marketing or medical claim. To evaluate function, investigators should measure the actual transformation or ecological outcome and ask whether observed diversity adds predictive value beyond other conditions. Statistical association may motivate a hypothesis, but ecological benefit requires a separate operational definition.

§5.10 Synthesis: communities are more than lists of taxa

Community ecology begins with a sampling frame. A profile of reads assigned to microbial groups summarizes the recovered sequence library, but it need not equal the true distribution of individuals. Extraction biases, differences in gene copy number, compositional constraints and environmental patchiness complicate comparisons. An abundance fraction always carries a denominator: reads classified, cells counted, biomass measured or flux attributed. Each choice answers a different question. Diversity indices combine some of these fractions into a scalar, but their interpretation depends on the classification level and the groups included. Shannon entropy is especially informative when interpreted beside richness and evenness, rather than presented as a universal measure of ecological health.

Biofilms demonstrate why spatial arrangement matters. Cells embedded in a matrix may experience concentration gradients of oxygen, nutrients and metabolites. An exponential attenuation curve with an adjustable characteristic length is only a compact mathematical illustration of spatial decline. It does not prove why the gradient exists. Diffusion, chemical transformation, uptake, adsorption and changing geometry can all contribute. A concentration measured at one depth cannot, by itself, identify the entire process. Imaging paired with chemical or physical measurements can test whether a proposed gradient model is useful, but model parameters should remain explicitly contextual.

Interactions make community behavior nonlinear. One lineage may release a metabolite that another consumes; the consumer may then change pH, substrate availability or the producer's growth rate. Competition and cooperation depend on resource budgets and environmental conditions, so the same two partners need not have a fixed relationship in every habitat. Host-associated microbiomes add further observational problems: diet, sampling time, medication and host physiology may covary. Comparing two microbial profiles is a starting point for hypothesis generation. A causal account requires appropriate controls, temporal or intervention evidence, and uncertainty about alternative explanations. These limits are an essential part of responsible microbiome interpretation.

§5.11 Quantitative investigations: interactive models

Ideal biofilm concentration attenuation

A controlled teaching model uses Depth (mm) and Attenuation length L (mm) to predict % surface value. Its stated relationship is 100 × exp(−depth/L). Exponential phenomenological shape; cannot determine diffusion versus reaction mechanisms. At a depth equal to L, compare to about 36.8% of surface concentration. Use the paired reference curve only to compare the stated parameter choice; it represents a mathematical counterfactual, not biological replicate evidence.

Shannon entropy of uneven community

A controlled teaching model uses Dominant group fraction and Number of equal minor groups to predict nats. Its stated relationship is −p ln p − (1−p) ln((1−p)/N). Other groups share the remaining probability equally; category resolution controls the index. Examine how evenness and the number of categories change the entropy. Use the paired reference curve only to compare the stated parameter choice; it represents a mathematical counterfactual, not biological replicate evidence.

THREE WORKED QUESTIONS

Step-by-Step Microbiology Solutions

Three original questions connect calculations, definitions and interpretation. Open each solution to follow the reasoning.

Solved Problem Example 5.1: Biofilm attenuation

A hypothetical surface community has C(x)/C0=exp(−x/L) with L=0.8 mm. Find the fraction at depths 0.4 and 1.6 mm.

Step

Depth and attenuation length must be in the same units. At 0.4 mm, x/L=0.5, giving exp(−0.5)≈0.6065.

Step

At 1.6 mm, x/L=2, giving exp(−2)≈0.1353. The deeper fraction is smaller because the model assumes monotonic exponential attenuation with distance.

Step

The calculation does not identify why the concentration falls. Diffusion, binding, transformation and consumption might all affect profiles. Never convert the fitted parameter into a clinical tolerance prediction.

Solved Problem Example 5.2: Two-category Shannon entropy

Calculate Shannon entropy for a two-group distribution with fractions 0.5/0.5 and compare it with 0.9/0.1.

Step

Use H=−Σp ln p, with natural logarithms. For equal fractions H=−2(0.5 ln0.5)=ln2≈0.6931 nats.

Step

For 0.9/0.1, H=−0.9 ln0.9−0.1 ln0.1≈0.3251 nats. Greater dominance reduces this entropy even though both examples contain two detected categories.

Step

Do not equate larger H with a healthy host or functioning ecosystem. The metric describes the distribution of measured categories and is affected by detection thresholds and compositional bias.

Solved Problem Example 5.3: Compositional change paradox

A sample initially has 100 cells of A and 100 of B. Later it has 100 of A and 900 of B. Compare absolute and relative changes for A.

Step

Initially A occupies 100/(100+100)=0.5, or 50% of the observed cells. Later its fraction is 100/(100+900)=0.1, or 10%.

Step

The absolute count of A is unchanged at 100 cells in this hypothetical perfectly measured system. Its relative abundance fell by 40 percentage points only because B grew.

Step

A sequencing chart showing a declining fraction for A cannot by itself demonstrate disappearance or reduced absolute abundance. Independent absolute quantification is needed for that inference.
