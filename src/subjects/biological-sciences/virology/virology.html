Unit 01 — Virus identity, structure and taxonomic reasoning

> **OpenSTEM Book 14, Virology.** Undergraduate editorial draft. Every numerical example and modeled curve uses fictional input. Expert review and publication approval pending. Do not treat these pages as laboratory protocols, clinical tools or instructions for manipulating viruses.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university virology courses. Eight units with five solved questions per unit and 16 interactive simulations illustrating viral plaque dilution counting, one-step replication kinetics, receptor saturation, clearance half-lives, and diagnostic predictive values. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§1.1 Learning objectives

A virus is best understood by distinguishing an extracellular object, a genetic information system and a series of contingent host interactions. Physical structure and classification provide useful labels, but neither determines human risk without substantial additional evidence. This unit connects matter, information and nomenclature while establishing measurement boundaries.

The five conceptual themes are definition versus observation; structure and function; taxonomy versus ecology; unit of measurement; uncertainty in classification. A reader completing this unit should be able to explain each of its five topics, compare at least two rival causes of a given observation, reproduce five quantitative answers, and critique a synthetic research design. Scientific maturity requires distinguishing the scale at which a fact has been measured from the scale at which an explanation is being proposed. If an observation describes nucleic-acid fragments, do not call it an infection count. If an outcome describes an immune-related marker, do not call it proven protection without validation.

§1.2 Foundations for evidence-based reasoning

Virology spans structural, cellular, ecological and clinical observations. A claim supported at one level may be unjustified at another. Check whether a measured signal is a proxy, a rate or a true outcome. Sampling time, location, group composition and denominators can change interpretation even when the plotted shape looks identical.

This unit uses models as tools for exploring assumptions. One parameter may be an arbitrary scale, while another represents a mathematical saturation point. Neither value should be mistaken for a calibrated property of a real virus. When interacting with a chart, first predict a direction, then alter one control, then explain the change in terms of the formula. Repeat with the second control and look for a limiting case. Observe how even a simple model can be illuminating for reasoning yet unsuitable for operational decisions. Reading the explicit warnings attached to each model is part of the exercise.

§1.3 Responsible research boundary

The descriptions are conceptual and pedagogical. No experiments requiring access to infectious material, clinical specimens, animals or specialized biosecurity facilities are needed or permitted by the lesson plan. Real research requires institution-specific ethical review, biosafety training and oversight. The synthetic case studies are not data collected from people, hospitals, laboratories or the environment. For work with real published material, cite the original source, verify context and licensing, and avoid reproducing identifiable records. Health, agricultural and public safety decisions should follow qualified professional guidance, not simplified student exercises.

§1.4 Topic 1.1 — What counts as a virus?

Mechanism and explanatory scope

Viruses are genetic elements that depend on cellular systems for multiplication, although the boundary between cellular life and viral agency depends on the question being asked. A virion is the extracellular package; an infection is a dynamic cellular process. Some infectious agents, including viroids and prions, do not meet ordinary virus definitions.

For definition versus observation, identify the unit of observation, the relevant timescale, and the step for which evidence is missing. A structural observation cannot alone prove a clinical or population-level effect.

Competing explanations

Calling every infectious object a virus confuses protein-only agents, subviral RNAs, and independently living microbes. The relevant comparison asks which chemical information is inherited and which replication resources must be supplied by a host. Whether something is alive is a philosophical framing rather than a test for detecting viral material.

Compare a biological change with a shifted sampling frame or instrument response. Connect the topic to host range, tropism and ecological specificity and state one observation that would differentiate the explanations.

Measurement and controls

Compare abstract descriptions of a particle, a naked circular RNA and a misfolded protein. Sort the examples by genome type, dependence on translation, and mode of inheritance. Record which observations would discriminate alternatives without growing or engineering agents.

Declare the outcome, unit, denominator, window and negative control. Technical repeats are not independent biological replicates; no fictional curve supplies a confidence interval or causal proof.

Limitations and connections

Definitions support classification, not a universal prediction of pathogenicity. The presence of viral nucleic acid alone may indicate residual material rather than a replicating infection.

Connect the topic to capsids, envelopes and virion architecture on an evidence ladder from molecules to populations. Name the missing assumption before moving between scales, and keep clinical or biosafety decisions outside this teaching model.

§1.5 Topic 1.2 — Capsids, envelopes and virion architecture

Mechanism and explanatory scope

Capsid proteins organize viral genomes into structures that may have icosahedral, helical or more complex symmetries. Some particles also possess host-derived lipid envelopes containing viral proteins. Physical form affects attachment, environmental persistence and exposure of structural epitopes, but virions are highly diverse.

For structure and function, identify the unit of observation, the relevant timescale, and the step for which evidence is missing. A structural observation cannot alone prove a clinical or population-level effect.

Competing explanations

A structural diagram is not evidence that every particle has the same stability or survives identical environmental conditions. Envelope status is associated with physicochemical differences, yet temperature, matrix, humidity and specific molecular composition strongly modify real outcomes.

Compare a biological change with a shifted sampling frame or instrument response. Connect the topic to what counts as a virus? and state one observation that would differentiate the explanations.

Measurement and controls

Use prepublished structural schematics or synthetic geometric models to compare symmetry and surface-to-volume ratios. Annotate scale bars, imaging uncertainty and the difference between an ideal capsid geometry and an experimentally resolved heterogeneous specimen.

Declare the outcome, unit, denominator, window and negative control. Technical repeats are not independent biological replicates; no fictional curve supplies a confidence interval or causal proof.

Limitations and connections

Do not infer the infectious dose of any virus from geometry. Visualized particles may include defective or noninfectious material, and microscopy without appropriate controls cannot establish viability.

§1.6 Topic 1.3 — Viral genome diversity and Baltimore groups

Mechanism and explanatory scope

Virus genomes may be DNA or RNA, single- or double-stranded, segmented or unsegmented, with positive or negative polarity for relevant RNA genomes. Baltimore groupings organize the route by which a virus produces translatable messenger RNA rather than providing a universal taxonomic genealogy.

For taxonomy versus ecology, identify the unit of observation, the relevant timescale, and the step for which evidence is missing. A structural observation cannot alone prove a clinical or population-level effect.

Competing explanations

Two viruses in the same Baltimore group need not be close evolutionary relatives or share an ecological niche. Conversely, a change in genome layout does not automatically imply a change in how every viral protein is synthesized.

Compare a biological change with a shifted sampling frame or instrument response. Connect the topic to capsids, envelopes and virion architecture and state one observation that would differentiate the explanations.

Measurement and controls

For several non-sequence schematic genomes, construct a conceptual information-flow diagram that identifies template, messenger RNA, translation and replication. Use arrows labeled by biochemical function without listing pathogen-specific sequence motifs or implementation instructions.

Declare the outcome, unit, denominator, window and negative control. Technical repeats are not independent biological replicates; no fictional curve supplies a confidence interval or causal proof.

Limitations and connections

Host polymerases and viral enzymes contribute differently across groups. A genome label cannot substitute for understanding where enzymes are packaged, expressed or recruited.

Connect the topic to ictv taxonomy, strains and naming conventions on an evidence ladder from molecules to populations. Name the missing assumption before moving between scales, and keep clinical or biosafety decisions outside this teaching model.

§1.7 Topic 1.4 — ICTV taxonomy, strains and naming conventions

Mechanism and explanatory scope

The International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses maintains a changing formal hierarchy for virus taxa. Taxonomic species, colloquial virus names, isolates, strains and clinically named variants are not interchangeable. Current formal names and placements should be checked against the latest master species list before publication.

For unit of measurement, identify the unit of observation, the relevant timescale, and the step for which evidence is missing. A structural observation cannot alone prove a clinical or population-level effect.

Competing explanations

The name most familiar from the news may describe a disease, a virus, or a historical group rather than the currently approved species taxon. A lineage assignment is an inference from specified evidence; it is not a standalone laboratory diagnosis.

Compare a biological change with a shifted sampling frame or instrument response. Connect the topic to viral genome diversity and baltimore groups and state one observation that would differentiate the explanations.

Measurement and controls

Construct a metadata table with columns for virus common name, taxon rank, release date, reference identifier and uncertainty. Compare an older scheme against the MSL41 release without claiming the nomenclature will remain fixed.

Declare the outcome, unit, denominator, window and negative control. Technical repeats are not independent biological replicates; no fictional curve supplies a confidence interval or causal proof.

Limitations and connections

Taxonomy is revised as evidence accumulates. Avoid interpreting a name change as proof of a sudden biological transformation; reclassification may occur without any observed change in an organism.

Connect the topic to host range, tropism and ecological specificity on an evidence ladder from molecules to populations. Name the missing assumption before moving between scales, and keep clinical or biosafety decisions outside this teaching model.

§1.8 Topic 1.5 — Host range, tropism and ecological specificity

Mechanism and explanatory scope

Host range concerns organisms in which some level of infection can occur; tissue tropism concerns the cell types or organs favored in a particular host context. Receptor availability, intracellular compatibility, immunity, anatomical access and ecology constrain these relationships. Entry is necessary for some pathways but is not by itself a demonstration of productive infection.

For uncertainty in classification, identify the unit of observation, the relevant timescale, and the step for which evidence is missing. A structural observation cannot alone prove a clinical or population-level effect.

Competing explanations

A cultured cell expressing an attachment receptor may bind particles while lacking the cellular environment for replication. Conversely, experimental uptake by an artificial cell model does not establish natural circulation among a species in the wild.

Compare a biological change with a shifted sampling frame or instrument response. Connect the topic to ictv taxonomy, strains and naming conventions and state one observation that would differentiate the explanations.

Measurement and controls

Draw a causal diagram separating contact, entry, replication, shedding and onward transmission. Evaluate a fictional host-range matrix as an inference problem, identifying where an unmeasured stage makes a positive signal ambiguous.

Declare the outcome, unit, denominator, window and negative control. Technical repeats are not independent biological replicates; no fictional curve supplies a confidence interval or causal proof.

Limitations and connections

Do not rank real species as transmission sources on receptor similarities alone. Host competence requires additional data and field context, and statements about zoonotic risk demand careful evidence.

Connect the topic to what counts as a virus? on an evidence ladder from molecules to populations. Name the missing assumption before moving between scales, and keep clinical or biosafety decisions outside this teaching model.

§1.9 Integrated synthetic research investigation: The difference between a particle count and an infection claim

Scientific question and competing models

This investigation tests how much can be inferred from limited numerical evidence. The first explanation is that virion index rises across categories. The rival explanation is that signal can rise through improved detection without a biological change. Each is formulated to account for a possible upward trend without supplying any actual biological or epidemiological measurements. The goal is not to make the plots resemble authentic virus experiments; it is to learn why similar-looking data can support different causal stories and how a discriminating observation improves a research plan.

The comparison must keep the outcome definition constant. Make a causal diagram with source, intermediate step, observed proxy and background variables. Place an unmeasured factor near the arrow it might influence, rather than listing confounders without indicating their pathway. A credible alternative explanation must be capable of producing a plausible observed difference. State what information would be required to refute each explanation rather than merely to generate an additional matching curve.

No uncertainty bands are supplied because there are no biological replicates. The table does not support p-values, confidence intervals or causal claims. Between stage zero and stage five, series A changes from 11.12 to 30.83; the difference is 19.71 index units. Series B changes from 12.5 to 21.77, a difference of 9.27 index units. These arithmetic descriptions are valid within the constructed table only.

A simple endpoint ratio, B over A, equals 0.706 if they are defined on comparable scales. A different normalization might change that ratio dramatically without changing the underlying plotted order. Plot both series with the invented stage index on the horizontal axis. It is incorrect to label the index in hours or days because the exercise deliberately supplies no physical time units. Try plotting the percent change from baseline instead, and explain why percent changes can exaggerate differences when the baseline is small.

Study design without pathogen experimentation

The most important planned independent measurement is: separate counting efficiency from any claimed functional state. The leading confound is instrument threshold and sample matrix. State how the independent measurement would behave under the two hypotheses and whether the proposed design could still produce the same answer under different mechanisms. Use only synthetic data, publicly documented nonidentifiable aggregate reports and pencil-and-paper reasoning. No live virus, infectious specimen, virus propagation, sequence engineering or physical containment procedure is authorized or required for this educational investigation.

Distinguish technical repeats, independent samples and randomized comparisons. An assay readout can be perfectly repeatable while its causal interpretation remains incorrect. Record denominator, missing observations and any change to the definition of a positive result.

Interpretation and competing-cause exercise

Evaluate the constructed series using three levels of claims. At the first level, directly report its numerical values. At the second, describe a statistical pattern such as monotonic rise, relative difference or saturation. At the third, discuss whether a particular biological mechanism is responsible, and explicitly state that the invented data cannot decide. A fourth level, practical intervention or policy, needs real outcomes and specialist scrutiny and cannot follow from these pages.

The strongest scientific answer may be that the present design is underdetermined: more than one account fits the observations. When the two rival hypotheses make identical predictions for the chosen outcome, collecting more of the same outcome alone may not resolve the ambiguity. Change the type of observation or the comparison structure. Write a paragraph describing a result that would contradict hypothesis one but be expected under hypothesis two, while refusing to treat the imagined result as if it actually occurred.

Six-part assignment and grading guide

  1. Write a one-sentence research question with a specified outcome and observation window (2 points).
  2. Draw a causal diagram with the two competing mechanisms and at least one confound (3 points).
  3. Calculate absolute and proportional changes from the synthetic table with correct labels (3 points).
  4. Explain how changing the sampling denominator could reverse a simplistic conclusion (3 points).
  5. Specify a safer, nonexperimental, computational distinction between the hypotheses (4 points).
  6. Write a short reproducibility statement with data provenance, invented status and three limitations (5 points).

Strong submissions separate calculation from explanation, use defensible language, and do not imply that a toy model proves a statement about an actual virus, patient, ecosystem or intervention. Weak submissions introduce unsupported numerical assumptions, hide their denominators or mistake plot smoothness for model validation.

§1.10 Unit synthesis: linking five concepts without forcing causality

The central lesson is that what counts as a virus?, capsids, envelopes and virion architecture, viral genome diversity and baltimore groups, ictv taxonomy, strains and naming conventions and host range, tropism and ecological specificity answer related but not identical questions. Combine them into an evidence map. In the first column, write what the object or process is. In the second, specify a directly observable variable. In the third, state what the observation fails to establish. In the fourth, propose a separate measurement or comparison that could discriminate two competing explanations. The ability to name what evidence does not support is as central to science as correctly calculating a number.

A worked critical-reading pattern

Start with a fictional headline claiming that a sample's measured index has doubled. Ask what the index measures and what unit was used. The number may describe a genome proxy, an assay output, a host marker or a reporting count. None is automatically a measure of an individual's contagiousness. Next, check whether the denominator is the same in the two periods. If collection criteria, instrument sensitivity, target population or data-processing rules have changed, the ratio may not compare like with like. Finally, decide whether alternative explanations predict different observable outcomes, and whether those outcomes were measured. You should be able to criticize the inference without asserting that the opposite explanation is necessarily true.

Build a two-stage evidence table: direct observations first, assumptions second. Note how a negative control or changed denominator could overturn a simple interpretation. Then decide which measurement would genuinely distinguish the candidate explanations.

Open-book examination problems

Essay A — causal chain. Choose the topic what counts as a virus? and write a diagram of five plausible links from observation to explanation. For every link, distinguish an observation from a proposed cause. Show where viral genome diversity and baltimore groups might supply a competing explanation. Write a 200-word note on which inference is least supported. An appropriate answer can include 'insufficient evidence' when the source cannot distinguish alternatives.

Essay B — dimensions and denominators. Compare the five numeric cases in this unit. Identify which quantities are percentages, unitless proportions, indices, rates or expectations. Explain why a probability cannot exceed one, while an expected number of events may exceed one. Show an extreme-value test for one formula and state why the result need not reflect any measured system.

Essay C — measurement design. Using host range, tropism and ecological specificity, choose a hypothetical data source and a misleading interpretation that could arise from changing its sampling frame. Specify a control, a sample unit, a reporting window and one independently measured corroborating variable. Make the design noninfectious, computational and ethically appropriate. Explain what a negative result would mean and why repeated technical observations might not establish reproducibility across biological systems.

Essay D — communicating uncertainty. Write two abstracts for the same invented table. The first should contain only direct numerical claims. The second may offer a possible mechanism but must label it as a hypothesis and describe its strongest competitor. Identify any phrase in the second abstract that could be mistaken for causal proof and revise it. This exercise values precise communication over confident-sounding conclusions.

Self-assessment checklist

  • I can distinguish presence, structure, biological activity, population dynamics and clinical outcome.
  • I can describe two plausible mechanisms for one invented pattern without assuming either is true.
  • I have correctly stated the denominator and units for each numeric answer.
  • I know which results are algebraic consequences of assumptions and which would require evidence.
  • I can identify where a synthetic model ends and where valid real-world inference would need to begin.
  • I can connect all five topics without implying every association is causal.

Complete the capstone investigation before referring to the interactive model sliders. A smooth curve that looks biological should be treated as a hypothesis generator, not as evidence. Keep a record of uncertain conclusions; the final unit revisits the same discipline in community and environmental virology.
