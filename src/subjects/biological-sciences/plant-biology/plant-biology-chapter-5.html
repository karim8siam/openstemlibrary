Transport and Resource Allocation

**Level:** Undergraduate plant biology · **Scope:** theory, evidence, data and experimental interpretation.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university plant biology courses. Eight units with exactly three solved questions per unit and 16 interactive simulations illustrating osmotic turgor, meristem cell division, stomatal conductance, light saturation curves, and xylem water potential. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition; it has not received independent academic peer review. Curriculum mapping does not imply university endorsement.

§5.1 Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, learners should be able to explain the four core topics, defend a prediction with a mechanistic model, identify measurement units, and evaluate at least one confounding variable.

5.1 Cohesion–tension

Core framework. This section develops evaporation-driven tensile forces, cavitation and hydraulic safety. In plant biology, structure and process cannot be separated: the same tissue changes its behavior with development, water status and environmental history. Start with observable features, specify what is measured, and only then choose a mechanism that could explain those observations. For cohesion–tension, distinguish a descriptive pattern from a causal explanation and state which comparison could falsify the proposed mechanism.

Mechanistic reasoning. Negative pressure in xylem water can support ascent but vulnerability to embolism limits performance. Connect this claim to three levels: molecules and membranes, cells and tissues, and the whole organism within its habitat. Identify the driving gradient or regulatory signal, where the resistance lies, and the time-scale of the response. This prevents a common mistake: interpreting a correlation as evidence that a single molecular route is sufficient to produce an organism-level result.

Worked scientific workflow. Sketch a soil–plant–atmosphere continuum and predict a drought-induced flow bottleneck. Record the experimental unit, the treatment, a matched control, a measured response and potential confounders. Collect baseline information first. Repeated measurements from one individual are not independent biological replicates. Present uncertainty and an alternative explanation even when the predicted pattern occurs.

Interpretation and limits. A measured effect may be altered by species identity, genotype, developmental phase, growth conditions or instrument calibration. Report the measurement's physical units and whether the result is a rate, a concentration, a flux or a probability. Do not extrapolate the qualitative teaching model into an agronomic forecast. A scientifically persuasive account connects the observation to a testable mechanism while keeping alternative explanations visible.

Practice prompts. (1) Draw a causal diagram for cohesion–tension. (2) Name two controls that would distinguish an environmental response from an ontogenetic effect. (3) Explain why biological replication and instrument calibration answer different questions. (4) Specify an outcome that would make you revise the initial hypothesis.

§5.3 Advanced investigation: transport pathways and source–sink dynamics

The physical nature of xylem and phloem flow differs

Xylem water moves through dead conductive tracheary elements under substantial negative pressure in many transpiring plants. Phloem carries sugars, amino acids, signaling compounds, and other solutes through living sieve elements connected to metabolically active tissues. In a pressure-flow account, loading near a source increases local phloem solute concentration, water enters from adjacent xylem, and the resulting pressure difference supports bulk transport toward lower-pressure regions where unloading occurs. Actual loading and unloading mechanisms vary across species and organs.

A developing fruit, actively growing root, or filling seed may be a strong sink; a photosynthetically mature leaf often functions as a source. Source and sink are functional roles rather than immutable organ labels. A young leaf can be a sink before it becomes a net exporter. A storage root can accumulate reserves in one season and mobilize them later. Girdling can help test the consequences of interrupting phloem in a stem, but it also injures tissue and can indirectly alter water and hormone relationships, so it is not a perfectly selective perturbation.

Isotope tracing requires disciplined inference

Pulse-label experiments can track the movement of labeled assimilates through a plant. Label appearance in an organ reflects a mixture of delivery, retention, metabolism, and pool dilution. A strong terminal label signal does not by itself demonstrate a higher instantaneous transport velocity. Sampling multiple times, normalizing to tissue mass, and accounting for initial source labeling improve interpretation. When the source organ changes its photosynthetic rate, source loading becomes a confounder.

Quantitative case: mass transfer rather than mere concentration

A hypothetical stem phloem sample has estimated sucrose concentration 0.50 mol L⁻¹ and sap volume flow rate 2.0 µL min⁻¹. If concentration and flow are approximately uniform, sucrose molar delivery is (0.50 mol L⁻¹)(2.0 × 10⁻⁶ L min⁻¹) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ mol min⁻¹ = 1.0 µmol min⁻¹. With sucrose molar mass near 342 g mol⁻¹, mass delivery is 0.342 mg min⁻¹. Concentration alone cannot establish a delivery rate; without independent velocity or flow-area information, mass flux is unknown. These numbers are illustrative, not direct field measurements.

Study design. Grow plants under matched conditions, define which organs are sources and sinks at the sampling stage, conduct a short labeled-carbon pulse where appropriate safety and facilities exist, and collect time-series observations from multiple biological individuals. Include untreated or sham-handled controls. Quantify source-specific labeling and tissue mass, and avoid confusing per-organ accumulation with fraction of total recovered label.

Diagnostic questions. Why is high sugar concentration not sufficient evidence of rapid export? What changes when a sink becomes a source? Which alternative effect complicates girdling interpretation? Why can phloem sap travel in different directions in different vascular bundles without violating the pressure-flow concept?

§5.5 Practicum 5: Signals, hormones, and growth

The question and why it matters

System: Auxin-dependent curvature in unilateral light. The central aim of this investigation is to move from a descriptive observation to a reproducible biological measurement. Experimental interpretations are strongest when the instrument output, the biological quantity of interest, and the causal claim are kept distinct. A quantitative value is useful only with its definition, units, assumptions and uncertainty. Many textbook diagrams intentionally omit these constraints; laboratory investigation is where those omissions become visible.

Experimental design

Germinate seedlings on moist substrate and orient the light source to one side in a light-tight chamber. Compare untreated seedlings, seedlings shielded at the tip, and seedlings with a translucent shield under otherwise comparable conditions. Photograph at a fixed interval with a calibration ruler. Trace curvature with a consistent geometrical definition. Avoid asserting an auxin-gradient measurement merely because bending was observed: auxin transport mechanisms require separate measurements or manipulations.

Before collecting data, write a hypothesis with a directional prediction, define an observation window, and select a primary outcome. Predefine exclusion criteria for damaged material and log departures from the protocol. Plan at least three truly independent biological replicates for a small teaching pilot, while recognizing that a formal sample-size calculation depends on expected effect and variability. Random allocation mitigates positional bias but cannot fix systematic instrument calibration error. Mask sample identifiers during image scoring if practical; if blinding is impossible, use automated measurements and a predeclared scoring rubric to reduce subjective decisions.

Worked quantitative interpretation

Suppose mean curvature angles after 6 h are 32°, 8°, and 29° in untreated, tip-opaque, and transparent-shield groups. The opaque cover is associated with a 24° lower mean than untreated. Without replicate counts and spread, this is an illustrative comparison only. Angle cannot be converted to differential growth rate without curvature geometry, initial shape, and tissue length.

A reproducible report should retain raw readings alongside any normalized value. If repeated values appear, show their distribution and the source of variability before constructing confidence intervals. Converting a measured number into a larger biological conclusion requires an explicit assumption chain. For example, a bulk gravimetric measurement does not resolve cell-level transport; a count of responsive cells does not measure pathway activation; an averaged population response does not characterize every individual. These distinctions prevent overstating the meaning of the calculated estimate.

Mistake to identify and repair

Does auxin always promote elongation? Its effect depends on tissue, concentration, developmental context, and hormonal interactions. Roots and shoots can exhibit different dose responses; a single arrow in a pathway cartoon is an incomplete physiological explanation.

The repair is to formulate two competing explanations and state a discriminating measurement. Draw a causal graph with the manipulated exposure, the proposed mediator, the measured endpoint, and at least one confounder. Separating a mechanistic hypothesis from its observable consequences is essential because several mechanisms can predict the same direction of change. When no available measurement separates the explanations, report the result as descriptive rather than mechanistically decisive.

Data record and quality controls

Record a unique replicate identifier; species, genotype and source where applicable; pretreatment growth conditions; measurement date; treatment assignment; instrument resolution; sample handling; and the raw endpoint. Use SI-compatible units and explicitly document any conversion. If a balance reads to 0.01 g, reporting a derived mass to six decimal places falsely suggests precision. Verify blank controls, out-of-range samples, and response saturation before averaging. If an instrument drifts over a session, record standards at both the beginning and end instead of simply assuming constancy. Archive sample exclusions with reasons, including exclusions made after seeing a result.

Distinguish technical replication, where one biological sample is measured repeatedly, from biological replication, where independently sampled individuals, populations, or experimental units are compared. Use the structure of the intervention to identify the correct unit for statistical analysis: if an entire container receives the treatment, the container is not interchangeable with each plant inside it. A design with many observations but few independent treated units can produce precise-looking yet misleading uncertainty estimates.

Extension challenge

Design a dose-response study that distinguishes increased auxin concentration from auxin redistribution; specify which measurements need to be spatially resolved.

Write a short report containing (1) a falsifiable hypothesis; (2) a labelled experimental diagram; (3) a raw-data table; (4) the result with correct units and one numerical uncertainty statement where replicate data allow it; (5) an alternative mechanism and a proposed follow-up test; and (6) a clear boundary between classroom illustration and generalizable biological inference. Include a paragraph on experimental ethics and local biosafety: ordinary plant work is low risk in many settings, but field collection permits, invasive species controls, nutrient-waste disposal and institutional guidance still matter.

§5.6 Quantitative research clinic: Source–sink movement and isotope-tracer logic

Phloem transport is often taught as movement from sources to sinks. In reality, organ identity changes with development: young leaves commonly import, mature leaves frequently export, and storage organs can reverse direction when they mobilize reserves. The pressure-flow framework links loading, osmotic water entry and hydrostatic pressure gradients, but the model alone does not reveal which compound moves or quantify its route through a specific plant.

Worked tracer accounting. Imagine a classroom isotope-tracer dataset in which 100 arbitrary units of a labelled carbon pulse enter a leaf. At a defined harvest, investigators recover 44 units in the labelled leaf, 28 in roots, 15 in stem tissues and 9 in other leaves. The detected recovery is 96 units, leaving 4 units unaccounted for. Reporting a 28% root allocation relative to the applied pulse differs from reporting 28/96=29.2% relative to recovered tracer. Either denominator can be appropriate if explicitly labelled. The missing fraction may include respiration, sampling loss or unmeasured pools; do not simply renormalize it away and pretend conservation has been verified.

The transport interpretation also depends on timing. A high stem label shortly after a pulse can signify a transient path rather than lasting storage. A single final harvest cannot distinguish rapid transport followed by turnover from slow transport followed by accumulation. Multiple destructive harvest times require different matched plants, not repeated harvesting of the same plant unless that design is feasible and explicitly tracked.

Alternative hypothesis. Reduced root labelling under drought might reflect reduced carbon export, altered root growth, changed respiratory losses or a modified pool of total fixed carbon. Pair tracer information with gas-exchange measurements and dry-mass estimates to distinguish a fraction from an absolute flux. When tracer work is impractical in a teaching lab, a transparent simulation can explore accounting logic without misrepresenting simulated distributions as measured biological evidence.

Solved Problem Example 5.1: controlled proportional model

A teaching experiment has a measured baseline of 11.2 units and a fractional change of 0.4. The task is to find the final value using the explicit assumption of proportional response. This is an arithmetic exercise, not a biological law.

Step

1. Define: baseline = 11.2 units; fractional increase = 0.4; both must refer to the same observable and experimental time interval.

2. Compute: final value = baseline × (1 + fractional increase) = 11.2 × 1.40 = 15.680 units.

3. Evaluate: the calculation assumes a linear proportional intervention without saturation. If biological feedback or resource limits operate, the measured final value can differ substantially. A valid test needs an independent control and biological replicates.
