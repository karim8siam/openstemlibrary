§5.1 Glycolysis and fermentation

Chemical mechanism and material constraints

Glycolysis converts one glucose into two pyruvate molecules through investment and payoff phases. The canonical net balance includes two ATP made by substrate-level phosphorylation and two NADH formed per glucose under the stated reaction accounting. Detailed balances also track water, inorganic phosphate, ADP and proton states; substrate carbon remains in two three-carbon molecules.

ATP consumption in early phosphorylation steps helps retain and activate the substrate; later ATP generation recovers the investment. Hexokinase, phosphofructokinase and pyruvate kinase occupy important control positions, but no single enzyme alone determines flux under all conditions.

Quantitative and experimental reasoning

In anaerobic fermentation, oxidation of NADH to NAD+ can sustain glycolytic flux by regenerating the oxidized cofactor. Lactic fermentation reduces pyruvate to lactate. Alcoholic fermentation in suitable organisms releases CO2 via pyruvate decarboxylation before reducing acetaldehyde to ethanol.

A stoichiometric yield such as two ATP per glucose is not a growth yield; maintenance energy, biomass synthesis and side reactions consume ATP. The number of ATP turnovers depends on flux and time, not just the algebraic reaction coefficients.

Limits of inference and neighboring pathways

To quantify a glycolytic rate, follow extracellular glucose disappearance together with lactate or ethanol formation across multiple time points. Normalize by viable biomass and quantify oxygen exposure. A net extracellular concentration difference can include transport changes and competing nutrient pathways.

Observe how inhibition of one step can alter upstream pools but leave another pool unchanged because of rapid regulation. Metabolomic snapshots alone do not identify every reaction rate, so pairing concentrations with time-resolved tracer experiments is essential.

Applied laboratory scenario

A yeast culture produces ethanol in a sugar-rich medium but shows a low dissolved-oxygen measurement. The investigators quantify glucose disappearance, ethanol appearance, CO2 release and biomass over a time course. They avoid saying that a single ethanol concentration proves anaerobic metabolism, because dilution, evaporation, respiration and storage of carbon in biomass can affect the balance. A mass-balance table compares carbon input with observed carbon products. The team repeats the experiment at several oxygen-transfer rates to determine how the route shifts with culture conditions. Net glycolytic ATP per glucose is a reaction coefficient, not a direct measurement of cellular ATP charge.

Counterfactual interpretation and discrimination

A culture takes up glucose and produces lactate, but the molar lactate output is less than twice glucose consumption. Carbon may be diverted into biomass, the pentose-phosphate pathway or other secreted products. Determine whether the relevant observation includes all culture phases and corrects for dilution. A theoretical glycolytic yield applies when the specified fraction of glucose proceeds through the canonical net pathway; it does not impose a fixed whole-cell lactate ratio under every growth condition. Isotope labeling can test the relative contribution of different carbon sources. A defensible mass balance includes all major carbon-containing outputs and clearly labels unmeasured terms.

§5.2 Citric acid cycle

Chemical mechanism and material constraints

Acetyl-CoA enters the citric acid cycle by condensing with oxaloacetate to form citrate. Two carbons are released as CO2 over the cycle, while reduced cofactors capture electrons for later oxidation. For each acetyl-CoA oxidized in the canonical net accounting, the cycle produces three NADH, one FADH2 and one GTP or ATP equivalent.

The two CO2 molecules evolved in one turn are not necessarily the same two carbon atoms newly introduced as acetyl-CoA. Carbon rearrangement and the sequence of decarboxylations mean isotope tracing is needed to assign carbon identity through early cycle turns.

Quantitative and experimental reasoning

Oxaloacetate is regenerated at the end of the idealized cycle, yet its pool can be depleted by biosynthetic withdrawal. Anaplerotic reactions replenish intermediates; cataplerotic reactions remove them. Thus cycle operation cannot be understood as a perfectly isolated closed loop in living cells.

Control of pyruvate dehydrogenase, isocitrate dehydrogenase and alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase couples carbon entry to energy state. High reduced-cofactor ratios can inhibit selected steps, causing substrate levels to change without any change in enzyme abundance.

Limits of inference and neighboring pathways

Quantify TCA function by measuring oxygen consumption under controlled coupling conditions and complementary stable-isotope label patterns in citrate, malate or glutamate. Oxygen consumption is linked to respiratory oxidation of reducing equivalents but is not a direct molecular count of TCA turns.

A diagram that labels all reactions “irreversible” oversimplifies the thermodynamic situation. Distinguish strongly driven net steps from near-equilibrium exchange and note that isotope exchange can persist even without large net flux.

Applied laboratory scenario

An isotope-labeled acetate pulse rapidly labels citrate but labels succinate more slowly. The time ordering is consistent with an upstream entry point, yet the pattern also depends on pool size and exchange. Researchers inspect isotopologue distributions in citrate, malate and glutamate, measure total metabolite concentrations and verify precursor enrichment. Oxaloacetate-related pools can change when intermediates are withdrawn for biosynthesis. The group avoids assuming that every newly introduced acetyl carbon leaves as carbon dioxide on the first turn. A model that distinguishes reversible exchange from net cycle flux is needed for a strong quantitative conclusion.

Counterfactual interpretation and discrimination

A citrate pool doubles after a treatment while an isotope pulse indicates slower replacement of existing citrate. Higher concentration and lower turnover can coexist when downstream utilization decreases. Measure upstream acetyl-CoA availability and downstream labeling to distinguish increased entry from reduced consumption. Isotopologue fractions must be interpreted alongside absolute pool sizes; enrichment alone can look slow in a greatly expanded pool. Note that a labeled acetyl group may exchange through pathways without completing a full TCA cycle. Any estimate of cycle flux therefore requires explicit assumptions about reversible exchange and anaplerotic reactions.

§5.3 Gluconeogenesis and reciprocal control

Chemical mechanism and material constraints

Gluconeogenesis synthesizes glucose from suitable precursors, including lactate, glycerol-derived carbon and some amino acids, primarily in relevant metabolic tissues. It is not the direct reversal of glycolysis because several glycolytic reactions are strongly driven in their physiological direction. Bypass enzymes provide alternative chemistry for those constraints.

Pyruvate carboxylase and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase help bypass pyruvate kinase, fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase bypasses phosphofructokinase, and glucose-6-phosphatase supports glucose release in tissues that express it. The overall conversion requires energetic input and suitable compartmental transport.

Quantitative and experimental reasoning

For synthesis of one glucose from two pyruvate, a commonly taught balance spends four ATP and two GTP, and uses two NADH under a defined substrate bookkeeping scheme. The exact overall physiological requirement varies with precursor and location; a lactate precursor alters the reducing-equivalent balance.

Whole-animal glucose output is not a direct readout of hepatocyte gluconeogenic flux. Glycogenolysis also contributes, and uptake by other organs influences plasma glucose concentration. Isotope dilution and suitable tracer methods can help separate sources.

Limits of inference and neighboring pathways

Analyze reciprocal regulation rather than treating glycolysis and gluconeogenesis as independent switches. Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate strongly affects the balance of PFK-1 and FBPase-1 activity, and hormonal signals adjust key enzyme states over different timescales.

A high intracellular pyruvate concentration need not indicate a high gluconeogenic rate because cofactors, enzyme activation, mitochondrial transport and product utilization can limit the conversion.

Applied laboratory scenario

A liver-cell experiment shows higher glucose secretion after a hormone treatment. That result alone cannot isolate gluconeogenesis because stored glycogen may supply released glucose. The team measures glycogen content before and after treatment, uses isotopic precursors to estimate newly synthesized glucose and records the energetic state. They inspect expression and phosphorylation of selected bypass enzymes over appropriate timescales. A control with a glycogen-depleted preparation can help separate sources, although it may alter cellular physiology. The interpretation therefore treats extracellular glucose as a combined output of multiple pathways rather than a direct readout of one enzyme.

Counterfactual interpretation and discrimination

A newly increased abundance of a gluconeogenic enzyme does not immediately prove an increased glucose production rate. Precursor supply, ATP/GTP availability, redox state and substrate transport may still constrain flux. Compare time-resolved enzyme protein with glucose output and tracer incorporation. A fast initial response before new protein synthesis is more consistent with post-translational or substrate-mediated control, while slower responses may involve expression. Neither timing pattern is unique, but the evidence can guide further tests. The report should distinguish enzyme capacity measured in extracts from actual carbon flux in intact tissue.

§5.4 Glycogen metabolism and pathway flux

Chemical mechanism and material constraints

Glycogen provides a polymeric reserve that can release glucose units through glycogen phosphorylase, primarily as glucose-1-phosphate from nonreducing ends. A debranching enzyme handles branch-specific linkages. Glucose-1-phosphate is converted to glucose-6-phosphate by phosphoglucomutase.

Glycogen synthase elongates chains using UDP-glucose, while branching enzyme transfers segments to create alpha-1,6 connections. Synthesis and breakdown are coordinated by covalent modification and allosteric signals rather than occurring as unregulated inverse reactions.

Quantitative and experimental reasoning

In skeletal muscle, glycogen-derived glucose-6-phosphate primarily supports local metabolism because myocytes typically lack the glucose-6-phosphatase system that enables direct release of free glucose into blood. The liver uses glycogen breakdown as one contribution to maintaining blood glucose.

Glycogen content and glycogen flux are distinct quantities. A tissue could have a relatively steady pool while simultaneous synthesis and breakdown occur, and a small pool might have high turnover if replenished rapidly.

Limits of inference and neighboring pathways

An enzymatic glycogen assay must account for competing free glucose, incomplete hydrolysis and sample normalization. Compare a hydrolyzed aliquot with an untreated aliquot and include a glycogen standard processed through the same extraction workflow.

The effect of one regulatory hormone depends on tissue receptor expression, phosphorylation state, energy demand and nutrient conditions. Avoid generalizing a single organ’s regulation to every cell in the body.

Applied laboratory scenario

A muscle extract has unchanged total glycogen before and after a 20-minute stimulation but produces more labeled lactate. One hypothesis is rapid glycogen synthesis and breakdown with a stable pool; another is greater glucose uptake. The group supplies distinguishable labeled external glucose, samples glycogen isotopologues and measures phosphorylase activity. The absence of a net glycogen change cannot eliminate a contribution from stored carbon. An enzyme assay normalized to protein concentration is compared with tissue-level metabolite flux to reveal the difference between potential catalytic capacity and actual pathway use.

Counterfactual interpretation and discrimination

Glycogen synthase activity is elevated in an enzyme assay, yet stored glycogen decreases over the following hour. The outcomes can coexist if degradation exceeds synthesis or if glucose precursors are unavailable in the intact cell. Measure phosphorylase state, glycogen carbon labeling and glucose transport alongside extract activity. A reaction-rate capacity measured under saturating substrate in vitro cannot be treated as the in vivo rate. For the overall pool, write dG/dt=vsynthesis−vbreakdown. This differential mass balance clarifies that a falling pool identifies only the difference between opposing fluxes, not either component separately.

§5.5 Conceptual introduction

Carbon metabolism can be understood as a set of transformations constrained by stoichiometry, enzymes and cellular objectives. A metabolic map appears to contain many arrows, but the arrows differ in meaning. Some indicate conversion of substrate carbon into products; some represent electron transfer or consumption of ATP; others denote regulation rather than chemical stoichiometry. To interpret a pathway, write balanced reactions for individual steps and then combine them, canceling intermediates that appear on both sides. The resulting overall equation describes net material conversion within chosen boundaries, not automatically the observable rate in a living cell.

Glycolysis offers a tractable example. The two three-carbon products, net two ATP and two NADH of the canonical conversion follow the sequence of investment and payoff reactions. Yet the fate of pyruvate depends on other pathways, oxygen availability and cellular compartmentation. Fermentation can regenerate NAD+ so that glycolysis continues when oxidative routes are insufficient for a particular organism or condition. The observation of lactate or ethanol in the surrounding medium does not by itself tell how much ATP was made. It combines production, transport, consumption and sometimes further chemical conversion.

Acetyl-CoA enters the citric acid cycle, whose net outputs include reducing equivalents and carbon dioxide. Students often imagine that the first carbon dioxide molecules released must be the carbons that entered most recently. Carbon rearrangements make this generally false during early turns, so tracing isotope positions produces a more precise explanation than simple entry-and-exit arrows. The intermediates also support amino-acid, lipid and other biosyntheses. When material is withdrawn, anaplerotic reactions can replenish the pool. A metabolic cycle is therefore not an isolated wheel turning without exchange with the rest of the cell.

Gluconeogenesis bypasses strongly driven glycolytic reactions to produce glucose from suitable precursors. It uses energy and appropriate enzymes, making a futile simultaneous full-speed cycle energetically undesirable. Regulatory signals influence the reciprocal balance between routes, though the balance is never a perfect global on/off switch. Glycogen adds a polymeric storage system with its own synthetic and degradative enzymes. The liver and skeletal muscle handle glycogen-derived carbon differently because their enzyme expression and physiological roles differ. This is a strong reminder that a statement about a pathway is incomplete unless the tissue and conditions are specified.

Flux measurements require more than metabolite concentrations. A pool of six millimoles can be renewed twice per minute or remain nearly static; one snapshot cannot distinguish those cases. Stable-isotope pulses, precursor labeling and timed product measurements add dynamic information, yet even tracer patterns depend on pool turnover and reversible exchange. Absolute flux estimates require a constrained network, identifiable parameters and measurement uncertainty. The practical aim is not to infer every cellular reaction at once, but to choose a limited number of mechanistic alternatives and design measurements that discriminate among them.

Metabolic stoichiometry remains a powerful first line of defense against impossible claims. If one glucose yields two pyruvates, products must carry the six original carbon atoms unless another carbon-containing substrate or product is included. A claim of generating unlimited ATP from a finite input ignores energy conservation. For any reported yield, ask what is counted: substrate converted, reducing cofactors formed, ATP generated by one pathway, ATP remaining after all demands, or growth per gram of nutrient. Their denominators differ and so do their biological meanings. Practicing this bookkeeping prepares students for the more complex accounting of fatty acids, nitrogen and membrane-coupled energy conversion.

§5.6 Quantitative model studio

Model 1: Glycolytic ATP balance

Glycolytic bookkeeping counts two net molecules of substrate-level ATP per glucose converted by the canonical pathway to two pyruvates. The teaching slider represents a quantity of glucose that passes completely through that net reaction, giving ATPnet=2×glucose input in matching mmol units. At 5 mmol glucose, the ideal net ATP formed is 10 mmol; this does not mean the culture’s ATP concentration rises by 10 mmol. ATP is also consumed by transport, maintenance, synthesis and other processes, while the pool may remain approximately steady despite high turnover. Fermentation can regenerate NAD+ without adding a new net oxidative-phosphorylation term to this simplified ledger. Students can compare the expected net ATP with the stoichiometric net formation of two NADH molecules and two pyruvates per glucose. A real experimental rate needs time: mmol substrate converted divided by duration. The model intentionally does not predict growth rate, oxygen consumption or energetic efficiency, all of which require additional reactions and conditions.

Investigation challenge: a culture consumes glucose at a constant rate but its measured ATP concentration is stable. Explain why this does not imply that glycolysis stopped. ATP is a rapidly cycling pool whose synthesis and consumption can be balanced. Use glucose flux in mmol per minute and convert the canonical net glycolytic ATP yield into mmol ATP produced per minute. Then compare that production flux with a hypothetical ATP demand from ion pumps and biosynthesis. If net demand exceeds this one source, other ATP-producing routes or consumption of stored nucleotide pools must compensate. The teaching chart supplies a stoichiometric production total for processed glucose, not the concentration of ATP remaining at any later moment.

Underlying relation: Net substrate-level ATP=2 mol per mol glucose; complete canonical glycolysis.

Model 2: TCA electron-carrier output

The citric-acid-cycle model counts three NADH molecules produced for each acetyl-CoA that completes the conventional cycle accounting. With 3 mmol acetyl-CoA, the modeled output is 9 mmol NADH, accompanied in the usual balance by 3 mmol FADH2 and 3 mmol nucleotide-triphosphate equivalent. Carbon atoms are not necessarily released in the sequence suggested by drawing only the first turn of a cycle, so isotope identity must be distinguished from stoichiometric totals. The linear relationship assumes a well-defined number of completed turns with sufficient oxaloacetate and required cofactors. Withdrawal of intermediates for biosynthesis can constrain throughput even when acetyl-CoA is abundant. An experimental investigation might combine labeled carbon tracing with NADH-sensitive optical signals, but oxidation of NADH by the respiratory chain may obscure net cofactor accumulation. Therefore a measured NADH concentration is not directly equal to the gross number of NADH molecules generated by the cycle.

Investigation challenge: calculate the canonical NADH production for several values of completed TCA turns, then ask why mitochondrial NADH concentration might fail to rise proportionally. NADH can be oxidized by downstream respiratory reactions while it is produced, causing a low or nearly constant pool despite a substantial production flux. Inhibiting respiration could change concentration without changing the nominal carbon stoichiometry of one cycle turn. Report NADH formation, NADH oxidation and the time derivative of its pool as separate variables. A simple conservation equation, dNADH/dt=production−consumption, clarifies why a measured snapshot cannot determine gross cycle turnover.

Underlying relation: Canonical three NADH per acetyl-CoA through the TCA cycle.

§5.7 Integrative fieldwork

Integrated investigation: a small culture receives a fixed input of glucose and the researcher wants to estimate the relative fractions reaching lactate, pyruvate oxidation and biomass precursors. Start with an explicit carbon balance: one glucose brings six carbon atoms. For each measured extracellular product, count carbon atoms per molecule and calculate the corresponding carbon equivalents. A higher lactate concentration is not automatically evidence of enhanced glycolytic throughput because lactate removal and medium exchange affect the pool. Use a known carbon-13 labeling pattern in the glucose input and collect time-resolved product isotopologues, along with absolute concentrations. If citrate becomes labeled, its carbon pattern must be interpreted through acetyl-CoA entry, oxaloacetate history and exchange reactions. Changes in glycogen stores represent another carbon sink or source and should be measured when relevant. The resulting flux map will have more unknowns than independent measurements unless additional constraints are supplied; state which quantities can be determined and which can only be bounded. The final report compares a simple stoichiometric model with the isotopic evidence and explains any mismatch by naming specific missing pathways or exchange processes rather than forcing all output into one presumed route. This is how reaction coefficients become a disciplined hypothesis about cellular metabolism.

THREE WORKED QUESTIONS

Step-by-Step Biochemistry Solutions

Three original questions connect calculations, definitions and interpretation. Open each solution to follow the reasoning.

Solved Problem Example 5.1: Glycolysis ATP yield

A culture converts 4.5 mmol glucose through the canonical glycolytic net stoichiometry. How many mmol net substrate-level ATP are produced?

Step

The net idealized stoichiometry is 2 mol ATP per mol glucose.

Step

Net ATP=2×4.5 mmol=9 mmol.

Step

This is a reaction balance, not the net ATP retained by the entire living culture.

Solved Problem Example 5.2: TCA carrier bookkeeping

How many mmol NADH and FADH2 emerge from the canonical citric acid cycle oxidation of 2.5 mmol acetyl-CoA?

Step

For each acetyl-CoA the usual TCA stoichiometry produces 3 NADH and 1 FADH2.

Step

NADH=3×2.5=7.5 mmol; FADH2=1×2.5=2.5 mmol.

Step

These carrier totals do not by themselves specify ATP production downstream.

Solved Problem Example 5.3: Gluconeogenesis ATP/GTP cost

In the simplified pyruvate-to-glucose gluconeogenic bookkeeping, what are the ATP and GTP costs for 1.5 mmol glucose?

Step

The simplified requirement per glucose is 4 ATP and 2 GTP from two pyruvate precursors.

Step

ATP=4×1.5=6 mmol; GTP=2×1.5=3 mmol.

Step

The route also needs reducing equivalents; precursor identity can alter the energetic balance.
