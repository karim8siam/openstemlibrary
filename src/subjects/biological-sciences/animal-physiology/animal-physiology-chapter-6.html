§6.1 What the unit asks you to explain

Animal Physiology — OpenSTEM Book 11. The path from feeding behavior to molecular absorption, fuel selection and heat production. All numerical case values in this book are synthetic teaching data, not measurements, clinical advice or husbandry recommendations. This editorial draft has not received independent peer review.

Physiology is a quantitative study of how living structures accomplish work and keep conditions within tolerable ranges. This unit examines digestion, nutrient exchange and bioenergetics while connecting molecular mechanisms to whole-organism outcomes. The goal is not only to identify a named structure or pathway, but to predict the direction of change, the time needed for a response, and the observation that would distinguish a proposed mechanism from an alternative. Learners should keep conservation of matter or energy in view whenever they write an equation.

By the end of this unit, you should be able to (i) explain its five linked topics in physical and biological terms; (ii) calculate and interpret five dimensioned toy examples; (iii) decide which measurements distinguish input, state and output; (iv) identify failure modes of the models; and (v) design an ethical comparison while naming confounding factors. Prerequisites are first-year biology, elementary chemistry, algebra, and an ability to read plots. Differential equations are discussed conceptually where useful but are not assumed.

§6.3 Integrated systems lens — Energy accounting must distinguish the boundary being measured

Food-label gross energy describes a resource offered to an animal, not automatically the energy retained after digestion. Fecal energy losses, urinary energy losses, methane production in some fermentative systems, metabolic heat, locomotion and tissue accretion occur at different stages. A careful energy ledger uses mutually exclusive categories and an observation interval. A study that reports only body-mass change may overlook shifts in water content and therefore misread an energy deficit.

Digestive tract specialization trades capacity, retention time, selectivity and transport cost. A fermentation chamber can convert complex polysaccharides into small molecules, but slower throughput may limit feeding under some conditions. Mechanical grinding increases surface area available for enzymes, while pH gradients protect enzyme specificity and tissue integrity. No one digestive strategy maximizes every possible food source or feeding schedule.

Enzyme saturation models are useful for separating affinity-related scale from maximal rate but are difficult to apply without modification to the whole gastrointestinal tract. Substrate concentration changes spatially and temporally; peristalsis alters residence time; endogenous secretions dilute the contents; and the epithelial barrier removes reaction products. The apparent uptake curve of an intact gut can therefore differ from the curve of a purified transporter or enzyme.

Microbial fermentation illustrates the difference between association and flux. Finding more DNA assigned to a particular microbial group after a fiber-rich diet does not prove it made more short-chain fatty acid or that the host absorbed more of that product. Composition, transcriptional activity, metabolite concentration and transfer into host circulation are linked but distinct layers. A causal hypothesis needs interventions and measurements across those layers while controlling altered feed intake.

ATP production is tied to muscle contraction, epithelial transport, secretion and thermoregulation. In exercise physiology the same measured oxygen consumption could accompany different fractions of energy used by each process. Fuel selection also changes with exercise duration and hormone state. A respiratory exchange ratio derived during rapidly changing breathing may reflect tissue CO₂ storage and ventilation dynamics, not only a stable carbohydrate-versus-fat fuel mixture.

In designing a feeding trial, randomize diet order when feasible, ensure nutritional adequacy and use species-appropriate welfare protocols. Establish whether biological replicates are animals, pens, tanks or repeated samples. If one treatment changes behavior and energy expenditure, an increase in apparent assimilation does not by itself establish increased growth efficiency. Report confidence intervals rather than treating small arithmetic differences as biologically meaningful.

§6.4 Five linked physiological topics

6.1 — Feeding strategies and digestive tract design

Biological mechanism and scope

Animals consume very different resources, from dissolved organic material to leaves, nectar or prey. Digestive anatomy reflects food quality, meal frequency, retention time and microbial partnerships. A short gut is not always a simple gut, and a larger digestive chamber is useful only when appropriate enzymes or symbionts can transform the ingested material.

Comparisons, exceptions and organism-level interpretation

Ruminants exploit fermentation before extensive small-intestinal digestion, while some hindgut fermenters place microbial conversion farther downstream. Birds may combine crop, glandular stomach and gizzard functions in different arrangements. Aquatic suspension feeders balance particle capture against the energetic cost of pumping water.

Observation, experiment and causal alternatives

Compare two hypothetical diets equal in gross calories but different in digestibility. Measure intake, fecal energy and body-mass change before inferring conversion efficiency. Feed composition affects water and mineral balance as well as available ATP, so a food's energy density does not tell the whole nutritional story.

Appropriate limits of the model

A tract drawing describes possible flow paths; it does not establish absorption efficiency or feeding performance in the wild.

Mechanism-to-evidence test

A reported change in feeding strategies and digestive tract design is not automatically a demonstration of the mechanism proposed to explain it. Draw a chain from the experimental input through a cellular or tissue intermediate to the whole-animal outcome. Next, name a competing pathway that could change the same final outcome, and predict a measurement that would distinguish the two explanations. Include when each signal should first appear, rather than measuring only the last endpoint. The relevant body compartment, temperature, developmental stage and experimental disturbance must be stated before generalizing. A simplified equation in this chapter is a transparent conditional calculation; it is not a biological law outside its specified assumptions.

Method: 100 × (gross − fecal energy) / gross. Substituting the given teaching values yields 70 %. Check that the dimensions agree before interpreting the result. Changing a numerator or denominator has a predictable direction only while the other inputs and model assumptions stay fixed.

6.2 — Enzyme kinetics and luminal digestion

Biological mechanism and scope

Digestive enzymes hydrolyze large polymers into absorbable products. Amylases, proteases and lipases operate on different chemical bonds, and their activity depends on pH, temperature, available surface and mixing. Secreted enzymes function alongside bile, motility and epithelial uptake, meaning in vivo digestion is a sequence of coupled processes rather than one isolated test-tube reaction.

Comparisons, exceptions and organism-level interpretation

An enzyme assay may show saturation as substrate concentration rises, but the gut simultaneously changes flow, secretion and substrate presentation. A high Vmax in vitro does not demonstrate that an enzyme is rate-limiting during a meal. Regulatory inhibition may protect tissue from premature enzyme activation or coordinate release with nutrient arrival.

Observation, experiment and causal alternatives

Construct a saturation curve from a dilute teaching enzyme reaction, calculate its half-maximal substrate concentration and check linear initial-rate conditions. Contrast these conditions with a gut lumen where substrate declines along the tract and more than one enzyme acts. Replicate measurements across pH rather than treating the optimum as universal.

Appropriate limits of the model

A fitted Michaelis–Menten curve approximates purified reaction kinetics; it is not a direct prediction of whole-animal food assimilation.

Mechanism-to-evidence test

A reported change in enzyme kinetics and luminal digestion is not automatically a demonstration of the mechanism proposed to explain it. 6.3 — Intestinal absorption and epithelial gradients

Biological mechanism and scope

Nutrients are absorbed across an epithelial barrier with a large effective surface area. Glucose and some amino acids can exploit sodium gradients maintained by basolateral pumps, while fatty-acid products follow different uptake and trafficking routes. Water and ion movement alter luminal concentrations, so uptake capacity cannot be inferred from concentration change alone.

Comparisons, exceptions and organism-level interpretation

Different intestinal segments express different transporters and encounter different substrates. Transport may become saturated, inhibited or downregulated over time. A luminal disappearance experiment can overestimate true assimilation if microbes transform substrate or if the compound is degraded before crossing into circulation.

Observation, experiment and causal alternatives

Compare transepithelial nutrient movement at low and high luminal concentrations using a transport-chamber model. Measure barrier integrity and distinguish flux per unit area from total absorption rate. A reduced flux might reflect fewer functional cells rather than a specific transporter defect.

Appropriate limits of the model

Absorption is a flux into the body; disappearance from the gut is not always equivalent to retained nutrient.

Mechanism-to-evidence test

A reported change in intestinal absorption and epithelial gradients is not automatically a demonstration of the mechanism proposed to explain it. 6.4 — Microbial fermentation and host–microbe exchange

Biological mechanism and scope

Microbial partners can transform fiber and other compounds that the host cannot efficiently digest enzymatically. Fermentation yields short-chain fatty acids, gases and biomass, which can change energy yield and luminal conditions. Microbes also interact with immune tissues and signaling molecules, so their significance is not restricted to calories.

Comparisons, exceptions and organism-level interpretation

A microbiome sequence dataset describes composition or genetic potential, not automatically the rate of fermentation or host absorption. Microbial diversity is not a universal scalar measure of health; a community's functional output depends on diet, host environment and species identity. Perturbing antibiotics or feed quality changes many variables simultaneously.

Observation, experiment and causal alternatives

Design a controlled diet-switch study with repeated measures of fiber intake, fermentation products and fecal energy. Match total calories and sampling time. Explain why a correlation between a bacterial taxon and weight gain does not prove that bacterium transfers energy efficiently to the host.

Appropriate limits of the model

Fermentation performance belongs to the host–microbe ecosystem and changes across diets and time rather than being a fixed property of a taxon.

Mechanism-to-evidence test

6.5 — Metabolic fuels, heat and energy expenditure

Biological mechanism and scope

Cells produce usable ATP by coupling the oxidation of carbohydrates, lipids and other substrates to metabolic pathways. Energy is conserved across work, chemical storage and heat release; an animal cannot turn all food energy into mechanical work. Basal, resting and field metabolic rates are measured under different conditions and should not be treated as interchangeable.

Comparisons, exceptions and organism-level interpretation

Temperature changes reaction rates but whole-animal metabolism involves active control and behavioral adjustment. In ectotherms, warming within a tolerated range often accelerates many physiological reactions, while endotherms may increase heat production in cold conditions. Fuel use depends on activity duration, hormones, diet and oxygen availability, not solely total metabolic rate.

Observation, experiment and causal alternatives

Estimate energy expenditure from an explicitly hypothetical oxygen consumption rate using a stated energy equivalent. Contrast the estimate with external work and calculate gross efficiency, explaining its dependence on baseline expenditure and measurement definitions. Never infer individual nutritional need from such a toy calculation.

Appropriate limits of the model

An energy budget must keep units and boundaries consistent: chemical intake, absorbed energy, work and heat have different definitions.

Mechanism-to-evidence test

§6.5 Integrative investigation — Fiber quality changes a hindgut-fermenter's energy budget

Setting. A herbivorous mammal switches gradually between two diets containing identical gross energy but different fiber accessibility. Its digestive microbes can ferment certain polysaccharides into metabolites that are available to the host. Retention time and gut movement also change, so the response cannot be assigned to one bacterial species or one enzyme.

Design. A controlled crossover design holds intake, age and activity as constant as possible while allowing washout. Measure ingested energy, fecal energy, body mass and selected fecal metabolites. The animal's own energy costs may change during adaptation, which makes short and long experimental windows scientifically distinct.

Evidence and alternatives. Suppose fecal energy falls after the switch: improved host digestion is only one explanation. Changed microbial biomass, different volatile losses or greater retention may contribute. A fecal sequence readout cannot establish the rate at which short-chain fatty acids enter host circulation.

Interpretation and transfer. Draw a Sankey-style conceptual energy ledger with gross intake, fecal loss, absorbed energy, urinary loss, heat and growth. Show why a diet with better apparent digestibility is not automatically better for reproduction or health. State which observations would support a microbial mechanism rather than only a nutritional association. The governing idea is conservation of energy across a complex community: metabolism redistributes chemical energy while host and microbes share some products and compete for others.

Reproducibility checklist. Declare the independent biological replicate and the direct measurement. Preselect a primary outcome, control environmental changes, and calibrate instruments. Preserve original data and computation steps rather than reporting rounded results alone. Compare the specific competing predictions before interpreting statistical significance. Respect animal welfare by using approved, appropriately noninvasive or existing datasets for educational comparisons.

§6.7 Working glossary and distinctions

The entries below state operational differences that matter when planning an experiment. Learn each term through a concrete measurement rather than memorizing a label in isolation.

Gross energy. Total chemical energy contained in food as determined under a defined measurement convention, not energy that the host necessarily absorbs.

Digestible energy. Dietary gross energy minus fecal energy loss, one stage of animal nutritional energy accounting.

Assimilation efficiency. Fraction of ingested matter or energy taken into the animal, distinct from conversion into growth or reproduction.

Foregut fermentation. Microbial breakdown preceding much host small-intestinal digestion, characteristic of several grazing strategies.

Hindgut fermentation. Microbial conversion in the posterior digestive tract, with distinct absorption and retention-time constraints.

Michaelis constant. The substrate concentration at which a simple Michaelis–Menten rate reaches half of Vmax under specified assay conditions.

Brush border. Apical surface specialization of absorptive intestinal cells with enzymes and transport proteins.

Sodium cotransport. Secondary active transport that couples movement of one solute to the energy stored in a sodium electrochemical gradient.

Motility. Coordinated muscular activity that moves and mixes gut contents, influencing enzyme exposure and absorption time.

Short-chain fatty acids. Small products of microbial fermentation such as acetate, propionate and butyrate that can participate in host metabolism.

ATP. A cellular energy-transfer molecule linking metabolism to transport, contraction and numerous biochemical tasks.

Aerobic metabolism. Cellular energy conversion linked to oxygen as the terminal electron acceptor in mitochondrial respiration.

Anaerobic glycolysis. ATP-generating glucose breakdown that can proceed without an immediate direct requirement for oxygen, with metabolic consequences.

Respiratory exchange ratio. Carbon dioxide output divided by oxygen uptake, informative for fuel balance under suitable steady-state assumptions.

Metabolic efficiency. A defined ratio of useful energy output to input, dependent on selecting consistent measurement boundaries.
