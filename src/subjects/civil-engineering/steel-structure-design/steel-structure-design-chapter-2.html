Tension Members, Net Area and Block Shear

Comprehensive university textbook unit covering fundamental theory, mathematical formulations, numerical modeling, and code-based design validation of Tension Members, Net Area and Block Shear. Developed for undergraduate civil engineering study.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Steel Structure Design study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§2.1 Intended learning outcomes

Intended learning outcomes

After this unit, a student should be able to construct a relevant free-body diagram, state the controlling simplifying assumptions, calculate an appropriate order-of-magnitude response, identify a competing limit state, and explain why a real building requires detailed verification under the governing jurisdiction's adopted rules.

§2.2 Design context

Design context

Analyze a tension plate with two alternative hole patterns. Calculate gross and two straight net areas and compare with an idealized force limit. Draw a block-shear path without inventing a code resistance. Discuss how a thicker plate, additional bolts and longer connection length would alter competing mechanisms; record why no one change improves every failure mode.

A productive way to study this unit is to connect its smallest material or member model to the whole building. Every force has an entry point and an exit point; every brace must connect to a supporting system; and every design resistance belongs to a particular structural limit state. The educational calculations in this book aim to train reliable modeling judgment, not to automate sign-off. When the calculation appears simple, spend additional time determining whether the assumed support, material response and loading state can actually occur.

§2.3 Gross yielding and tensile deformation

Lesson 2.1: Gross yielding and tensile deformation

Physical principle

The gross section describes the material area that carries average axial force away from discontinuities. Initial axial strain is P/(AE) for a prismatic elastic member, and total elongation is PL/(AE). This average deformation model cannot capture stress concentration near holes, abrupt section changes, or restraint-induced secondary bending.

Engineering method and interpretation

When examining a tension member, trace the force from the applied connection into the usable steel section. Separate gross yielding from net-section rupture: one may develop distributed plastic deformation while another concentrates fracture along the least resistant connected path. Neither check implies that the connection itself is adequate.

Worked engineering illustration

A plate 180 mm wide and 12 mm thick has Ag=2160 mm². With fy=355 MPa its gross section nominal yield force is 766.8 kN. This is a mechanical comparison force, not the code design resistance after factors and all other limit states.

Model boundary and field check

A member may be long enough that service elongation is unacceptable well before gross yielding. Check deflection and connection movement alongside ultimate strength.

§2.4 Net-section fracture and staggered holes

Lesson 2.2: Net-section fracture and staggered holes

Physical principle

At a bolted connection the net fracture path can be shorter than the gross width because holes remove metal. Where holes are staggered, the critical rupture path may turn between adjacent rows; the geometry correction and multiple candidate paths must be evaluated instead of simply subtracting the number of all holes from one transverse line.

Engineering method and interpretation

Map the bolt arrangement and candidate fracture paths with their actual hole dimensions, edge distances and pitches. A hole nominally specified by bolt diameter is often larger because clearance is required. Net-area approaches can also be reduced by shear lag if only part of a member is connected.

Worked engineering illustration

For a straight path crossing two 22 mm holes in a 240 mm wide, 14 mm plate, the net area is (240−44)×14=2744 mm². With an illustrative ultimate stress of 510 MPa, an ideal net-section force would be 1399.44 kN before applicable effective-area rules and design factors.

Model boundary and field check

Do not apply a stagger correction to an arbitrary candidate path without examining its geometry. A different path can still govern through a shorter net width or a block-shear route.

§2.5 Block shear and connection-region failures

Lesson 2.3: Block shear and connection-region failures

Physical principle

A connection region may fail along a three-sided path involving longitudinal shear surfaces and a transverse tension surface. That mechanism is different from uniform net-section fracture. The available path depends on bolt layout, edge distance, plate thickness and the actual load direction; a narrow end connection may create an unfavorable free edge.

Engineering method and interpretation

Sketch a proposed rupture block. Label gross and net shear areas separately from the net tension area, and check any holes intersecting each plane. Effective force transfer can depend on eccentricity, connection stiffness, bolt bearing and the sequence of load sharing. The teaching laboratory intentionally stops short of providing a complete block-shear strength formula.

Worked engineering illustration

Consider a plate with two rows of bolts and short end distances. Increasing bolt diameter can improve fastener strength while reducing the connected plate’s remaining net metal. Increasing plate width may improve one tensile path while doing little for a short longitudinal shear path.

Model boundary and field check

A high-grade plate connected with too few bolts can fail at the connection before the gross member is fully mobilized. The force path must remain continuous through every detail.

§2.6 Tension-member systems and serviceability

Lesson 2.4: Tension-member systems and serviceability

Physical principle

Tie rods, hangers, cables, truss members and bracing diagonals all carry tensile forces but differ in geometry, end conditions and the role of pretension. A tension-only element can become slack when load reverses. Adjacent braces may then become necessary to close the lateral force path.

Engineering method and interpretation

Use a whole-system model to determine which members are active in each loading direction. Confirm geometric compatibility: slackness, connection slip and thermal movement can alter the effective stiffness of a nominally axial system. For connection details, check the ability to inspect, replace and protect exposed tension members.

Worked engineering illustration

For a 3 m tie with area 1200 mm², E=200 GPa, and 150 kN tension, elongation is 150,000×3000/(1200×200,000)=1.875 mm. If the same tie must restrain a rotating mechanism, this displacement could be important even when stress remains modest.

Model boundary and field check

The truss model assumes pinned joints and axial bars. Real eccentricities, weld details or asymmetric connections may introduce bending and fatigue demands.

§2.7 Numerical case studies

Numerical case studies

Case 2.1: A straight net section through holes

Given: b=260 mm, t=12 mm, two holes d=22 mm, Fu=510 MPa.

Computation: An=(260−44)×12=2592 mm²; ideal force=1321.92 kN.

Interpretation: This ideal force is not design resistance and ignores stagger, shear lag, block shear and connection strength.

Independent audit: Before accepting the numerical answer, identify the dimension of the resulting quantity and confirm it matches the left-hand side of the formula. Replace the nominal input with an independently selected second input and repeat the calculation by hand. A single correct number can conceal an incorrect sign convention, wrong load level, or missing connection effect. State the physical phenomenon left outside the formula.

Case 2.2: Service extension of a long tension rod

Given: P=115 kN, L=7500 mm, A=980 mm², E=200000 MPa.

Computation: δ=PL/(AE)=4.40 mm.

Interpretation: Whether this movement is tolerable is a functional question; the yield-force check alone does not answer it.
