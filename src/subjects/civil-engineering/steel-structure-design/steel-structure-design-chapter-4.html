§4.1 Intended learning outcomes

Intended learning outcomes

After this unit, a student should be able to construct a relevant free-body diagram, state the controlling simplifying assumptions, calculate an appropriate order-of-magnitude response, identify a competing limit state, and explain why a real building requires detailed verification under the governing jurisdiction's adopted rules.

§4.2 Design context

Design context

Compare two beam sections with different strong-axis inertia and weak-axis properties under the same roof load. Calculate equilibrium actions, elastic deflection and the ideal lateral–torsional critical moment. Explain whether added flange bracing is more effective than increasing material grade, and separate serviceability from ultimate design.

A productive way to study this unit is to connect its smallest material or member model to the whole building. Every force has an entry point and an exit point; every brace must connect to a supporting system; and every design resistance belongs to a particular structural limit state. The educational calculations in this book aim to train reliable modeling judgment, not to automate sign-off. When the calculation appears simple, spend additional time determining whether the assumed support, material response and loading state can actually occur.

§4.3 Internal actions and elastic bending

Lesson 4.1: Internal actions and elastic bending

Physical principle

A steel beam under transverse load develops shear force and bending moment according to equilibrium. In Euler–Bernoulli bending, curvature κ=M/(EI) connects moment to elastic deformation. The sign conventions must remain consistent: the derivative of moment relates to shear, and the derivative of shear relates to distributed loading.

Engineering method and interpretation

Begin each beam problem with a free-body diagram and support reactions. Draw shear and moment diagrams before choosing a section: errors in tributary loads or support idealization are not corrected by increasing steel grade. Distinguish point loads, distributed loads, moving loads and applied end moments.

Worked engineering illustration

For a 7 m simple beam carrying 18 kN/m uniformly, each reaction is 63 kN, maximum moment is wL²/8=110.25 kN·m, and the moment passes through zero at both supports. The maximum shear magnitude occurs adjacent to each support.

Model boundary and field check

Combined loading should be handled by superposition only when the linear model is appropriate. Local load introduction, concentrated web bearing, vibrations and torsion may demand additional analysis.

§4.4 Bending resistance and cross-section behavior

Lesson 4.2: Bending resistance and cross-section behavior

Physical principle

A fully laterally restrained compact beam can develop substantial moment before global lateral instability. Yet flange and web proportions may limit plastic rotation or effective resistance. Elastic section modulus and plastic section modulus describe different stress distributions; one must not replace the other without checking cross-section classification.

Engineering method and interpretation

Determine which flange is in compression under each load case and where restraints exist. Restraints must have sufficient strength and stiffness and a reliable force path. In continuous beams the compression face changes between sagging and hogging regions, so a top-flange brace may not protect every segment.

Worked engineering illustration

An ideal elastic section with S=600,000 mm³ subjected to 150 kN·m has extreme fiber stress M/S=150×10⁶/600,000=250 MPa. That simple stress check does not establish a design bending resistance or adequate lateral–torsional stability.

Model boundary and field check

Web shear, flange local buckling, lateral–torsional buckling, bearing, fatigue and serviceability are independent potential checks. A beam is adequate only if the relevant limit states are all met.

§4.5 Lateral–torsional buckling and bracing

Lesson 4.3: Lateral–torsional buckling and bracing

Physical principle

An unbraced compression flange can translate sideways while the beam twists, creating a lateral–torsional instability. The elastic critical moment depends on weak-axis stiffness, torsional stiffness, warping resistance, unsupported length, end conditions and bending-moment distribution. Loading above the shear center can alter the instability tendency.

Engineering method and interpretation

Trace each lateral restraint to its support path and verify the compression flange is actually restrained where the design assumes it. Roof purlins do not automatically provide a brace if their connections, diaphragm action or own stability are inadequate. During erection the member may be unbraced even if a slab will restrain it later.

Worked engineering illustration

In the ideal uniform-moment model, decreasing unbraced length increases the calculated critical moment. The sensitivity is stronger than a simple linear proportionality because torsion and warping terms scale differently with length. Explore the laboratory as a trend study, not as a stand-alone code check.

Model boundary and field check

AISC and Eurocode use different resistance procedures and parameters. Do not transfer an elastic Mcr from an illustrative graph directly to a design-strength comparison.

§4.6 Deflection, vibration and serviceability

Lesson 4.4: Deflection, vibration and serviceability

Physical principle

Users of a building observe movement, floor vibration and visible sagging long before ultimate member resistance is reached. For a simple prismatic beam under uniform load, maximum elastic deflection grows with the fourth power of span. Thus extending a span by 20% increases deflection by a factor 1.2⁴≈2.07 when all else is fixed.

Engineering method and interpretation

Specify which load combination and serviceability criterion applies, including occupancy, sensitive finishes, equipment and construction tolerances. Consider the composite action of concrete floor slabs only if the interface is designed and detailed. Connection slip and long-term effects can depart from the ideal model.

Worked engineering illustration

For L=8 m, w=12 kN/m, E=200 GPa and I=220×10⁶ mm⁴, δmax=5wL⁴/(384EI)≈14.55 mm. The beam might have adequate ultimate bending strength while its deflection still requires evaluation against project-specific limits.

Model boundary and field check

A simple static deflection calculation does not predict floor vibration response. Modal frequency, mass, damping and forcing dynamics belong to a separate model.

Interpretation: These results follow equilibrium and do not include lateral–torsional buckling or concentrated load effects.
