Structural Systems, Bracing, Industrial Trusses and Frames

Comprehensive university textbook unit covering fundamental theory, mathematical formulations, numerical modeling, and code-based design validation of Structural Systems, Bracing, Industrial Trusses and Frames. Developed for undergraduate civil engineering study.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Steel Structure Design study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§7.1 Intended learning outcomes

Intended learning outcomes

After this unit, a student should be able to construct a relevant free-body diagram, state the controlling simplifying assumptions, calculate an appropriate order-of-magnitude response, identify a competing limit state, and explain why a real building requires detailed verification under the governing jurisdiction's adopted rules.

§7.2 Design context

Design context

Analyze a symmetric roof truss for vertical load and a reversed uplift scenario. Identify which members switch from compression to tension. Develop a brace-connection schedule highlighting members that require compression stability, fatigue, or alternate load-path review. Distinguish erection and completed configurations.

A productive way to study this unit is to connect its smallest material or member model to the whole building. Every force has an entry point and an exit point; every brace must connect to a supporting system; and every design resistance belongs to a particular structural limit state. The educational calculations in this book aim to train reliable modeling judgment, not to automate sign-off. When the calculation appears simple, spend additional time determining whether the assumed support, material response and loading state can actually occur.

§7.3 Roof trusses and space-frame load paths

Lesson 7.1: Roof trusses and space-frame load paths

Physical principle

Ideal pin-jointed trusses convert nodal loads into axial forces. Their economy depends on geometry, node placement, compression-member stability and joint construction. A long diagonal in compression may govern overall member size despite carrying a smaller axial force than a compact tension chord.

Engineering method and interpretation

Construct a joint free-body diagram and distinguish chord, diagonal and vertical members. Use equilibrium to determine axial signs before selecting a member. Secondary bending may arise where roof loads enter between panel points or where actual connection lines fail to intersect at a node.

Worked engineering illustration

For two symmetrical roof struts inclined at 35° to horizontal and supporting a 125 kN apex load, each ideal axial compression is P/(2 sin35°)≈109 kN. The corresponding horizontal reaction magnitude is Fcos35°≈89.3 kN at each support, balanced by the tie or support restraint.

Model boundary and field check

Roof diaphragm behavior, wind uplift, buckling of top chords and temporary erection stability are separate system checks; the two-bar laboratory is not a full truss design.

§7.4 Braced frames, concentrically and eccentrically braced systems

Lesson 7.2: Braced frames, concentrically and eccentrically braced systems

Physical principle

Diagonal braces increase story lateral stiffness by mobilizing axial deformation. Compression diagonals may buckle while tension diagonals remain effective, so cyclic response depends on the specific bracing concept and detailing. An eccentrically braced system may intentionally use a yielding link but requires specialized design rules.

Engineering method and interpretation

Trace diaphragm shear to collectors, brace nodes, base plates and foundations. Identify brace-force reversal for wind or earthquake loading, and examine the additional vertical or horizontal forces introduced into columns and beams. Check that architectural openings do not interrupt an assumed continuous bracing line.

Worked engineering illustration

Suppose lateral shear of 300 kN is resisted by one ideal brace at 45° with no other contributions. The axial force magnitude needed to provide 300 kN horizontal component is 300/cos45°≈424 kN. The vertical component at the joint is also about 300 kN and must enter the adjacent frame load path.

Model boundary and field check

Neither this single-bar equilibrium nor a static elastic analysis captures brace buckling hysteresis, connection fracture, seismic detailing or energy dissipation.

§7.5 Industrial frames, gantries and crane actions

Lesson 7.3: Industrial frames, gantries and crane actions

Physical principle

Industrial structures frequently carry repeated lifting loads, equipment vibration, impact, thermal forces and construction-stage loads. A gantry or crane runway introduces concentrated moving wheel reactions, lateral surge, longitudinal braking and fatigue-sensitive details that differ from ordinary office floor loads.

Engineering method and interpretation

Separate the operating states: parked equipment, maximum lifted load, braking, wheel loads at critical positions and maintenance conditions. Track each load through runway beams, brackets, columns and foundations. Investigate local web bearing, support fatigue, torsion and service deflection.

Worked engineering illustration

A simply supported runway beam subject to a single centered 80 kN wheel load on a 6 m span has ideal peak moment PL/4=120 kN·m, before other actions and dynamic amplification. Moving the wheel changes reactions and moment at specific sections.

Model boundary and field check

Use the applicable machinery, crane and structural standards. Wheel impact factors cannot be invented from a generic textbook rule for a real design.

§7.6 Robustness, redundancy and staged construction

Lesson 7.4: Robustness, redundancy and staged construction

Physical principle

Structural robustness is not equivalent to checking every element at nominal gravity load. Progressive failure can start when a local connection or support is lost and load redistributes through alternate routes. Some systems benefit from ductile continuity; others need explicit ties, redundancy or compartmentalization.

Engineering method and interpretation

Review critical members and connections for accidental actions where applicable. Model realistic erection sequence and brace installation timing. Temporary conditions can have different load combinations and less lateral restraint, so construction engineers must provide a stability plan consistent with the method statement.

Worked engineering illustration

Imagine a roof braced bay is omitted during assembly. The final global frame model may predict a stable triangulated system, but the incomplete system can behave as a four-bar mechanism with very low in-plane stiffness. No increase in section yield strength repairs the missing geometric restraint.

Model boundary and field check

A robust detail is one that can be built, inspected, maintained and understood under unusual loading. Robustness is a system property, not a single strength ratio.

§7.7 Numerical case studies

Numerical case studies

Case 7.1: Triangular apex truss forces

Given: Apex load P=180 kN, symmetric bars 40° to horizontal.

Computation: Each compressive bar force=180/(2sin40°)≈140 kN.

Interpretation: Joint horizontal reactions need a tie or lateral support path; compression instability remains unchecked.

Independent audit: Before accepting the numerical answer, identify the dimension of the resulting quantity and confirm it matches the left-hand side of the formula. Replace the nominal input with an independently selected second input and repeat the calculation by hand. A single correct number can conceal an incorrect sign convention, wrong load level, or missing connection effect. State the physical phenomenon left outside the formula.

Case 7.2: Illustrative lateral force in a braced bay

Given: Story shear V=200 kN; one brace at 50° to horizontal.

Computation: Axial brace force≈200/cos50°≈311 kN.

Interpretation: The additional vertical joint-force component must reach beams and columns, not disappear from the model.
