Flood Frequency Analysis, Extreme Values and Hydrologic Risk

Comprehensive university textbook unit covering fundamental theory, mathematical derivations, computational hydraulics, and engineering design validation of Flood Frequency Analysis, Extreme Values and Hydrologic Risk. Developed for undergraduate civil engineering study.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Hydrology study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§6.1 Case study: Regulated river flood frequency

Case study: Regulated river flood frequency

A 38-year annual-maximum record includes two periods with different upstream reservoir operations. Pooling them yields a mean and standard deviation, but the stationarity assumption is not established. The 100-year quantile may be convenient to plot yet scientifically ambiguous without a treatment of regulation and uncertainty.

Investigation task. Fit a classroom Gumbel estimate, then write two conflicting explanations for the apparent tail change and identify evidence distinguishing them.

The unit as part of a watershed investigation

This unit asks an engineer to defend tail inference as a quantitative argument rather than a number inserted into a generic formula. The natural control volume is the selected hydrologic subsystem and its actual inflow and outflow boundaries. Write the unit of every parameter and specify the temporal support of the observations; an hourly intensity, a daily depth and an annual volume cannot be substituted for each other without integration. A concept sketch should show the site boundaries, measurement stations, surface and subsurface pathways, relevant human infrastructure and the time interval over which the process operates. Interpret the final result relative to the real decision at regulated river flood frequency, not merely against an arbitrary calculator threshold.

Field evidence, scale and uncertainty

The most important practical complication in flood frequency and risk is that model inputs are estimates of spatially and temporally varying states. Two instruments reporting the same variable may not have comparable averaging periods or calibration. The impact of an uncertain coefficient depends on the derivative of the output with respect to that coefficient and on where the operating point falls relative to a threshold. If a predicted response appears robust, deliberately test a neighboring process that the simple model omits: channel blockage, antecedent saturation, high tailwater, sensor drift, groundwater exchange or a different storm shape. Record failures of the model openly, rather than editing the inputs until a visually pleasing curve appears.

Professional inference standard

An educational simulation is useful when it generates a falsifiable prediction. Before using any of the four lessons to make a design claim, identify an independently measurable output, a second approximation with different assumptions and a failure consequence. For regulated river flood frequency, a field campaign might require time-synchronized precipitation and flow observations, a georeferenced inventory of hydraulic structures, separate dry- and wet-weather site visits and a provenance ledger for every parameter. The exercises below are intentionally synthetic and no parameter set is asserted to describe a real Bangladesh, US or other watershed. Local codes and agency-approved hydrologic methods supersede these teaching screens.

§6.2 Peak-flow records and annual maxima

Lesson 6.1 — Peak-flow records and annual maxima

Hydrological mechanism

Flood frequency inference begins with a consistent definition of independent events and a trustworthy streamflow series. Annual maxima and peaks over threshold support different statistical models; treating multiple correlated peaks as independent biases inference. Changes in river regulation, land cover and gauge rating may make a long record nonhomogeneous. The engineering objective is not merely fitting a smooth curve but establishing what the fitted tail is allowed to represent.

Governing expression and dimensions

Teaching relation: AEP=p and return period T=1/p for a stationary event definition. Every term must be traced to its physical definition before numerical evaluation. A hydrologic depth expressed in millimeters corresponds to one thousand cubic meters for every square kilometer of area. A discharge expressed in cubic meters per second becomes volume only after integrating over seconds. A rate reported over a nominal day or hour is not a spatial average by itself. State whether the equation is a conservation balance, idealization, calibrated empirical law or fitted statistical relation; that classification determines what evidence is needed before transfer to a new catchment.

Units and source audit. For peak-flow records and annual maxima, list the observed quantities, estimated coefficients and chosen reference conditions separately. Include the dates and methods used to obtain each. Calculate one reference state using all quantities in compatible units and round only after a dimensional check. Then reverse a sign convention or convert to a different set of units and verify that the physical meaning survives. A unit-consistent equation may still be wrong because its assumptions do not match the setting.

Applied decision: Evaluate the sensitivity of a flood estimate to a newly discovered rating change

The central decision is not whether a formula can be evaluated; it is whether the value carries information sufficient for an engineering action. In this situation, first identify the outlet, observation period and decision variable. Establish a low, central and high forcing scenario using values that can be defended by measurements or explicitly labeled hypotheses. Predict how the response changes before running the model. A prediction that contradicts conservation of water, plausible timing or an independently measured constraint requires investigation even when the graph looks smooth.

For this particular case the dominant caution is that censored historical observations and mixed populations can distort the sample. Assess which boundary condition is most likely to be violated. For an event analysis, compare the timing of inputs and outputs; for a seasonal balance, separate fluxes from storage; for statistical estimates, distinguish frequency assumptions from observed counts. Do not claim a coefficient calibrated in one environment is universal. State one missing measurement and the practical difficulty of obtaining it.

Mechanism checks and falsification

Counterfactual A — scale. Suppose the area or response time is changed while keeping some input depth or intensity fixed. Decide whether the output should scale linearly, sublinearly or nonlinearly, and explain which physical assumption controls that conclusion. For peak-flow records and annual maxima, the relevant limiting cases include a nearly dry condition, a very large forcing and a coefficient approaching the edge of its documented range. A model that produces negative water depth, impossible storage or negative coefficients without a clear warning is not fit for student use.

Counterfactual B — observation. Ask how a stage recorder, pluviometer, soil-moisture probe or properly maintained flow measurement could disprove the chosen interpretation. Compare two alternatives: an error in the input and an error in the process structure. Error in a timestamp may alter the inferred lag without changing storm totals; an unmodeled flow diversion may alter volume without a rainfall error. Use these fingerprints to choose the next data collection action.

Cross-topic connection. This lesson connects to extreme-value screening and gumbel quantiles because a model transfers its assumptions through the wider analysis. It also affects uncertainty, nonstationarity and decision thresholds, which may rely on the same boundary records but at a different temporal or spatial resolution. A strong report shows these dependencies explicitly, identifies the quantities shared by two calculations and avoids treating correlated errors as independent measurements.

§6.3 Extreme-value screening and Gumbel quantiles

Lesson 6.2 — Extreme-value screening and Gumbel quantiles

Hydrological mechanism

Gumbel quantiles provide a transparent introduction to an extreme-value distribution. A method-of-moments fit translates a sample mean and standard deviation into location and scale, then computes a quantile for an assumed annual exceedance probability. This is only a classroom comparison, not a universal choice for regulated flood estimates. Tail uncertainty, skewness, outliers and the adopted regional method can be decisive.

§6.4 Flood hazards, exposure and consequences

Lesson 6.3 — Flood hazards, exposure and consequences

Hydrological mechanism

Flood hazard describes physical conditions such as inundation depth, velocity, duration and sediment loads. Exposure counts people and assets potentially affected; vulnerability concerns how they respond. A high-flow probability by itself is not a social risk estimate. Levee overtopping and floodplain encroachment may create threshold consequences, so mapping probability without failure pathways can be misleading.

Sensitivity analysis explores local response; ensembles and scenarios explore a wider range of plausible conditions.
