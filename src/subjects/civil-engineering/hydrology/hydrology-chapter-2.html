Precipitation, Hyetographs and Design Storm IDF Curves

Comprehensive university textbook unit covering fundamental theory, mathematical derivations, computational hydraulics, and engineering design validation of Precipitation, Hyetographs and Design Storm IDF Curves. Developed for undergraduate civil engineering study.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Hydrology study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§2.1 Case study: Short-burst storm in a market town

Case study: Short-burst storm in a market town

Three stations register 82, 110 and 61 mm in a 6-hour storm. Polygon area weights are 0.25, 0.45 and 0.30, producing 88.3 mm basin-average rainfall. A radar product suggests the strongest 30-minute burst fell between stations, exposing the difference between storm total and sub-hourly intensity.

Investigation task. Compute the arithmetic mean and the weighted mean and explain which missing measurements control uncertainty in peak drain loading.

The unit as part of a watershed investigation

This unit asks an engineer to defend areal and temporal rainfall as a quantitative argument rather than a number inserted into a generic formula. The natural control volume is a delineated watershed and its observational network. Write the unit of every parameter and specify the temporal support of the observations; an hourly intensity, a daily depth and an annual volume cannot be substituted for each other without integration. A concept sketch should show the site boundaries, measurement stations, surface and subsurface pathways, relevant human infrastructure and the time interval over which the process operates. Interpret the final result relative to the real decision at short-burst storm in a market town, not merely against an arbitrary calculator threshold.

Field evidence, scale and uncertainty

The most important practical complication in precipitation and design storms is that model inputs are estimates of spatially and temporally varying states. Two instruments reporting the same variable may not have comparable averaging periods or calibration. The impact of an uncertain coefficient depends on the derivative of the output with respect to that coefficient and on where the operating point falls relative to a threshold. If a predicted response appears robust, deliberately test a neighboring process that the simple model omits: channel blockage, antecedent saturation, high tailwater, sensor drift, groundwater exchange or a different storm shape. Record failures of the model openly, rather than editing the inputs until a visually pleasing curve appears.

Professional inference standard

An educational simulation is useful when it generates a falsifiable prediction. Before using any of the four lessons to make a design claim, identify an independently measurable output, a second approximation with different assumptions and a failure consequence. For short-burst storm in a market town, a field campaign might require time-synchronized precipitation and flow observations, a georeferenced inventory of hydraulic structures, separate dry- and wet-weather site visits and a provenance ledger for every parameter. The exercises below are intentionally synthetic and no parameter set is asserted to describe a real Bangladesh, US or other watershed. Local codes and agency-approved hydrologic methods supersede these teaching screens.

§2.2 Point and areal precipitation

Lesson 2.1 — Point and areal precipitation

Hydrological mechanism

A rain gauge samples precipitation at one location, whereas a basin-average storm estimate integrates spatially variable rainfall. Arithmetic averaging, Thiessen weights, isohyets, radar adjustment and bias correction are different representations with different assumptions. A spatial average can preserve water volume while losing the short intense burst that controls local urban drainage.

Governing expression and dimensions

Teaching relation: P_A=sum(w_i P_i) where sum(w_i)=1. Every term must be traced to its physical definition before numerical evaluation. A hydrologic depth expressed in millimeters corresponds to one thousand cubic meters for every square kilometer of area. A discharge expressed in cubic meters per second becomes volume only after integrating over seconds. A rate reported over a nominal day or hour is not a spatial average by itself. State whether the equation is a conservation balance, idealization, calibrated empirical law or fitted statistical relation; that classification determines what evidence is needed before transfer to a new catchment.

Units and source audit. For point and areal precipitation, list the observed quantities, estimated coefficients and chosen reference conditions separately. Include the dates and methods used to obtain each. Calculate one reference state using all quantities in compatible units and round only after a dimensional check. Then reverse a sign convention or convert to a different set of units and verify that the physical meaning survives. A unit-consistent equation may still be wrong because its assumptions do not match the setting.

Applied decision: Estimate storm rainfall for a three-gauge rural subcatchment

The central decision is not whether a formula can be evaluated; it is whether the value carries information sufficient for an engineering action. In this situation, first identify the outlet, observation period and decision variable. Establish a low, central and high forcing scenario using values that can be defended by measurements or explicitly labeled hypotheses. Predict how the response changes before running the model. A prediction that contradicts conservation of water, plausible timing or an independently measured constraint requires investigation even when the graph looks smooth.

For this particular case the dominant caution is that gauge undercatch and orographic gradients can bias even mathematically correct area weights. Assess which boundary condition is most likely to be violated. For an event analysis, compare the timing of inputs and outputs; for a seasonal balance, separate fluxes from storage; for statistical estimates, distinguish frequency assumptions from observed counts. Do not claim a coefficient calibrated in one environment is universal. State one missing measurement and the practical difficulty of obtaining it.

Mechanism checks and falsification

Counterfactual A — scale. Suppose the area or response time is changed while keeping some input depth or intensity fixed. Decide whether the output should scale linearly, sublinearly or nonlinearly, and explain which physical assumption controls that conclusion. For point and areal precipitation, the relevant limiting cases include a nearly dry condition, a very large forcing and a coefficient approaching the edge of its documented range. A model that produces negative water depth, impossible storage or negative coefficients without a clear warning is not fit for student use.

Counterfactual B — observation. Ask how a stage recorder, pluviometer, soil-moisture probe or properly maintained flow measurement could disprove the chosen interpretation. Compare two alternatives: an error in the input and an error in the process structure. Error in a timestamp may alter the inferred lag without changing storm totals; an unmodeled flow diversion may alter volume without a rainfall error. Use these fingerprints to choose the next data collection action.

Cross-topic connection. This lesson connects to storm temporal pattern and hyetographs because a model transfers its assumptions through the wider analysis. It also affects extreme rainfall, uncertainty and climate, which may rely on the same boundary records but at a different temporal or spatial resolution. A strong report shows these dependencies explicitly, identifies the quantities shared by two calculations and avoids treating correlated errors as independent measurements.

§2.3 Storm temporal pattern and hyetographs

Lesson 2.2 — Storm temporal pattern and hyetographs

Hydrological mechanism

Storm depth is the sum of time-interval intensities multiplied by interval duration. A design burst near the beginning can produce different runoff from the same total depth arriving after soils have wetted. Rainfall time series must specify aggregation conventions, the interval label and time zone. Converting daily rainfall into a uniform hourly series destroys the peak-timing information needed for short response basins.

§2.4 Intensity-duration-frequency relationships

Lesson 2.3 — Intensity-duration-frequency relationships

Hydrological mechanism

An IDF curve summarizes statistical precipitation magnitude as a function of duration and exceedance level. A stylized i=a/(t+b)^n is a convenient teaching response, not a replacement for locally issued frequency estimates. Return periods describe the reciprocal of an annual exceedance probability only within a stationary interpretation; they are not a countdown clock.

Teaching relation: i(t)=a/(t+b)^n; P=i t for consistent units. Every term must be traced to its physical definition before numerical evaluation. §2.5 Extreme rainfall, uncertainty and climate

Lesson 2.4 — Extreme rainfall, uncertainty and climate

Hydrological mechanism

A frequency estimate depends on data length, observation quality, distribution assumptions, trends and physical setting. Confidence intervals often widen in the far tail, where a convenient single return-period number can imply unsupported certainty. Nonstationary rainfall, changing land use and compound coastal or riverine extremes require scenario-based reasoning. Separate observed trends, hypothetical stress tests and adopted design policy rather than presenting one as the other.
