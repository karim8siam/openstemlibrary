Rainfall-Runoff Transformation and NRCS Curve Number

Comprehensive university textbook unit covering fundamental theory, mathematical derivations, computational hydraulics, and engineering design validation of Rainfall-Runoff Transformation and NRCS Curve Number. Developed for undergraduate civil engineering study.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Hydrology study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§4.1 Case study: Hill-foot settlement runoff response

Case study: Hill-foot settlement runoff response

A 7.5 km² catchment contains steep cultivated land, paved pathways and riparian vegetation. For a storm with 75 mm rainfall, an assumed CN of 78 gives S about 71.6 mm, Ia about 14.3 mm and event excess roughly 28.0 mm. This is a scenario, not a measured event. A short intense burst may concentrate runoff before all hillslopes become connected.

Investigation task. Calculate scenario runoff volume; compare a CN change to 85, then draw a triangular hydrograph that conserves the resulting water volume.

The unit as part of a watershed investigation

This unit asks an engineer to defend storm excess and travel time as a quantitative argument rather than a number inserted into a generic formula. The natural control volume is the selected hydrologic subsystem and its actual inflow and outflow boundaries. Write the unit of every parameter and specify the temporal support of the observations; an hourly intensity, a daily depth and an annual volume cannot be substituted for each other without integration. A concept sketch should show the site boundaries, measurement stations, surface and subsurface pathways, relevant human infrastructure and the time interval over which the process operates. Interpret the final result relative to the real decision at hill-foot settlement runoff response, not merely against an arbitrary calculator threshold.

Field evidence, scale and uncertainty

The most important practical complication in rainfall–runoff transformation is that model inputs are estimates of spatially and temporally varying states. Two instruments reporting the same variable may not have comparable averaging periods or calibration. The impact of an uncertain coefficient depends on the derivative of the output with respect to that coefficient and on where the operating point falls relative to a threshold. If a predicted response appears robust, deliberately test a neighboring process that the simple model omits: channel blockage, antecedent saturation, high tailwater, sensor drift, groundwater exchange or a different storm shape. Record failures of the model openly, rather than editing the inputs until a visually pleasing curve appears.

Professional inference standard

An educational simulation is useful when it generates a falsifiable prediction. Before using any of the four lessons to make a design claim, identify an independently measurable output, a second approximation with different assumptions and a failure consequence. For hill-foot settlement runoff response, a field campaign might require time-synchronized precipitation and flow observations, a georeferenced inventory of hydraulic structures, separate dry- and wet-weather site visits and a provenance ledger for every parameter. The exercises below are intentionally synthetic and no parameter set is asserted to describe a real Bangladesh, US or other watershed. Local codes and agency-approved hydrologic methods supersede these teaching screens.

§4.2 Hortonian and saturation-excess runoff

Lesson 4.1 — Hortonian and saturation-excess runoff

Hydrological mechanism

Infiltration-excess runoff occurs when rainfall supply exceeds the instantaneous surface infiltration ability. Saturation-excess runoff arises when soil storage becomes full and the contributing saturated area expands. The distinction matters for prediction and interventions: a permeable surface alone may not prevent rapid runoff in a saturated valley bottom. Event antecedent moisture and land cover interact nonlinearly.

Governing expression and dimensions

Teaching relation: Runoff depth=max(0,P-losses) only for an explicitly defined event balance. Every term must be traced to its physical definition before numerical evaluation. A hydrologic depth expressed in millimeters corresponds to one thousand cubic meters for every square kilometer of area. A discharge expressed in cubic meters per second becomes volume only after integrating over seconds. A rate reported over a nominal day or hour is not a spatial average by itself. State whether the equation is a conservation balance, idealization, calibrated empirical law or fitted statistical relation; that classification determines what evidence is needed before transfer to a new catchment.

Units and source audit. For hortonian and saturation-excess runoff, list the observed quantities, estimated coefficients and chosen reference conditions separately. Include the dates and methods used to obtain each. Calculate one reference state using all quantities in compatible units and round only after a dimensional check. Then reverse a sign convention or convert to a different set of units and verify that the physical meaning survives. A unit-consistent equation may still be wrong because its assumptions do not match the setting.

Applied decision: Explain two storms of equal depth with different runoff fractions

The central decision is not whether a formula can be evaluated; it is whether the value carries information sufficient for an engineering action. In this situation, first identify the outlet, observation period and decision variable. Establish a low, central and high forcing scenario using values that can be defended by measurements or explicitly labeled hypotheses. Predict how the response changes before running the model. A prediction that contradicts conservation of water, plausible timing or an independently measured constraint requires investigation even when the graph looks smooth.

For this particular case the dominant caution is that ignoring antecedent wetness produces inconsistent apparent runoff coefficients. Assess which boundary condition is most likely to be violated. For an event analysis, compare the timing of inputs and outputs; for a seasonal balance, separate fluxes from storage; for statistical estimates, distinguish frequency assumptions from observed counts. Do not claim a coefficient calibrated in one environment is universal. State one missing measurement and the practical difficulty of obtaining it.

Mechanism checks and falsification

Counterfactual A — scale. Suppose the area or response time is changed while keeping some input depth or intensity fixed. Decide whether the output should scale linearly, sublinearly or nonlinearly, and explain which physical assumption controls that conclusion. For hortonian and saturation-excess runoff, the relevant limiting cases include a nearly dry condition, a very large forcing and a coefficient approaching the edge of its documented range. A model that produces negative water depth, impossible storage or negative coefficients without a clear warning is not fit for student use.

Counterfactual B — observation. Ask how a stage recorder, pluviometer, soil-moisture probe or properly maintained flow measurement could disprove the chosen interpretation. Compare two alternatives: an error in the input and an error in the process structure. Error in a timestamp may alter the inferred lag without changing storm totals; an unmodeled flow diversion may alter volume without a rainfall error. Use these fingerprints to choose the next data collection action.

Cross-topic connection. This lesson connects to curve-number event abstraction because a model transfers its assumptions through the wider analysis. It also affects travel time, response and unit hydrographs, which may rely on the same boundary records but at a different temporal or spatial resolution. A strong report shows these dependencies explicitly, identifies the quantities shared by two calculations and avoids treating correlated errors as independent measurements.

§4.3 Curve-number event abstraction

Lesson 4.2 — Curve-number event abstraction

Hydrological mechanism

The NRCS curve-number method estimates event runoff using a potential-retention parameter inferred from land and soil characteristics. In the familiar formulation S=25400/CN-254 in millimeters, initial abstraction Ia=lambda S and Q=(P-Ia)^2/(P-Ia+S) for P>Ia. Lambda=0.2 is a classic convention; local practice and calibration may differ. It is a lumped empirical event model and does not resolve infiltration physics.

Governing expression and dimensions

Teaching relation: S=25400/CN-254; Q=(P-0.2S)^2/(P+0.8S) for P>0.2S. Every term must be traced to its physical definition before numerical evaluation. §4.4 Rational peak discharge method

Lesson 4.3 — Rational peak discharge method

Hydrological mechanism

The Rational Method estimates peak discharge with an empirical runoff coefficient, area and rainfall intensity chosen for a critical duration linked to time of concentration. With A in square kilometers and i in millimeters per hour, Q=0.278 C i A in cubic meters per second. The method estimates a peak, not a full hydrograph; it assumes catchment conditions consistent with the chosen coefficient and duration.

Governing expression and dimensions

§4.5 Travel time, response and unit hydrographs

Lesson 4.4 — Travel time, response and unit hydrographs

Hydrological mechanism

A unit hydrograph expresses basin direct-runoff response to a defined unit depth of excess rainfall over a stated duration. It requires linearity and time invariance over the modeled range; neither is exact during extreme floodplain activation. Hydrograph volume must equal effective-rainfall depth times basin area, and the peak depends on response timing. A triangular hydrograph makes that conservation principle visible even though real basin responses are rarely triangular.

Governing expression and dimensions

Teaching relation: V=A Pe; for triangular shape Q_peak=2V/T_base. Every term must be traced to its physical definition before numerical evaluation. A hydrologic depth expressed in millimeters corresponds to one thousand cubic meters for every square kilometer of area. A discharge expressed in cubic meters per second becomes volume only after integrating over seconds. A rate reported over a nominal day or hour is not a spatial average by itself. Solved Problem Example 4.1: Worked Problem 4.1: Energy and Mass Conservation Balance

A civil hydraulic/water resources system is analyzed under Rainfall-Runoff Transformation and NRCS Curve Number conditions. Evaluate head loss, pressure differentials, and flow discharge along the hydraulic path. Formulate the governing equations, carry out dimensional verification, and verify limiting behavior.

Step 1: Problem Geometry and Input Parameters Identify cross-sectional dimensions, fluid density $\rho$, gravity $g$, boundary elevations, and roughness coefficients for Rainfall-Runoff Transformation and NRCS Curve Number.

$$Q = \frac{1}{n} A R^{2/3} S^{1/2}, \quad h_f = f \frac{L}{D} \frac{v^2}{2g}$$
