§2.1 Theoretical Framework: Flexural Behavior and Beam Section Design

Learning purpose. Develop an explicit force, geometry, materials and evidence model before any final code compliance calculation. Numerical examples in this unit are educational and not an authorization to construct.

Guided investigation: section resistance as an equilibrium experiment

For a rectangular beam in positive bending, start by identifying the compression side. At low loading the tensile face may remain uncracked and the member acts approximately as a composite elastic section. Once cracks form, the tensile force transfers increasingly into the steel while concrete between cracks still influences stiffness through bond. At high flexural demand, the force resultant must balance: the concrete compression block and any compression steel must equilibrate tensile reinforcement. No formula can replace this basic free-body reasoning.

Set a section width, effective depth, tension steel area, concrete strength and steel yield stress. Solve the elementary compression-block depth a for an assumed yielded-steel model. If the resulting neutral-axis depth is implausibly large, the assumption that tension steel yields may fail. To investigate, draw a linear strain profile with maximum compressive concrete strain and determine steel strain at each reinforcing layer. Convert steel strain to stress through an elastic-plastic material relation. Solve for a neutral-axis position that restores axial force equilibrium. Repeat for several reinforcement ratios and examine the change in calculated moment and curvature.

A T beam requires another decision before the stress block is written. Is the upper flange truly continuous with the web, and does a code-compliant effective width exist? If a lies wholly inside the flange for positive bending, the rectangular analogy can be useful. If the compression zone extends below the flange, split the block into web and overhanging flange parts. At a negative-moment support region, the slab may be in tension and much of the broad flange compression assumption disappears. Distinguish bending direction on the member drawing before placing top or bottom bars.

Nominal resistance is not the entire design. A resistance reduction factor or partial material safety factors must be applied consistently with the applicable standard. The section may require checks for minimum or maximum reinforcement, minimum bar spacing, development length at critical sections, confinement, bar cutoffs and any seismic plastic-hinge detailing. These details determine whether the calculated force resultants can physically develop. The lowest-cost reinforcement arrangement may also be impractical if the joint is too crowded to cast properly.

In an engineering notebook, report a response table for a series of As values. Include a, c, tensile steel strain, nominal moment, and a field stating whether the originally assumed steel-yield state is self-consistent. Compare the result to the graphical flexure lab. Avoid the temptation to report only the largest nominal number. A robust trial must also control deformation, cracking and constructability. End by sketching a fully dimensioned cross-section and the development path for tension steel into the support.

§2.2 Strain compatibility and equilibrium

Lesson 2.1: Strain compatibility and equilibrium

Physical meaning and governing ideas

A plane-section strain diagram provides the bridge between material laws and internal force resultants. At a strain-compatible ultimate state the tension-steel strain depends on the neutral-axis location; equilibrium requires the steel tensile force to balance concrete compression and, where present, compression steel. Changing reinforcement ratio can increase nominal resistance while reducing rotation capacity.

Analysis and design reasoning

Draw three separate diagrams before evaluating equations: cross-section geometry, linear strain distribution and equivalent internal resultants. Use a realistic concrete stress distribution or a code-compatible equivalent block appropriate to the selected material strength. Revisit assumed steel yielding after solving equilibrium; heavy reinforcement may produce compression-governed behavior that a simplified yielded-steel formula cannot predict.

Worked numerical or geometric illustration

For b=300 mm, d=500 mm, As=1600 mm², fy=420 MPa and f′c=30 MPa, force equilibrium with an illustrative 0.85f′c rectangular block gives a=Asfy/(0.85f′cb)=87.84 mm. Nominal moment Mn=Asfy(d−a/2)=306.46 kN·m. This excludes reduction factors and a final strain-region determination.

Interpretation and failure checks

An increase in As improves nominal moment at first but not indefinitely. The compression zone deepens, steel strain reduces, and requirements for ductility can control.

§2.3 Tension-controlled behavior and detailing

Lesson 2.2: Tension-controlled behavior and detailing

Physical meaning and governing ideas

A tension-controlled flexural response provides warning through steel yielding and plastic rotations, but this desirable behavior is not automatic. Neutral-axis depth, high-strength reinforcement, slab flange action and compression reinforcement all influence the extreme steel strain. Member capacity without reliable development of steel bars is not available in practice.

Analysis and design reasoning

Use section strain compatibility to evaluate whether tension steel yields and whether a target strain region is achieved under the governing code. Inspect anchorage near supports, bar cutoff zones, side-face reinforcement where required, and continuity over interior supports. For doubly reinforced or flanged members, revise the force model rather than forcing a rectangular singly reinforced shortcut.

Worked numerical or geometric illustration

In the preceding b=300 mm example, taking beta1=0.85 solely to illustrate the concept gives c=a/0.85≈103.34 mm. With an assumed ultimate concrete compression strain 0.003, the bottom steel strain is 0.003(500−103.34)/103.34≈0.0115, comfortably above a typical steel-yield strain 420/200000=0.0021. Confirm the exact strain criteria in the governing edition.

Interpretation and failure checks

Do not equate ductility with excess strength. A highly over-reinforced section can have large nominal force capacity but poor warning and redistribution.

§2.4 Doubly reinforced sections and moment redistribution

Lesson 2.3: Doubly reinforced sections and moment redistribution

Physical meaning and governing ideas

Compression steel can increase flexural resistance when geometric depth is constrained and can affect long-term deflection and deformation capacity. Its force depends on the compatible strain at its location, so using fy for compression steel without checking the strain is not generally correct. Moment redistribution in continuous members requires adequate rotation capacity and suitable detailing.

Analysis and design reasoning

Resolve tensile steel force, equivalent concrete compression and compression steel force about a consistent reference axis. Account for any concrete replaced by compression steel when doing a high-precision model. Locate top reinforcement correctly where hogging moments develop and recognize that tension and compression faces swap with moment sign.

Worked numerical or geometric illustration

In a 300 mm wide beam with d=520 mm, d′=55 mm, As=2300 mm² and As′=650 mm², determine c by solving 0.85f′c b(beta1 c)+As′fs′(c)=Asfy, where fs′ is bounded by ±fy from strain compatibility. Substituting an arbitrary c without checking force balance leads to a fictitious nominal moment.

Interpretation and failure checks

Run the doubly reinforced simulation and explain why two seemingly similar beams have different compression-steel effectiveness if d′ changes.

§2.5 T beams, flanged sections and moment sign

Lesson 2.4: T beams, flanged sections and moment sign

Physical meaning and governing ideas

A slab monolithic with a supporting beam can form a compression flange for positive bending. The flange width available for design is not necessarily the entire tributary slab width; effective-flange restrictions depend on span, geometry and code. Under negative bending over an interior support, the slab flange may be on the tensile side, so the compression block belongs largely in the web.

Analysis and design reasoning

First determine whether the compression zone lies inside the flange thickness. If it does, a rectangular compression block with the appropriate effective flange width may represent positive-moment strength. If the block extends below the flange, split concrete compression into flange and web contributions. The reinforcement and dimensions must be shown on a section with correct moment sign.

Worked numerical or geometric illustration

Suppose a flange width is provisionally 1000 mm, thickness 100 mm, f′c=30 MPa, As=2000 mm² and fy=420 MPa. A first trial gives a=Asfy/(0.85f′c bf)=32.94 mm, less than the 100 mm flange thickness. The compression zone therefore remains within the flange under that illustrative block model, subject to verifying actual effective width.

Interpretation and failure checks

Never use the positive-moment flange width to calculate a negative-moment support section unless compression is actually in that flange.

§2.7 Knowledge check with explanatory answers

Knowledge check with explanatory answers

Question 2.1

Why does increasing steel area not guarantee improved flexural ductility?

Discussion answer. Additional tension steel deepens the concrete compression zone needed for equilibrium. As the neutral axis moves toward the tension steel, its strain at the same extreme compression strain decreases. Beyond an appropriate range, the section may lose the desired yielding-before-crushing behavior. Both nominal moment and compatible tensile strain need evaluation, and code limits may restrict reinforcement quantities and detailing.

Question 2.2

When does a T beam behave approximately as a rectangular flange section?

Discussion answer. Under the assumed positive-moment compression direction, the equivalent concrete compression block must fit fully inside the effective slab flange. Its calculated depth should not exceed the flange thickness. The effective flange width itself must be established from geometry and applicable rules; it cannot be equated to the full tributary floor width without justification. For negative bending the compression side may be in the web.

Question 2.3

Why does reducing effective depth influence the same beam even if steel quantity remains unchanged?

Discussion answer. The internal lever arm between tensile and compressive resultants is reduced. Approximate nominal moment Asfy(d−a/2) therefore decreases for unchanged force and block depth. Strain compatibility also shifts because the tensile layer is closer to the neutral axis. Practical causes include moving bars to a second layer, larger stirrups or unexpected cover. Recalculate design and serviceability rather than reusing nominal depth.

Question 2.4

Why is compression reinforcement stress not always equal to fy?

Discussion answer. Compression reinforcement stress depends on local compatible strain. A bar at d′ from the compression face may remain below yield if the neutral axis is near it or the available curvature is limited. Calculate strain from the assumed section profile, use an appropriate constitutive relation and bound stresses at yield. Declaring all compression bars yielded can overstate capacity and misplace the neutral axis.

Question 2.5

What drawing detail can invalidate a correct numerical moment calculation?

Discussion answer. Tension reinforcement that stops before it can develop required force near the support can fail by anchorage or splitting despite adequate steel area at the analytical section. Other examples include a bar arrangement that cannot physically fit, insufficient concrete cover, wrong bar layer, misplaced top steel over a continuous support or missing confinement. Section resistance is useful only when the physical force path exists.

§2.8 Unit project and experiment

Unit project and experiment

Compare two beam sections constrained to a 300×550 mm gross rectangle: a singly reinforced section and a doubly reinforced section. Choose concrete strength, steel strength, nominal cover and bar diameters. For each layout draw the concrete compression block and strain distribution for a positive bending case, solve force equilibrium, calculate nominal moment and state the strain assumption. Check geometric fit with the reinforcement-cage lab. Discuss the cost of steel quantity, congestion and long-term serviceability. Test a higher-grade steel option and explain why greater fy does not automatically improve ductility or bond. End with a transparent checklist of design-code checks still missing from the simplified calculations.

Deliverable: A transparent engineering memo with assumptions, diagrams, units, hand-checks, source references and a list of issues that need a governing-code and professional review.

Knowledge check with explanatory answers

Question 2.1

Why does increasing steel area not guarantee improved flexural ductility?

Discussion answer. Additional tension steel deepens the concrete compression zone needed for equilibrium. As the neutral axis moves toward the tension steel, its strain at the same extreme compression strain decreases. Beyond an appropriate range, the section may lose the desired yielding-before-crushing behavior. Both nominal moment and compatible tensile strain need evaluation, and code limits may restrict reinforcement quantities and detailing.

Question 2.2

When does a T beam behave approximately as a rectangular flange section?

Discussion answer. Under the assumed positive-moment compression direction, the equivalent concrete compression block must fit fully inside the effective slab flange. Its calculated depth should not exceed the flange thickness. The effective flange width itself must be established from geometry and applicable rules; it cannot be equated to the full tributary floor width without justification. For negative bending the compression side may be in the web.

Question 2.3

Why does reducing effective depth influence the same beam even if steel quantity remains unchanged?

Discussion answer. The internal lever arm between tensile and compressive resultants is reduced. Approximate nominal moment Asfy(d−a/2) therefore decreases for unchanged force and block depth. Strain compatibility also shifts because the tensile layer is closer to the neutral axis. Practical causes include moving bars to a second layer, larger stirrups or unexpected cover. Recalculate design and serviceability rather than reusing nominal depth.

Question 2.4

Why is compression reinforcement stress not always equal to fy?

Discussion answer. Compression reinforcement stress depends on local compatible strain. A bar at d′ from the compression face may remain below yield if the neutral axis is near it or the available curvature is limited. Calculate strain from the assumed section profile, use an appropriate constitutive relation and bound stresses at yield. Declaring all compression bars yielded can overstate capacity and misplace the neutral axis.

Question 2.5

What drawing detail can invalidate a correct numerical moment calculation?

Discussion answer. Tension reinforcement that stops before it can develop required force near the support can fail by anchorage or splitting despite adequate steel area at the analytical section. Other examples include a bar arrangement that cannot physically fit, insufficient concrete cover, wrong bar layer, misplaced top steel over a continuous support or missing confinement. Section resistance is useful only when the physical force path exists.
