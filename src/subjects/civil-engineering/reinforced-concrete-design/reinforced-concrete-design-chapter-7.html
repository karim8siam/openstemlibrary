§7.1 Theoretical Framework: Serviceability, Crack Control, Bond and Detailing

Learning purpose. Develop an explicit force, geometry, materials and evidence model before any final code compliance calculation. Numerical examples in this unit are educational and not an authorization to construct.

Guided investigation: designing for use rather than only collapse prevention

A member may have adequate ultimate moment resistance and still produce unacceptable cracking, vibration or sag. Serviceability relates to how a building performs during ordinary occupation, with both mechanical and human consequences. Cracks near wet areas or in exposed external members may influence durability; floor deflection can damage partitions or cause ponding; large rotations at slab edges can compromise façade joints. Strength and serviceability use different action combinations and models, so they need separate records.

Start with a simple reinforced beam and compare uncracked gross inertia with a cracked transformed-section inertia. At cracking, concrete in tension no longer carries the full tensile stress assumed in a gross elastic analysis. The tensile steel becomes comparatively more important and the neutral axis changes position. An effective short-term stiffness model attempts to bridge the cracked and uncracked ranges, but no single elastic EI captures all load histories. Creep adds deformation under sustained load; shrinkage can change curvature even without an increase in imposed external action.

For crack control, compare two reinforcement layouts with approximately equal steel area: fewer large bars and more smaller bars. Their nominal flexural resistance may be similar while steel stress distribution, bond transfer and crack spacing differ. An idealized crack opening w≈sΔε is a useful dimensional model because it connects a length to a strain, but it does not supply the mean crack spacing or strain-transfer difference. Those must come from an appropriate detailed method and relevant conditions of restraint and cover.

Now examine anchorage. At a support, a bar can carry a large tensile force only if sufficient embedded length or reliable anchor hardware transfers that force to concrete. A constant bond-stress equation is a classroom balance of surface shear and bar axial force; it omits splitting, restraint, bar position, end conditions, hooked or headed anchorage and splice group effects. A lap region must also be detailed so that many bars are not all discontinued in a congested, high-stress location without permitted provisions.

Combine numerical checks with an actual cage drawing. Show clear cover to the outermost steel, dimensions to bar centroids, stirrup hooks, layer separation and development beyond critical sections. Trace each designed force to a piece of physical reinforcement. Have a colleague review the drawing without access to the design calculations and ask whether the load path remains understandable. Good serviceability practice combines predictive models, placement quality and inspection.

§7.2 Cracking under service loads

Lesson 7.1: Cracking under service loads

Physical meaning and governing ideas

Reinforcement controls crack widths by distributing tensile force across many cracks but does not usually keep a flexural member entirely uncracked. Surface crack openings depend on steel strain, bond-slip, cover, bar spacing, tension stiffening and load duration. Appearance, water-tightness, durability and occupant experience can set different serviceability criteria.

Analysis and design reasoning

Use the selected code’s serviceability method and realistic service load combinations. Explain a crack result as an estimate with uncertainty, not a precise prediction; shrinkage and temperature cracks may not follow a flexural bending model. Distribution of steel may matter more to crack control than a single large bar with identical area.

Worked numerical or geometric illustration

In a deliberately simplified strain-transfer model, mean crack spacing s=150 mm and mean strain difference 650 microstrain produce w≈150×650×10⁻⁶=0.0975 mm. Actual design width could differ significantly because the assumed spacing and strain transfer are uncalibrated.

Interpretation and failure checks

A wide crack may identify unexpected restraint or bond deterioration. Diagnosing the load and restraint source is more useful than merely covering the crack.

§7.3 Deflection, creep and shrinkage

Lesson 7.2: Deflection, creep and shrinkage

Physical meaning and governing ideas

Immediate member deformation depends on cracking, reinforcement, span and restraint. Long-term deflection adds creep under sustained load and shrinkage-induced curvature. A gross-section elastic prediction can underestimate sagging when cracking reduces stiffness, while a fully cracked model may be inappropriate at low moment.

Analysis and design reasoning

Compute at least a gross stiffness bound and a cracked stiffness estimate when learning section mechanics. Use code-compatible effective stiffness or time-dependent methods for design. Compare the total deformation to the relevant finishes, partitions and functional limits, including differential deflection after construction of brittle components.

Worked numerical or geometric illustration

For a singly reinforced 300 mm wide beam with d=500 mm, As=1600 mm² and f′c=30 MPa, an approximate concrete elastic modulus is 4700√30≈25743 MPa. The elastic modular ratio Es/Ec≈7.77 enters the cracked transformed-section neutral-axis equation b x²/2=nAs(d−x).

Interpretation and failure checks

A beam that meets immediate deflection limits may still experience unacceptable long-term sag if sustained load fraction and creep are ignored.

§7.4 Development, laps, hooks and anchorage

Lesson 7.3: Development, laps, hooks and anchorage

Physical meaning and governing ideas

Rebar force must be transmitted into surrounding concrete over finite length. Bond is not a universal constant: confining reinforcement, surface characteristics, bar location, concrete strength, cover and transverse pressure affect splitting and pullout. Hooked bars, headed bars, mechanical couplers and lap splices have different force-transfer paths.

Analysis and design reasoning

Start from the bar force that must be developed at each location. Identify adequate available length beyond a critical section, and detail continuation or mechanical anchorage when the member is too short. Concentrating many laps in one high-stress region can create congestion and weaken local confinement. Inspection must verify where reinforcement actually ends.

Worked numerical or geometric illustration

A simple average bond equilibrium T=πdb ld τ and steel yield force T=πdb² fy/4 gives ld=db fy/(4τ). A 20 mm bar at 420 MPa with an assumed average bond stress τ=2.5 MPa yields ld=840 mm. This is strictly an illustrative force-transfer length, not a code development length.

Interpretation and failure checks

Failure at a cut-off bar can occur even though a distant cross-section has abundant calculated flexural resistance.

§7.5 Cage fit, constructability and drawings

Lesson 7.4: Cage fit, constructability and drawings

Physical meaning and governing ideas

Reinforcement detailing is an engineering model of force continuity constrained by physical space. Clear spacing, hooks, bend radii, stirrup closure, cover and congestion affect strength through bond and concrete placement. A reinforcement schedule must communicate dimensions, bar marks, placement tolerance and splice instructions unambiguously.

Analysis and design reasoning

Prepare a full-size section near critical locations, not merely a neat single-line beam symbol. Verify that the designed bar count fits with cover and stirrups, concrete aggregate can pass, and vibrating equipment can consolidate the material. Use an as-built check where deviations may invalidate nominal d or anchorage.

Worked numerical or geometric illustration

For a 300 mm wide beam with 40 mm cover, 10 mm stirrup, four 20 mm bars in one row, clear spacing is [300−2(40+10)−4×20]/3=40 mm. Five 20 mm bars would leave [300−100−100]/4=25 mm, which changes the construction decision.

Interpretation and failure checks

A digital model is not buildable simply because the bars appear non-overlapping at screen resolution. Examine tolerances, layered arrangements and joint congestion.

§7.7 Knowledge check with explanatory answers

Knowledge check with explanatory answers

Question 7.1

Why can a beam be stronger in bending but worse in crack control?

Discussion answer. A layout with large bars may provide ample area for nominal bending strength yet develop wider crack spacing and less favorable surface crack distribution. Steel stress, cover, spacing, bond and restraint are relevant. Crack control should use code-specific service-load criteria; moment strength alone does not determine crack width.

Question 7.2

What is the role of transformed-section analysis in deflection?

Discussion answer. It allows a cracked concrete section with steel reinforcement to be represented by an equivalent stiffness under elastic assumptions. Concrete tension is neglected; steel area is scaled by the modular ratio. The resulting cracked inertia offers insight into stiffness sensitivity but is not necessarily the effective inertia at every load level or time.

Question 7.3

Why is a uniform-bond development-length calculation incomplete?

Discussion answer. The equation assumes average τ remains constant along a bar, neglecting splitting, cover, confinement, placement and surface effects. Real strain and bond distributions are nonuniform. Development and splice provisions account for influences not present in the simple circumference-force balance. The illustrative formula can teach equilibrium but cannot specify a construction lap.

Question 7.4

How does a reinforcement fit calculation support structural performance?

Discussion answer. If intended bars cannot fit within cover and stirrup boundaries, the installed arrangement will differ from the design model or concrete consolidation will be compromised. Actual effective depth, bond, durability and confinement may change. Cage fit must be checked with realistic bar diameters, clear spacing, hooks, aggregate passage and tolerances.

Question 7.5

Why can long-term deflection differ from a short-term estimate?

Discussion answer. Creep under sustained stress, shrinkage curvature and evolving cracking change the stiffness and deformation over time. Temporary formwork and the age at loading influence the history. A short-term gross-section deflection cannot capture partition damage or façade movement that develops after occupancy.

§7.8 Unit project and experiment

Unit project and experiment

A reinforced concrete floor shows visible cracks and increasing sag after occupancy. Build competing hypotheses: under-reinforcement, excessive construction loading, shrinkage restraint, poor curing, creep, inadequate anchorage, or differential support movement. For each hypothesis identify one observation or test that would increase or decrease its plausibility. Reconstruct the as-built reinforcement depth and cover from reliable evidence rather than assuming drawings are exact. Compute simple uncracked and cracked stiffness estimates and document their limitations. Use the cracking and deflection labs to explore sensitivity. Present an investigation plan distinguishing non-destructive screening, intrusive inspection and an engineer-supervised structural assessment; do not declare the floor safe from simulation results.

Deliverable: A transparent engineering memo with assumptions, diagrams, units, hand-checks, source references and a list of issues that need a governing-code and professional review.

Knowledge check with explanatory answers

Question 7.1

Why can a beam be stronger in bending but worse in crack control?

Discussion answer. A layout with large bars may provide ample area for nominal bending strength yet develop wider crack spacing and less favorable surface crack distribution. Steel stress, cover, spacing, bond and restraint are relevant. Crack control should use code-specific service-load criteria; moment strength alone does not determine crack width.

Question 7.2

What is the role of transformed-section analysis in deflection?

Discussion answer. It allows a cracked concrete section with steel reinforcement to be represented by an equivalent stiffness under elastic assumptions. Concrete tension is neglected; steel area is scaled by the modular ratio. The resulting cracked inertia offers insight into stiffness sensitivity but is not necessarily the effective inertia at every load level or time.

Question 7.3

Why is a uniform-bond development-length calculation incomplete?

Discussion answer. The equation assumes average τ remains constant along a bar, neglecting splitting, cover, confinement, placement and surface effects. Real strain and bond distributions are nonuniform. Development and splice provisions account for influences not present in the simple circumference-force balance. The illustrative formula can teach equilibrium but cannot specify a construction lap.

Question 7.4

How does a reinforcement fit calculation support structural performance?

Discussion answer. If intended bars cannot fit within cover and stirrup boundaries, the installed arrangement will differ from the design model or concrete consolidation will be compromised. Actual effective depth, bond, durability and confinement may change. Cage fit must be checked with realistic bar diameters, clear spacing, hooks, aggregate passage and tolerances.

Question 7.5

Why can long-term deflection differ from a short-term estimate?

Discussion answer. Creep under sustained stress, shrinkage curvature and evolving cracking change the stiffness and deformation over time. Temporary formwork and the age at loading influence the history. A short-term gross-section deflection cannot capture partition damage or façade movement that develops after occupancy.

A reinforced concrete floor shows visible cracks and increasing sag after occupancy.
