§4.1 Theoretical Framework: Mixture Proportioning and Trial Batches

Core investigation: Three alternative mixture designs.

Unit framework. Mixture quantities have mass, moisture and volume bases. A trial sheet that silently switches between these bases can be numerically neat yet physically wrong.

Capstone evidence task. Audit a proposed mixture for a 1 m³ target using three independent methods: total volume, effective water and batch ticket reconciliation.

§4.2 Learning outcomes

Learning outcomes

At the end of this unit, explain each of its four topics without substituting a numerical model for measured concrete behavior; solve the displayed material balance or test interpretation with consistent units; choose a verification measurement; and document why a field conclusion may differ from the classroom scenario.

§4.3 Laboratory and field interpretation

Laboratory and field interpretation

The experiments in this unit deliberately distinguish physical observation from a mathematical surrogate. Before using a laboratory to defend a structural or construction decision, document the material source, temperatures, specimen dimensions, test method, reference basis and limitations of calibration. Record negative results and unexpected trends: such outcomes are often more informative than a tidy curve that happens to fit the assumed model.

§4.4 Absolute-volume balance

4.1 Absolute-volume balance

Physical explanation

The absolute-volume method checks whether constituent volumes, including air, sum to the desired concrete yield. Mass is converted to volume using an appropriate density or specific gravity; aggregate values require care because bulk and apparent specific gravities are not interchangeable. The initial quantities are starting estimates subject to testing and correction, not a substitute for trial batching.

Worked engineering interpretation

A preliminary mix per cubic metre contains water 170 kg, binder 360 kg with density 3100 kg/m3, sand 800 kg at SSD particle density 2650 kg/m3, coarse aggregate 1020 kg at 2700 kg/m3, and air 2%. The volume sum is 0.170+0.116+0.302+0.378+0.020=0.986 m3, leaving about 0.014 m3 to adjust before claiming one cubic metre yield.

Application A: precast yard

Consider precast yard, where the central practical constraints include demoulding schedule, moisture retention, batch consistency and accelerated curing. The lesson on absolute-volume balance becomes a decision only after a responsible engineer states the exposure, intended function, material evidence and tolerable uncertainty. For this case start by identifying which quantities come from a mixture record and which depend on field construction. Make a table with the current state, intended change and predicted consequence. Do not equate a clean theoretical trend with successful placement: operation-specific limits can reverse the preferred option.

A useful thought experiment is to compare an apparently favorable laboratory value against an observed field defect. If the laboratory result supports the material but the member shows insufficient consolidation or cracking, the next investigation is not to discard the observation. Look for restrained slab formwork, finishing timing, transport history and the position of reinforcement; these can make the same nominal composition behave differently. State a falsifiable prediction for each alternative. For example, if the issue arises from a changed moisture condition, repeated batch-level corrections should reduce the discrepancy while other variables remain controlled.

Application B: mid-rise residential slab

Now transfer the principle to mid-rise residential slab. The governing constraint changes to restrained slab formwork, finishing timing, transport history and the position of reinforcement. A robust interpretation should revisit the boundary condition, not simply reuse a recommendation developed for the previous setting. Define the two most consequential failure modes and show what evidence would detect them early. The environmental and measurement variables must be recorded before ranking alternatives, particularly when transport, curing or local geometry can differ across a single structure.

For the measurement lens: Choose at least two measurements that respond to different mechanisms. State exactly what each test measures, in which units, and whether the test observes a specimen, the full member, or only a local surface. A simple correlation does not establish causation. Report sampling variation separately from variation in the real production process.

§4.5 Water–binder ratio and performance

4.2 Water–binder ratio and performance

Physical explanation

Water-to-binder ratio is an influential mix-design control variable, but a universal linear strength equation is not justified. Strength depends on materials, air, curing, age and the degree of reaction. Any strength-versus-w/cm graph used in class must be identified as a calibrated demonstration curve or fitted to measured trial-batch data.

Worked engineering interpretation

A teaching power-law fit is f=30×(0.50/wcm)^1.6 MPa at a specified age, with all other conditions fixed. At w/cm 0.45 it predicts about 35.5 MPa. This is a local mathematical illustration, not a design value or evidence that an actual project mix will reach that strength.

§4.6 Aggregate moisture and field corrections

4.3 Aggregate moisture and field corrections

Physical explanation

Batching must distinguish oven-dry, saturated-surface-dry and actual wet aggregate conditions. Surface water contributes to mixing water, whereas absorption capacity can remove water from the fresh paste if aggregate begins below SSD. A usable adjustment combines measured total moisture, absorption and target SSD aggregate mass. Stockpile moisture can change markedly after rain or across different levels.

Worked engineering interpretation

For 800 kg SSD sand, absorption 1.0% and measured total moisture 4.0% on oven-dry basis, dry mass is 800/1.01=792.08 kg. As-batched wet mass is 792.08×1.04=823.76 kg. Surface water is (0.04-0.01)×792.08=23.76 kg, so reduce measured mixing-water addition by approximately 23.8 kg.

§4.7 Trial batching and optimization

4.4 Trial batching and optimization

Physical explanation

A good mixture must satisfy multiple objectives simultaneously: fresh handling, hardened properties, durability, cost and embodied impact. Trial batches should hold some variables fixed so causes can be inferred; uncontrolled one-at-a-time site adjustments obscure relationships. Acceptance is based on relevant test evidence and the contract, not on a single simulated prediction.

Worked engineering interpretation

Three trial mixtures reduce binder content while keeping target w/cm unchanged through lower water demand. A useful comparison records aggregate grading, admixture dose, air content, placing behavior, strength development and durability proxy. A low-cement option should be rejected if it cannot reliably fill the reinforcement.

§4.9 Extended reading: mixture design as constrained optimization

Extended reading: mixture design as constrained optimization

Understand the basis of every quantity

Concrete proportions are simultaneously expressed as masses, volumes, and ratios. Confusion arises when the mass per unit batch is mistaken for mass per cubic metre or when oven-dry aggregate data are combined with SSD densities. An absolute-volume balance uses particle densities to estimate how the ingredients occupy the target volume. A practical mixture worksheet must also account for intentional or measured air. The calculated yield is a check, not proof of actual yield, because real aggregate bulk conditions, entrained voids and batching tolerances need to be established.

Begin with a trial mixture and write water, cement, SCMs, fine aggregate, coarse aggregate, admixture and air on separate lines. Where possible, include the material density source. Convert each mass to an absolute volume using consistent units. Sum the volumes and compare with one cubic metre; solve for a constituent only if it is reasonable to hold the others fixed. The most dangerous error is to make an arbitrary change purely to force the sum to one without checking whether the corrected mixture can still be placed. An elegant balance sheet cannot guarantee adequate aggregate separation, paste film thickness or pump flow.

SSD corrections need correct algebra

Aggregate moisture is often measured relative to oven-dry mass. If an SSD target mass is known and absorption A is expressed as a fraction of oven-dry mass, the oven-dry equivalent is MSSD/(1+A). If actual total moisture T is measured on the same basis, as-batched mass is Mdry(1+T). The excess above SSD contributes Mdry(T−A) of free surface water. This can be negative: aggregate below SSD may remove water from the paste. The sign has a physical meaning and should never be forced positive by a spreadsheet formatting rule.

A robust production sheet checks the algebra under limiting values. At T=A the surface-water correction should be zero. At T>A the wet mass should be greater than SSD mass and additional batch water should decrease. At T

Strength is not the only mixture target

A lower water-to-binder ratio often supports a denser hardened paste, but no universal empirical formula predicts a particular concrete's strength from w/cm alone. Aggregate composition, air, cement and SCM chemistry, curing temperature and age matter. A fitted strength sensitivity law is useful when a laboratory has enough measured trials to estimate parameters; its extrapolation beyond the tested material range should be rejected unless new evidence supports it. The educational strength model in the lab is deliberately labeled hypothetical so students can explore nonlinearity without mistaking the curve for a code requirement.

The real design problem is constrained. The mixture must meet minimum handling quality, hardened performance, relevant exposure requirements and the project schedule. It may also need lower embodied emissions or cost. These objectives can conflict. A blend with less cement may require a more effective admixture system and careful early curing. A very dense paste can make autogenous deformation and early restraint more significant. A high binder mix may be easy to finish but cause elevated heat and shrinkage. It is not appropriate to choose the lowest calculated carbon value while ignoring the operational risk of nonconforming placement.

Design the trials to reveal cause

A useful trial program changes one or two physical design variables across controlled mixtures. For example, vary w/cm while keeping paste volume approximately stable, then vary paste volume while trying to keep w/cm stable. This separation helps reveal whether improved workability arises from additional water or additional lubrication volume. Use replicates where test variation is significant and schedule measurements at defined ages. Examine both early and later strength, fresh stability, air, surface transport and any project-specific durability exposure.

The completed trial-batch report should include all mass and volume calculations, effective mixing-water ledger, admixture identity and dose, observed slump or spread, density, temperatures, test specimens and uncertainty. Record any unplanned change instead of hiding it in a final corrected table. If the selected alternative is not the mathematically cheapest mixture, explain the acceptance constraints that make it the practical choice. This is how mixture proportioning becomes an engineering decision rather than a spreadsheet exercise.

§4.10 Integrated case 4 — A one-cubic-metre mixture does not yield a cubic metre

Integrated case 4 — A one-cubic-metre mixture does not yield a cubic metre

Project setting. A trial mixture uses 170 kg water, 360 kg binder, 800 kg SSD fine aggregate, 1020 kg SSD coarse aggregate and an estimated 2% air. The mix designer uses nominal particle densities of 3100, 2650 and 2700 kg/m³ for binder, fine aggregate and coarse aggregate respectively. The plant reports an apparent batch yield lower than intended.

Arithmetic and interpretation. Water volume is 0.170 m³. Binder solid volume is 360/3100 ≈ 0.1161 m³. Fine aggregate contributes approximately 800/2650 ≈ 0.3019 m³, and coarse aggregate 1020/2700 ≈ 0.3778 m³. Air contributes 0.0200 m³. The sum is approximately 0.9858 m³. This already demonstrates a predicted shortfall of about 1.4% even before considering actual density and air measurements. Increasing any constituent solely to make the arithmetic total exactly one would ignore workability, cost and other performance constraints.

Data-quality problem. Suppose one aggregate density was taken from a manufacturer brochure, while the rest were measured on the actual stockpiles. A wrong density can create a systematic error that appears consistent across every batch. Suppose further that the delivered sand is wetter than SSD, but wet aggregate mass was substituted directly for SSD mass without adjusting water. The calculated volume balance might appear improved while the actual fresh mixture develops excess effective water. These errors have different physical consequences and cannot be merged into one “batch factor.”

Investigation plan. Verify particle densities and relevant moisture bases from representative measurements, confirm the air-content measurement, and calculate the absolute-volume ledger again. Produce a trial batch with recorded ingredient masses and determine actual yield using an appropriate field density or gravimetric process. If measured and predicted yield disagree, inspect entrained air, segregation, batching error and assumed material properties before making a new mixture. Repeat at enough batches to judge whether the error is systematic or intermittent.

Optimization decision. A change that improves calculated yield may also alter paste content and pumpability. The mix designer should document both volumetric balance and performance observations. If the final proposal changes binder or water substantially, repeat the hardened and fresh tests necessary for its use rather than carrying over results from an earlier composition. The laboratory's absolute-volume plot is an arithmetic teaching aid. It cannot certify that a specific truck load meets a contract because it uses fixed illustrative densities and does not physically weigh or measure that load.
