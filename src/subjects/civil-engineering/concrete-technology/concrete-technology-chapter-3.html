§3.1 Theoretical Framework: Fresh Concrete and Rheology

Core investigation: Workability dispute on a congested element.

Unit framework. A fresh mixture responds to prior shear, time and temperature, so nominal workability can change between sampling points. Slump and spread are operations-specific tests; neither measures every rheological property.

Capstone evidence task. Develop a timed testing sequence that avoids attributing a delayed flow loss to an unrecorded additional dosage.

§3.2 Learning outcomes

Learning outcomes

At the end of this unit, explain each of its four topics without substituting a numerical model for measured concrete behavior; solve the displayed material balance or test interpretation with consistent units; choose a verification measurement; and document why a field conclusion may differ from the classroom scenario.

§3.3 Laboratory and field interpretation

Laboratory and field interpretation

Two mix designs pass the target slump but one produces voids in a heavily reinforced mockup. Plan a repeatable placement test, including mixing time, discharge sequence, vibration regime, air and temperature records. Form hypotheses for blockages, bleeding and segregation separately. Recommend evidence required before either mixture can be accepted for the actual element. Include documentation of worker safety and the distinction between changing admixture dose and adding water.

The experiments in this unit deliberately distinguish physical observation from a mathematical surrogate. Before using a laboratory to defend a structural or construction decision, document the material source, temperatures, specimen dimensions, test method, reference basis and limitations of calibration. Record negative results and unexpected trends: such outcomes are often more informative than a tidy curve that happens to fit the assumed model.

§3.4 Workability as a system property

3.1 Workability as a system property

Physical explanation

Workability includes the ability to mix, transport, place, consolidate and finish without objectionable segregation. Slump is only one operational indicator and does not uniquely identify viscosity or segregation resistance. A high-range water reducer can increase slump without an equivalent increase in free water, which is why slump alone does not reveal w/cm.

Worked engineering interpretation

Two trial batches each give 170 mm slump. The first has a moderate paste volume and admixture; the second uses additional water and little admixture. Their strength and durability expectations differ even though one observed number matches. The engineer must compare mixture records and appropriate fresh tests.

Application A: mid-rise residential slab

Consider mid-rise residential slab, where the central practical constraints include restrained slab formwork, finishing timing, transport history and the position of reinforcement. The lesson on workability as a system property becomes a decision only after a responsible engineer states the exposure, intended function, material evidence and tolerable uncertainty. For this case start by identifying which quantities come from a mixture record and which depend on field construction. Make a table with the current state, intended change and predicted consequence. Do not equate a clean theoretical trend with successful placement: operation-specific limits can reverse the preferred option.

A useful thought experiment is to compare an apparently favorable laboratory value against an observed field defect. If the laboratory result supports the material but the member shows insufficient consolidation or cracking, the next investigation is not to discard the observation. Look for demoulding schedule, moisture retention, batch consistency and accelerated curing; these can make the same nominal composition behave differently. State a falsifiable prediction for each alternative. For example, if the issue arises from a changed moisture condition, repeated batch-level corrections should reduce the discrepancy while other variables remain controlled.

Application B: precast yard

State exactly what each test measures, in which units, and whether the test observes a specimen, the full member, or only a local surface. A simple correlation does not establish causation. Report sampling variation separately from variation in the real production process.

§3.5 Segregation and bleeding

3.2 Segregation and bleeding

Physical explanation

Segregation occurs when coarse aggregate, mortar and water lose a homogeneous spatial distribution during handling. Bleeding is upward migration of water; it can create weak surface zones and channels under reinforcement. Mixture cohesion, aggregate distribution, vertical drop, vibration energy and finish timing all matter. Changes to one variable can improve workability and simultaneously worsen stability.

Worked engineering interpretation

A contractor reports coarse aggregate collected beneath a chute while cement-rich mortar accumulated at the leading edge. Adding water without diagnosis may aggravate the condition. Compare discharge sequence, aggregate moisture, gradation, travel time and admixture dosing before approving any adjustment.

§3.6 Setting and time-dependent response

3.3 Setting and time-dependent response

Physical explanation

Setting is the transition from a mobile material to a stiffening skeleton, whereas measurable strength gain continues afterward. Hot weather changes reaction rates and evaporation; mixing time, cement chemistry, admixture compatibility and haul duration also influence setting. False set and flash set are different mechanisms and require different diagnoses.

Worked engineering interpretation

At 32°C a delivery arrives 45 minutes later than planned and exhibits rapid slump loss. Simply dosing additional water may violate agreed proportions. The appropriate investigation includes a time-stamped batch record, concrete temperature, admixture history and a trial of approved procedures.

§3.7 Rheology and specialized mixtures

3.4 Rheology and specialized mixtures

Physical explanation

Rheological descriptions often use yield stress and plastic viscosity to characterize fresh concrete flow. Self-consolidating mixtures must flow under their own weight and resist segregation while passing congested reinforcement. A mixture that spreads readily may still block at an obstruction or segregate after placement. The relevant tests are selected for the intended operation rather than for the convenience of one quick measurement.

Worked engineering interpretation

Suppose trial mixture A has large spread but obvious aggregate accumulation at the perimeter, while B is slightly less fluid and remains homogeneous. A performance-based decision should not automatically prefer A; blocking and stability evidence can be more important than maximum flow.

§3.9 Extended reading: how fresh properties govern placement decisions

Extended reading: how fresh properties govern placement decisions

Workability is not a scalar

Fresh concrete is a suspension of particles in reactive paste. Its behavior depends on stress level, shear history, temperature, elapsed time and the interaction of particles with admixtures. Slump measures a particular deformation under gravity after standardized handling; it does not separately report yield stress, viscosity, segregation resistance, pumpability or passing behavior through reinforcement. A mixture can show a large slump and still segregate. Another can have a moderate slump yet consolidate well with the planned equipment. The appropriate question is whether the concrete can be transported, placed and finished in the required member under the actual site process.

Rheological discussions often begin with a Bingham-like approximation: after yield, applied shear stress is an intercept plus plastic viscosity times shear rate. That educational representation allows students to distinguish the onset of flow from resistance during continued deformation. Actual fresh concrete can exhibit thixotropy, structural buildup, shear-dependent behavior and changes during hydration. A fitted Bingham curve might approximate a given observation range without describing the behavior after rest or repeated pumping. Therefore the lab is a mathematical comparison tool, not a numerical translation from rheometer settings to slump or a prediction of formwork pressure.

Time sequence must be documented

Consider a truck arriving after a traffic delay. Workability measurements at the plant, arrival and discharge may differ even if no extra water was added. Temperature affects reaction rate and evaporation, prolonged mixing changes air and dispersion, and the timing of admixture addition can change the observed response. For any comparison, the data sheet should include batching timestamp, water additions, temperature, equipment and testing timestamp. Without these records, it is easy to blame a cement source for a slump-loss pattern that actually depends on transport or testing time.

It is also necessary to distinguish setting from loss of apparent flow. A mixture may lose mobility while the bulk chemical reactions are still developing; changes in flocculation can affect the fresh skeleton without implying that its final set has been reached. Conversely, a delayed set is not necessarily evidence of adequate workability during placement. Process decisions require separate monitoring of those attributes. Any extra water or chemical admixture proposed on site needs to be authorized within the applicable mix specifications and recorded so subsequent strength and durability results can be interpreted.

Stability and segregation are separate design objectives

A paste-rich mixture may flow readily yet suffer bleeding or plastic settlement, while one with insufficient fine material may shed coarse particles during transport. Segregation causes spatial changes in composition and can make the surface and interior behave differently. Excessive vibration can drive separation in an unstable mixture; insufficient vibration can leave air pockets and honeycombing. The correct compaction strategy therefore depends on both the material and the element geometry. The laboratory should study how aggregate blend, paste volume, viscosity modifying admixtures and water reducer doses affect stability under representative handling.

Bleeding deserves particular attention near slab surfaces and horizontal reinforcement. Migrating water can create weak layers or local channels. Premature finishing may trap surface water beneath a worked surface, leading to delamination or dusting. The presence of a visible surface sheen should be investigated before finishing rather than hidden with additional cement or dry dusting. Site training should include recognizing the condition, recording environmental effects and adjusting the timing of operations in an authorized way.

Self-consolidating concrete requires multiple checks

Self-consolidating systems are designed to fill and pass through obstacles under their own weight while resisting segregation. Flowing ability, passing ability and stability are distinct. A high spread diameter is not sufficient proof that large particles will pass congested reinforcement. A mixture can block even when it looks fluid in an unobstructed test. Relevant trial measurements and full-scale mockups help reveal how aggregate size, bar spacing, viscosity and admixture compatibility interact.

For a learning trial, select two mixes with equal nominal w/cm but different fine-particle distribution. Record the flow curve and a stability observation after a fixed rest period. Predict which mix may require more vibration and what quality defect would be likely if the wrong procedure were applied. The conclusions should be stated as hypotheses until mockup or field measurements support them. This discipline prevents a single convenient test result from standing in for the entire process.

§3.10 Integrated case 3 — Two concrete mixtures share one slump result

Integrated case 3 — Two concrete mixtures share one slump result

Project setting. A bridge parapet contains closely spaced reinforcement near several anchor pockets. The subcontractor proposes two concrete mixtures that each show a nominal 180 mm slump shortly after mixing. The site team assumes they are interchangeable. During a trial pour, one produces visible mortar accumulation with coarse particles settling in some regions; the other fills difficult spaces more evenly, though it initially appears slightly harder to push along the form.

Reasoning from the evidence. Equal slump does not prove equal stability, viscosity, passing ability or retention. The first material may have inadequate cohesion, a grading imbalance or too much effective water. The second could be appropriately proportioned but may still have poor performance after an extended pumping period. The correct comparison requires timed flow tests, a representative formwork mockup, records of aggregate moisture, air and temperature, and evaluation of segregation. The cause cannot be inferred from visual appearance alone.

Use of the rheology model. Consider a simplified Bingham description with a yield stress of 80 Pa and plastic viscosity of 16 Pa·s. At a shear rate of 5 s⁻¹, the calculated stress is 80 + 16×5 = 160 Pa. A second hypothetical material with yield stress 50 Pa and viscosity 26 Pa·s gives 180 Pa at the same rate. Which flows more easily depends on the applied stress and deformation regime; the curves may cross at different rates. The model illustrates that two rheological parameters are needed to describe even this simplified system. It cannot convert those values to a standardized slump or predict a reinforcement-blocking threshold.

Field trial. Design a short testing schedule including an initial fresh test, a repeat after expected transport time, observation after passing a congested mockup, and a stability check. Keep mixing history as consistent as possible. Document visible segregation, voids and the amount of intervention needed. Define in advance which defect would lead to rejection or a design revision under the project specification. Avoid correcting a problem by undocumented water addition, because the altered mixture may no longer correspond to tested strength and durability evidence.

Decision point. If both mixtures can meet measured strength, their suitability may still differ because of placement. Prefer the material with demonstrated filling and stability under the intended operations rather than the one with the highest free-space flow number. State what additional tests are needed before full production. Students should also identify how a poorly prepared mockup could create an apparent material failure and design a repeat to separate the two explanations.
