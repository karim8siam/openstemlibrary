§2.1 Theoretical Framework: Hydration, Heat and Microstructure

Core investigation: Early-age thermal and curing case.

Unit framework. Hydration evolves spatially because capillary water, reaction products and temperature are not uniform. Early thermal data cannot uniquely determine phase composition.

Capstone evidence task. Construct two possible explanations for an apparent reduction in heat release: slower intrinsic reaction or reduced thermal mass. Identify measurements that distinguish them.

§2.2 Learning outcomes

Learning outcomes

At the end of this unit, explain each of its four topics without substituting a numerical model for measured concrete behavior; solve the displayed material balance or test interpretation with consistent units; choose a verification measurement; and document why a field conclusion may differ from the classroom scenario.

§2.4 Hydration reaction pathways

2.1 Hydration reaction pathways

Physical explanation

Hydration is a coupled dissolution, nucleation and growth process that forms calcium silicate hydrate, calcium hydroxide and aluminate-containing phases. A single “percent reacted” number can be pedagogically useful, but it cannot represent all phase-dependent reactions. Some particles remain unhydrated because of pore-water availability and diffusion limits; curing history affects the reachable degree of reaction.

Worked engineering interpretation

A model sets the asymptotic reacted fraction to 0.85 and uses alpha(t)=0.85×[1-exp(-(0.08t)^1.2)] with t in days. At day 7 the model gives about 0.34. The exponent and rate are illustrative parameters, not a universal hydration law or field specification.

Application A: precast yard

Consider precast yard, where the central practical constraints include demoulding schedule, moisture retention, batch consistency and accelerated curing. The lesson on hydration reaction pathways becomes a decision only after a responsible engineer states the exposure, intended function, material evidence and tolerable uncertainty. For this case start by identifying which quantities come from a mixture record and which depend on field construction. Make a table with the current state, intended change and predicted consequence. Do not equate a clean theoretical trend with successful placement: operation-specific limits can reverse the preferred option.

A useful thought experiment is to compare an apparently favorable laboratory value against an observed field defect. If the laboratory result supports the material but the member shows insufficient consolidation or cracking, the next investigation is not to discard the observation. Look for restrained slab formwork, finishing timing, transport history and the position of reinforcement; these can make the same nominal composition behave differently. State a falsifiable prediction for each alternative. For example, if the issue arises from a changed moisture condition, repeated batch-level corrections should reduce the discrepancy while other variables remain controlled.

Now transfer the principle to mid-rise residential slab. The governing constraint changes to restrained slab formwork, finishing timing, transport history and the position of reinforcement. §2.5 Water availability, pores and curing

2.2 Water availability, pores and curing

Physical explanation

Pore structure evolves because hydration consumes water and creates solid reaction products, while evaporation may interrupt reactions near the exposed surface. Continuous connected capillaries can facilitate fluid transport, but porosity, connectivity and transport coefficients are not identical. Surface wetting may have little effect on deep internal humidity during a very short curing event.

Worked engineering interpretation

Two specimens begin with the same w/cm and cement content. One is sealed for a week; the second is exposed to dry wind after one day. Even if their 28-day compressive strengths happen to match, their surface sorptivity and resistance to ingress may differ. Compare a transport test rather than treating strength alone as a complete durability measure.

§2.6 Heat generation and thermal gradients

2.3 Heat generation and thermal gradients

Physical explanation

Hydration is exothermic. Heat accumulates in large members when the surface cannot remove it as quickly as the interior releases it. The resulting temperature rise and later cooling create restrained strain; the risk depends on geometry, thermal conductivity, tensile capacity, restraint and ambient conditions. A simplified lumped-temperature model is appropriate only when internal temperature can reasonably be represented by one state.

Worked engineering interpretation

If 400 kg/m3 binder releases 220 kJ/kg during a chosen period, the released energy is 88 MJ/m3. With approximate volumetric heat capacity 2.4 MJ/(m3 K), an adiabatic temperature rise would be 36.7 K. This is an upper-bound classroom calculation for that assumed reaction heat; real members lose heat.

§2.7 Microstructure characterization

2.4 Microstructure characterization

Physical explanation

Microstructural evidence comes from multiple methods: polished sections can reveal cracks and air voids; X-ray diffraction identifies crystalline phases; thermogravimetry helps quantify selected reaction products; electron microscopy shows texture. Sample preparation can change the structure being observed, especially through drying and polishing artifacts. No single image should be used as a complete proof of durability.

Worked engineering interpretation

A set of images shows apparent reduction in large voids after an altered curing regime. Before claiming an improvement, quantify sampled area, segmentation threshold, specimen history and repeatability. Measurements at micron resolution cannot directly measure the full connected pore network responsible for chloride transport.

§2.9 Extended reading: hydration experiments and evidence quality

Extended reading: hydration experiments and evidence quality

Reaction progress is not one number

The convenient image of cement as a powder that uniformly “sets” hides a set of reactions beginning at different rates. Silicate reaction products develop a binding skeleton, while aluminate chemistry and sulfate availability influence early behavior. As hydration progresses, water has to move through an evolving porous network toward unreacted regions. Consequently two cements can reach similar strengths after several weeks through different early pathways. A single degree-of-reaction curve is useful for teaching sensitivity but cannot resolve phase-dependent reactions or substitute for calorimetry, X-ray phase analysis, thermogravimetry and microstructural examination used together.

An isothermal calorimeter measures a heat flow rate relative to its instrument method; integration gives cumulative heat under particular conditions. The curve often contains periods of low apparent activity, acceleration and subsequent deceleration. Peaks should not be assigned blindly to exact reactions because cement composition, admixtures and supplementary binders modify timing and overlap. For a trial matrix, keep water-to-binder ratio and test temperature controlled while changing one material parameter at a time. Look for the pattern across replicates rather than reporting one peak as a fundamental constant of the mixture. A separate specimen program can examine when changes in calorimetric response are reflected in strength and resistivity.

Temperature history changes the meaning of age

A concrete element at 12°C is not equivalent to an otherwise identical element at 30°C after the same number of clock hours. Maturity methods summarize aspects of the temperature-time history; the Nurse–Saul formulation accumulates the temperature above a chosen datum. It is convenient for exposing the difference between calendar age and curing temperature, but the resulting index cannot determine strength until a relationship is calibrated for that mixture using appropriate tests. A mixture change invalidates simple carryover of the calibration. Extremely cold temperatures, high early temperatures and changes in reaction pathways also limit a simplistic interpretation of one index.

The lab's synthetic temperature curve deliberately offers three inputs: mean temperature, daily swing and datum temperature. If the datum is changed, the numerical index changes even though the actual temperature record does not. This is not a failure of mathematics; it reveals that the index depends on a chosen model. Students should plot the same physical temperature history with two proposed datum values, explain the difference, and make clear why a contractor cannot use an arbitrary maturity threshold as a release criterion.

Heat balance and real temperature gradients

An adiabatic estimate divides released energy per cubic metre by volumetric heat capacity. It gives a useful upper-bound scale if the assumed heat is consistent with the period being considered. However, real large members exchange heat with formwork, air, soil and curing water. Temperature gradients, not just the maximum concrete temperature, are critical because the core and surface can expand and cool differently. Thermal strain may be partly free and partly restrained by adjacent material. Early tensile strength and creep are changing simultaneously, so one fixed elastic stress calculation is inadequate for crack prediction.

A lumped model assumes spatial uniformity and balances heating against one heat-loss coefficient. It is intentionally a learning model rather than a solution for a three-dimensional raft foundation. Students should vary binder content and heat-loss factor to identify the direction of the peak-temperature change, then draw a member section with core and surface sensor positions. A separate thermal analysis would need geometry, thermal diffusivity, boundary convection and actual heat-generation kinetics. A concrete temperature sensor should be cross-checked against an ambient record; a mislocated probe can exaggerate or hide a gradient.

Curing is moisture management, not a calendar ritual

Hydration needs suitable moisture and temperature. Curing practices might include retaining water, limiting evaporation with membranes, wet coverings, or controlling environmental exposure. The chosen method should match the geometry, surface access, temperature and desired performance. Losing moisture early near a surface can have consequences for transport and abrasion even when later cylinder strengths appear reasonable. Moisture supply is especially significant for dense systems that may self-desiccate. A claim such as “cured seven days” is incomplete without evidence of what humidity or temperature the concrete actually experienced.

An effective comparison of curing methods uses matched mixtures, recorded initial conditions, replicated specimens and at least one measurement sensitive to surface quality. A strength test alone may miss the effect of poor near-surface curing on absorption. The response variable must match the mechanism being investigated. Record when curing began, how continuity was maintained, and any interruption from finishing or early exposure. Those operational details are often more valuable than another digit in the assumed hydration-model rate constant.

§2.10 Integrated case 2 — Thermal monitoring of an insulated concrete foundation

Integrated case 2 — Thermal monitoring of an insulated concrete foundation

Project setting. A moderately large foundation is placed under warm daytime conditions, while the forecast predicts a cool night and a windy morning. The mix contains enough binder to generate significant heat during its early reaction. Construction managers want to remove formwork quickly, but the engineering team is concerned about temperature differences between the interior and surface. Their initial model predicts one uniform concrete temperature.

First estimate. Consider 390 kg/m³ binder and an assumed cumulative heat release of 230 kJ/kg over the investigated early period. Released energy is 89.7 MJ/m³. Dividing by a hypothetical volumetric heat capacity of 2.4 MJ/(m³·K) gives a no-loss temperature-rise scale near 37.4 K. This is a useful check on order of magnitude, not a prediction of a real peak. The actual reaction rate changes with temperature and binder composition, and heat moves to soil, formwork and the exposed environment. A concrete section is not a perfectly insulated laboratory calorimeter.

Measurement strategy. Place calibrated temperature sensors at several depths from core to surface, adding an ambient sensor outside direct solar heating. Record timestamps and elevations so the thermal profile is reconstructable. Log whether insulation, wet coverings or wind barriers change during curing. A single sensor at an arbitrary point cannot demonstrate that the gradient is controlled. Compare the measured response with a simplified lumped result only to identify the missing physics: internal conduction and boundary transfer. If gradients are important, use a spatial transient heat-conduction analysis supported by material thermal properties and measured boundary conditions.

Curing and construction choices. Form removal may increase surface cooling while core temperature remains high. Added insulation may reduce a steep gradient but could increase peak internal temperature if it substantially limits heat dissipation. These are competing effects requiring a project-specific analysis. Any maturity-strength relationship used to plan removal needs calibration for the actual mixture and relevant testing; the arbitrary model settings in a classroom lab are not adequate. Document the basis of the concrete's assumed early tensile properties and restraint conditions when interpreting cracking risk.

Investigation. Change binder mass and cooling factor in the heat-model laboratory and note peak timing as well as peak magnitude. Then sketch two plausible cross-section temperature profiles with different surface conditions. Ask which profile might create stronger restrained tension even if it produces a lower mean concrete temperature. A useful final report should provide measured conditions, assumptions, uncertainty and the escalation condition for independent thermal evaluation.
