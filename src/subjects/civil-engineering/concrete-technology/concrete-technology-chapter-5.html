§5.1 Theoretical Framework: Hardened Concrete Mechanics

Core investigation: Cylinder results and long-term movement.

Unit framework. Failure under compression is not the only performance state: cracking and long-term deformation can govern serviceability. Material variation may be spatial and age dependent.

Capstone evidence task. Write two separate evidence chains: one for accepting a strength test and one for predicting restrained long-term deformation.

Laboratory and field interpretation

Propose specimens and environmental monitoring to estimate the additional properties, identify early-age and drying shrinkage mechanisms, and show how restraint changes stress from free deformation.

The experiments in this unit deliberately distinguish physical observation from a mathematical surrogate. Before using a laboratory to defend a structural or construction decision, document the material source, temperatures, specimen dimensions, test method, reference basis and limitations of calibration. Record negative results and unexpected trends: such outcomes are often more informative than a tidy curve that happens to fit the assumed model.

§5.4 Compressive strength and testing

5.1 Compressive strength and testing

Physical explanation

Compressive strength depends on specimen geometry, moisture and curing conditions, loading procedure and material state. A core taken from a structure is not automatically equivalent to a laboratory-cured cylinder. Strength test scatter comes from both real production variation and measurement variation; a single low result should trigger investigation, not arithmetic manipulation.

Worked engineering interpretation

For a cylinder 100 mm in diameter carrying 470 kN at failure, loaded area is π(0.100)^2/4=0.007854 m2. Nominal compressive stress is 470000/0.007854=59.84 MPa. This computed stress is not a corrected acceptance strength without the applicable specimen and test requirements.

Application A: mid-rise residential slab

Consider mid-rise residential slab, where the central practical constraints include restrained slab formwork, finishing timing, transport history and the position of reinforcement. The lesson on compressive strength and testing becomes a decision only after a responsible engineer states the exposure, intended function, material evidence and tolerable uncertainty. For this case start by identifying which quantities come from a mixture record and which depend on field construction. Make a table with the current state, intended change and predicted consequence. Do not equate a clean theoretical trend with successful placement: operation-specific limits can reverse the preferred option.

A useful thought experiment is to compare an apparently favorable laboratory value against an observed field defect. If the laboratory result supports the material but the member shows insufficient consolidation or cracking, the next investigation is not to discard the observation. Look for demoulding schedule, moisture retention, batch consistency and accelerated curing; these can make the same nominal composition behave differently. State a falsifiable prediction for each alternative. For example, if the issue arises from a changed moisture condition, repeated batch-level corrections should reduce the discrepancy while other variables remain controlled.

Application B: precast yard

Now transfer the principle to precast yard. The governing constraint changes to demoulding schedule, moisture retention, batch consistency and accelerated curing. For the measurement lens: Choose at least two measurements that respond to different mechanisms. State exactly what each test measures, in which units, and whether the test observes a specimen, the full member, or only a local surface. A simple correlation does not establish causation. Report sampling variation separately from variation in the real production process.

§5.5 Modulus, tensile strength and fracture

5.2 Modulus, tensile strength and fracture

Physical explanation

Concrete is not an ideal linear elastic solid. Initial tangent, secant and dynamic moduli are different quantities, and they vary with composition, age and moisture. Concrete is weak in direct tension relative to compression, and cracking localizes because fracture energy and aggregate interlock matter. A simple elastic model can be useful before cracking but should not be extended uncritically beyond its domain.

Worked engineering interpretation

At 15 MPa applied compressive stress and an assumed secant modulus of 30 GPa, axial strain in a linear teaching model is 15/30000=0.00050 or 500 microstrain. An actual stress–strain curve bends with increasing load, so the modulus must be referenced to the intended stress interval.

§5.6 Creep and shrinkage

5.3 Creep and shrinkage

Physical explanation

Shrinkage reflects several mechanisms including drying and autogenous processes. Creep is increasing strain under sustained stress, with size, humidity, age at loading and material composition influencing response. When deformation is restrained, these volume changes can create tensile stress and cracking. Simple exponential or logarithmic laws illustrate trends but are not validated design models.

Worked engineering interpretation

If a fully restrained 300 microstrain free shrinkage were converted elastically with E=30 GPa, the nominal stress would be 9 MPa. That naive result is often unrealistic because creep, cracking and restraint compliance relax the stress. The example demonstrates why a free-shrinkage measurement alone does not calculate field crack width.

§5.7 Long-term performance and uncertainty

5.4 Long-term performance and uncertainty

Physical explanation

Elastic modulus, strength, creep and shrinkage are correlated only imperfectly. An engineer should document measured properties, test conditions, fit parameters and confidence intervals before extrapolating to long service lives. Environmental exposures and construction sequences can dominate small differences in predicted material properties.

Worked engineering interpretation

A model calibrated with 28-day and 56-day specimens should not claim to predict 40-year creep from those observations alone. Evaluate sensitivity to humidity and member size, compare alternative models, and retain evidence of how specimens were handled.

§5.9 Extended reading: strength testing and long-term deformation

Extended reading: strength testing and long-term deformation

What compression testing actually reports

A compressive test divides a measured failure load by a prescribed loaded area, but the interpretation rests on specimen geometry, preparation, moisture condition and test procedure. Cylinders, cubes and extracted cores are not automatically interchangeable. End preparation and alignment can affect the stress field in the specimen, while testing equipment and loading rate contribute measurement variation. Field-cured and standard-cured specimens answer different questions. A strength test is consequently a combination of material evidence and measurement-system evidence; a low result should trigger review of both.

Strength develops over time and under different curing histories. The phrase “28-day strength” is incomplete without the specimen type and curing history. Laboratory measurements may be used to establish a mixture's potential, while actual in-place behavior can be influenced by temperature, consolidation and moisture. Acceptance decisions are governed by the applicable specifications, testing methods and relevant jurisdiction; they should not be inferred from the existence of an isolated favorable result. A responsible report lists specimen identifiers, casting and testing times, dimensions, failure loads, calculated stresses and unusual failure descriptions.

Elastic modulus depends on how it is measured

A loading curve in concrete is not perfectly linear. A secant modulus relates stress and strain between defined points, while a tangent modulus relates to a local slope. Dynamic tests probe a different response regime. Aggregate stiffness, volume, microcracking and moisture can alter the observed modulus even among concretes with similar compressive strength. It is therefore not defensible to convert one strength measurement into a precision deflection prediction through a universal formula when a project demands reliable deformation estimates.

An elastic approximation still teaches useful mechanics. Under a stated stress and secant modulus, predicted strain is stress divided by modulus. Students should keep MPa and GPa consistent, then use a strain gauge or displacement measurement to see where the linear idealization ceases to fit. As loading grows, microcrack development can curve the stress–strain relation before a visible macrocrack appears. Observing that curvature is not a mathematical nuisance; it is part of the material response that a designer must understand.

Shrinkage and creep are different processes

Shrinkage can occur without external mechanical loading, while creep is time-dependent deformation under sustained stress. Drying and autogenous shrinkage have different moisture and reaction drivers. A thick member and a thin member cast from the same concrete may experience different internal moisture gradients, so their strains and cracking patterns may differ. Age at loading also matters because the matrix becomes stiffer and its creep potential evolves as hydration proceeds.

A simplistic multiplication of free shrinkage strain by modulus and restraint can produce a large estimated stress. Real members relax some stress through creep, cracking, reinforcement action and movement of supporting elements. The degree of restraint is spatially variable. A model for restrained shrinkage should ideally include environmental history, member dimensions, stiffness evolution and an appropriate relaxation relationship. The educational stress envelope deliberately applies a fixed relaxation factor to expose sensitivity, but this assumption must never be treated as a design value.

Crack control is not the same as high strength

A stronger concrete may possess a higher modulus and suffer greater thermal or autogenous strain under some circumstances. If the mixture cracks early, its later transport performance can be compromised regardless of its high measured compressive strength. This does not mean high strength is undesirable; it means compressive strength must be evaluated together with tensile resistance, fracture behavior, shrinkage, restraint and detailing. The best mixture for an unrestrained compression cylinder may not be the best mixture for a massive, restrained structural element.

To investigate a serviceability concern, design a specimen program that separately measures strength, modulus, shrinkage and creep under controlled humidity and temperature. Compare field observations of cracks with construction timing and reinforcement layout. In the final report state which mechanism is supported by the evidence and which remains speculative. An unsupported diagnosis can lead to costly repairs that fail to treat the root cause.

§5.10 Integrated case 5 — A high-strength slab cracks early

Integrated case 5 — A high-strength slab cracks early

Project setting. Two concretes are proposed for a heavily restrained floor. The high-strength candidate has a lower nominal w/cm and a higher reported cylinder strength. A trial panel, however, shows fine cracking shortly after early exposure, while the lower-strength candidate remains visually intact at that time. The project team asks whether the cracking can be ignored because the higher-strength mix exceeds the compressive requirement.

Mechanisms to test. First establish when cracks appeared relative to setting, finishing, restraint and curing. Plastic shrinkage is possible before adequate strength development if evaporation outruns available surface water. Thermal contraction can create restraint after an early temperature peak. Drying and autogenous shrinkage may develop at different rates and with different internal moisture histories. A high compressive strength is not a proof of low shrinkage, adequate tensile performance or absence of cracking. Geometry, reinforcement and surface conditions must be included.

Stress sensitivity. For a deliberately simplistic calculation, consider 350 microstrain free shrinkage, elastic modulus 30 GPa and effective restraint fraction 0.55. Multiplication without relaxation would give 30,000 MPa×0.000350×0.55 ≈ 5.78 MPa nominal stress. Applying a teaching relaxation fraction of 45% reduces it to about 3.18 MPa. Neither value is a defensible direct predictor of actual crack width. Concrete stiffness, creep, tensile capacity, gradients and cracking change with time; the assumed effective restraint is itself difficult to establish.

Evidence program. Prepare matched specimens for compressive strength, modulus, shrinkage under controlled humidity, and any appropriate restrained-cracking test. Measure concrete temperature during the early period and log curing start and interruptions. Affected panels should be mapped for crack width, orientation, position and timing. Compare environmental data across panels rather than assuming the difference lies only in binder content. Where deflection matters, include sustained-load deformation evidence rather than relying solely on elastic modulus inferred from strength.

Practical conclusion. The preferred concrete is the one that meets all the required service conditions with acceptable constructability, not necessarily the one with the largest cylinder-strength number. If the high-strength mixture can be modified to control early cracking while retaining the advantages, retest the altered mixture. If not, the alternative may be more reliable. Students should state separately what the observations establish, which mechanisms remain hypotheses and which test outcome would lead them to change their recommendation.
