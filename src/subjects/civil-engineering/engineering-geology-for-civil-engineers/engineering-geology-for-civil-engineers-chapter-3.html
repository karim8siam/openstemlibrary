§3.1 Theoretical Foundations: Structural Geology, Folds, Faults and Geological Field Mapping

Faults, folds, bedding and fractures can steer groundwater flow, isolate weak rock, or define block geometry. Structural mapping is therefore about predicting orientations and continuity at the engineering scale, not only identifying geological history.

§3.2 Outcomes and a practical engineering question

Outcomes and a practical engineering question

By the end of this unit, a student should be able to translate the geologic observations in structural geology and geological mapping into a documented alternative ground model, quantify at least one relevant sensitivity, and explain what additional evidence would change the decision. The goal is not memorization of terms but a defendable sequence: observation → interpretation → process model → engineering implication → uncertainty.

A usable field record contains the datum, location, measurement method, orientation or depth, date, observer, evidence strength and confidence. A drawing without those attributes is difficult to audit when field conditions change.

§3.3 Four connected lessons

Four connected lessons

3.1 Strike, dip, trend and plunge

Core physical interpretation. Orientations are angles in three-dimensional space; specify reference north, right-hand rule, inclination sign and whether a section is normal or oblique to strike. Apparent dip depends on section azimuth.

Civil-engineering case. An oblique roadcut shows an apparently shallow layer though its true dip is steep; an unwary designer may mislocate the contact at excavation depth.

Common error. The apparent-dip formula assumes planar bedding and correct azimuth conventions.

Recommended observational method. Check measurement signs with a sketch and convert field readings to a consistent convention before stereographic plotting.

From field observation to a working ground model. Begin by recording what could actually be seen or measured when investigating strike, dip, trend and plunge. In a coastal embankment, the team may encounter salinity-sensitive pore fluid while tidal groundwater variation changes boundary conditions. Divide the record into physical evidence, a preferred interpretation, and at least one plausible competing explanation. For example, a curved reflectivity anomaly, a change in drilling return or a wet joint might be consistent with multiple geological situations. A good model therefore predicts several independent observables rather than treating one indirect signal as a unique material label.

Scale and engineering decision. The scale of the question for strike, dip, trend and plunge must match the scale of the decision. A hand specimen can establish mineral type or intact strength but cannot prove the continuity of a weak layer over a bridge footprint. Conversely, a regional map can reveal structural trends but rarely resolves a narrow fracture zone at a tunnel face. Define whether the decision concerns a meter-scale foundation, a tens-of-meters roadcut, or a kilometre-long alignment, then select mapping, boring, laboratory testing and monitoring accordingly. State the range of plausible boundary positions and explain how each affects stability, excavation, drainage and the construction sequence.

Measurement protocol. For strike, dip, trend and plunge, first state the expected range and diagnostic features, then calibrate instruments and confirm the reference datum. Photograph the observation with a scale and north indication, record adverse as well as favorable evidence, and label samples so the record can be reconstructed. Preserve original orientation data before transformation. Where the first interpretation is uncertain, obtain an independent check rather than selecting a value just because it fits a familiar textbook category. Compare day-to-day conditions when moisture or stress changes could affect what is measured.

Quantitative reasoning. Choose a calculation proportional to the physical process instead of using a formula because it is available. Write the dimensions, units, reference directions and boundary assumptions before substituting numbers. Work with ranges for the parameters least constrained by field evidence. A sensitivity plot can reveal which parameters matter most, but its smoothness is not evidence that the geological variation is smooth. Every plotted result should be traced to the original observation, the simplifying constitutive law, and a check against an independent physical limit.

Interpretation and risk. In coastal embankment settings, a mistaken assessment of salinity-sensitive pore fluid may be more consequential under tidal groundwater variation. Define a failure of the working hypothesis: for example, repeated water-level readings contradict the assumed flow direction, measured joint planes diverge from the mapped structural domain, or a test pit intersects a contact outside its expected depth interval. Explain the difference between an uncertainty that merely widens a construction cost range and one that introduces a new safety-critical failure mechanism. Assign new evidence collection to the latter first.

Alternative hypothesis exercise. Hypothesis A assumes strike, dip, trend and plunge follows the dominant regional trend; hypothesis B assumes a local feature interrupts that trend. Draw a vertical section for both, label what is observed versus extrapolated, and compare the expected outcome of a borehole, scanline or geophysical traverse. Record what measurement would falsify either interpretation. The useful result is a testable prediction—not a forced choice between unverified assumptions.

Field-report exercise. Draft a one-page section called “Evidence and limits for Strike, dip, trend and plunge.” Include the measurement conditions, the most consequential model uncertainty, the observation that would reduce it, and the implication for a civil-engineering decision. Explain why the quantities should not be interpreted as final design parameters without project-specific evidence and qualified review.

3.2 Fault zones and displacement

Core physical interpretation. A fault comprises a core, associated damage zone and host rock; engineering response varies across all three. Offset markers reveal relative displacement but do not always determine the true slip vector.

Civil-engineering case. A dam tunnel encounters clay gouge and increased inflow along a mapped fault, even where intact rock on both sides is competent.

Common error. A mapped lineament is not proof of an active fault; activity and hazard require separate evidence.

Recommended observational method. Characterize fault width, gouge mineralogy, permeability contrast, orientation and evidence for recent movement.

3.3 Kinematic screening of rock slopes

Core physical interpretation. Planar, wedge and toppling failures require specific geometry between slope faces and discontinuities. A stereographic screening can flag geometrically feasible modes but it cannot establish stability without forces and strength.

Civil-engineering case. A bedding plane dips out of a 65-degree rock cut at 34 degrees with low roughness and seeping water.

Common error. Do not treat a daylighting check as a factor-of-safety calculation or ignore joint persistence.

Recommended observational method. Use multiple joint measurements, field-defined friction ranges and a stereonet or vector method.

3.4 Spatial uncertainty between observations

Core physical interpretation. Geological interpolation between boreholes, exposures and geophysics is underdetermined. Confidence intervals widen where contacts are complex, sampling is sparse or extrapolation crosses a structural boundary.

Civil-engineering case. Two boreholes 80 m apart encounter similar sandstone yet a buried channel could occupy their midpoint.

Common error. A smooth interpolated cross-section is a communication device, not evidence that all contacts are smooth.

Recommended observational method. Develop at least two alternative plausible models and target new investigations where their consequences differ.

§3.4 Integration of the two interactive laboratories

Integration of the two interactive laboratories

This unit uses Lab 05 and Lab 06. Both are stand-alone offline models. Start with the default case, predict the direction of change, perform an input sweep, and export the result. Critically compare the model to field observations: which assumptions are physically justified and which idealizations are merely convenient?

A laboratory is not complete until the student has submitted the predicted trend, the observed numerical response with units, a physical explanation, an estimate of sensitivity, and a paragraph on why the result cannot be applied directly to a real project.

§3.5 Synthesis case: competing geological explanations

Synthesis case: competing geological explanations

A team is examining a urban excavation affected by buried fill and utility corridors. The investigation was conducted while pumping-induced drawdown was relatively quiet, creating a risk that the observed state is not representative of the critical construction condition. The team has a preliminary map, two discrete observations and a short monitoring record. Neither spatial continuity nor transient behavior is fully constrained.

Construct three competing cross-sections: one with laterally continuous units, one with an abrupt structural boundary, and one with a buried erosional or weathering feature. For each cross-section, list the predicted drill response, the predicted groundwater response, and the construction problem that would arise if it were true. Rank only the value of information, not a numerical probability unsupported by data. Identify the most efficient next test and the observations that would cause you to revise the construction method.

Finally write a limitations paragraph for a geotechnical or engineering-geology report. This paragraph must say what was measured, what was inferred, the dates and groundwater conditions, and which assumptions need checking during construction. Separate a local observation from a regional geological generalization and make all units consistent.
