§6.1 Theoretical Foundations: Rock Slope Stability, Kinematic Analysis and Mass-Movement Hazards

Slope assessment connects geomorphic evidence, material properties, water pressures and event triggers. Some hazards involve small frequent failures and others rare large failures; a single static safety factor cannot summarize every consequence.

§6.2 Outcomes and a practical engineering question

Outcomes and a practical engineering question

By the end of this unit, a student should be able to translate the geologic observations in rock slope processes and mass-movement hazards into a documented alternative ground model, quantify at least one relevant sensitivity, and explain what additional evidence would change the decision. The goal is not memorization of terms but a defendable sequence: observation → interpretation → process model → engineering implication → uncertainty.

A usable field record contains the datum, location, measurement method, orientation or depth, date, observer, evidence strength and confidence. A drawing without those attributes is difficult to audit when field conditions change.

§6.3 Four connected lessons

Four connected lessons

6.1 Movement mechanisms and field indicators

Core physical interpretation. Falls, topples, planar slides, rotational slides, flows and spreads have different kinematic signatures. Cracks, tilted trees, displaced drains and fresh scarps can help reconstruct movement.

Civil-engineering case. A steep roadcut shows debris at the toe and open joints but no large persistent scar; block fall remains plausible.

Common error. Absence of historical records is not evidence of no hazard, especially where records are sparse.

Recommended observational method. Build a mechanism inventory tied to measured geometry, weathering, groundwater and event history.

From field observation to a working ground model. Begin by recording what could actually be seen or measured when investigating movement mechanisms and field indicators. In a rock tunnel, the team may encounter fault-related alteration while excavation sequence changes boundary conditions. Divide the record into physical evidence, a preferred interpretation, and at least one plausible competing explanation. For example, a curved reflectivity anomaly, a change in drilling return or a wet joint might be consistent with multiple geological situations. A good model therefore predicts several independent observables rather than treating one indirect signal as a unique material label.

Scale and engineering decision. The scale of the question for movement mechanisms and field indicators must match the scale of the decision. A hand specimen can establish mineral type or intact strength but cannot prove the continuity of a weak layer over a bridge footprint. Conversely, a regional map can reveal structural trends but rarely resolves a narrow fracture zone at a tunnel face. Define whether the decision concerns a meter-scale foundation, a tens-of-meters roadcut, or a kilometre-long alignment, then select mapping, boring, laboratory testing and monitoring accordingly. State the range of plausible boundary positions and explain how each affects stability, excavation, drainage and the construction sequence.

Measurement protocol. For movement mechanisms and field indicators, first state the expected range and diagnostic features, then calibrate instruments and confirm the reference datum. Photograph the observation with a scale and north indication, record adverse as well as favorable evidence, and label samples so the record can be reconstructed. Preserve original orientation data before transformation. Where the first interpretation is uncertain, obtain an independent check rather than selecting a value just because it fits a familiar textbook category. Compare day-to-day conditions when moisture or stress changes could affect what is measured.

Quantitative reasoning. Choose a calculation proportional to the physical process instead of using a formula because it is available. Write the dimensions, units, reference directions and boundary assumptions before substituting numbers. Work with ranges for the parameters least constrained by field evidence. A sensitivity plot can reveal which parameters matter most, but its smoothness is not evidence that the geological variation is smooth. Every plotted result should be traced to the original observation, the simplifying constitutive law, and a check against an independent physical limit.

Interpretation and risk. In rock tunnel settings, a mistaken assessment of fault-related alteration may be more consequential under excavation sequence. Define a failure of the working hypothesis: for example, repeated water-level readings contradict the assumed flow direction, measured joint planes diverge from the mapped structural domain, or a test pit intersects a contact outside its expected depth interval. Explain the difference between an uncertainty that merely widens a construction cost range and one that introduces a new safety-critical failure mechanism. Assign new evidence collection to the latter first.

Alternative hypothesis exercise. Hypothesis A assumes movement mechanisms and field indicators follows the dominant regional trend; hypothesis B assumes a local feature interrupts that trend. Draw a vertical section for both, label what is observed versus extrapolated, and compare the expected outcome of a borehole, scanline or geophysical traverse. Record what measurement would falsify either interpretation. The useful result is a testable prediction—not a forced choice between unverified assumptions.

Field-report exercise. Draft a one-page section called “Evidence and limits for Movement mechanisms and field indicators.” Include the measurement conditions, the most consequential model uncertainty, the observation that would reduce it, and the implication for a civil-engineering decision. Explain why the quantities should not be interpreted as final design parameters without project-specific evidence and qualified review.

6.2 Planar sliding resistance

Core physical interpretation. A planar rock block can be idealized with weight, joint cohesion, joint friction and uplift. This bounded model supports sensitivity analysis, not design approval for a natural rock slope.

Civil-engineering case. Rain raises water pressure along an already daylighting bedding surface, sharply lowering effective normal force.

Common error. Cohesion measured on small fresh specimens may be inappropriate for long weathered joints.

Recommended observational method. Bracket joint strength, pore-pressure scenarios and persistence; compare with mapped failure mechanisms.

6.3 Rockfall motion and protection concepts

Core physical interpretation. Rockfall depends on block shape, release mechanism, restitution, fragmentation, surface roughness and slope profile. Simple ballistic arcs help explain energy, not hazard reach or barrier design.

Civil-engineering case. A cut slope above a road releases blocks that bounce unpredictably after reaching a bench.

Common error. A single coefficient of restitution cannot represent every contact or rough rock shape.

Recommended observational method. For actual protection design use surveyed terrain, probabilistic trajectories, impact energies and qualified guidance.

6.4 Rainfall thresholds and monitoring

Core physical interpretation. Rainfall may elevate pore pressure, reduce suction or initiate erosion, often with time lags. Site-specific thresholds require historical event datasets and monitoring rather than a universal rain total.

Civil-engineering case. A slope is stable after an intense short storm yet moves after prolonged moderate rain that keeps the regolith saturated.

Common error. Rainfall correlations are not deterministic forecasts, particularly under changing land use.

Recommended observational method. Pair rainfall, pore pressure, displacement and antecedent moisture in the same time reference frame.

§6.4 Integration of the two interactive laboratories

Integration of the two interactive laboratories

This unit uses Lab 11 and Lab 12. Both are stand-alone offline models. Start with the default case, predict the direction of change, perform an input sweep, and export the result. Critically compare the model to field observations: which assumptions are physically justified and which idealizations are merely convenient?

A laboratory is not complete until the student has submitted the predicted trend, the observed numerical response with units, a physical explanation, an estimate of sensitivity, and a paragraph on why the result cannot be applied directly to a real project.

§6.5 Synthesis case: competing geological explanations

Synthesis case: competing geological explanations

A team is examining a reservoir abutment affected by fractured carbonate zones. The investigation was conducted while long-term seepage was relatively quiet, creating a risk that the observed state is not representative of the critical construction condition. The team has a preliminary map, two discrete observations and a short monitoring record. Neither spatial continuity nor transient behavior is fully constrained.

Construct three competing cross-sections: one with laterally continuous units, one with an abrupt structural boundary, and one with a buried erosional or weathering feature. For each cross-section, list the predicted drill response, the predicted groundwater response, and the construction problem that would arise if it were true. Rank only the value of information, not a numerical probability unsupported by data. Identify the most efficient next test and the observations that would cause you to revise the construction method.

Finally write a limitations paragraph for a geotechnical or engineering-geology report. This paragraph must say what was measured, what was inferred, the dates and groundwater conditions, and which assumptions need checking during construction. Separate a local observation from a regional geological generalization and make all units consistent.
