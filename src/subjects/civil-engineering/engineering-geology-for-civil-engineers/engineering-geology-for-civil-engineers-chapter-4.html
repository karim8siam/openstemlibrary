§4.1 Theoretical Foundations: Hydrogeology, Seepage Flow and Water-Rock Interaction Mechanics

Water couples geological structure with effective stress, weathering and construction response. Hydrogeologic observations are time-dependent, often affected by pumping, rainfall and excavation. Measurements need datum, instrument depth and a timestamp.

§4.2 Outcomes and a practical engineering question

Outcomes and a practical engineering question

By the end of this unit, a student should be able to translate the geologic observations in groundwater and water–rock interaction into a documented alternative ground model, quantify at least one relevant sensitivity, and explain what additional evidence would change the decision. The goal is not memorization of terms but a defendable sequence: observation → interpretation → process model → engineering implication → uncertainty.

A usable field record contains the datum, location, measurement method, orientation or depth, date, observer, evidence strength and confidence. A drawing without those attributes is difficult to audit when field conditions change.

§4.3 Four connected lessons

Four connected lessons

4.1 Hydraulic head and effective stress

Core physical interpretation. Hydraulic head is elevation plus pressure head; effective vertical stress approximates overburden minus pore-water pressure under suitable saturated conditions. Perched and confined water can violate simple water-table assumptions.

Civil-engineering case. A temporary excavation encounters artesian pressure below a low-permeability layer even though shallow standpipes appear dry.

Common error. A single measured water level is not a universal hydraulic boundary condition.

Recommended observational method. Plot piezometric head by screened horizon and review seasonal records and local drainage.

From field observation to a working ground model. Begin by recording what could actually be seen or measured when investigating hydraulic head and effective stress. In a rock tunnel, the team may encounter fault-related alteration while excavation sequence changes boundary conditions. Divide the record into physical evidence, a preferred interpretation, and at least one plausible competing explanation. For example, a curved reflectivity anomaly, a change in drilling return or a wet joint might be consistent with multiple geological situations. A good model therefore predicts several independent observables rather than treating one indirect signal as a unique material label.

Scale and engineering decision. The scale of the question for hydraulic head and effective stress must match the scale of the decision. A hand specimen can establish mineral type or intact strength but cannot prove the continuity of a weak layer over a bridge footprint. Conversely, a regional map can reveal structural trends but rarely resolves a narrow fracture zone at a tunnel face. Define whether the decision concerns a meter-scale foundation, a tens-of-meters roadcut, or a kilometre-long alignment, then select mapping, boring, laboratory testing and monitoring accordingly. State the range of plausible boundary positions and explain how each affects stability, excavation, drainage and the construction sequence.

Measurement protocol. For hydraulic head and effective stress, first state the expected range and diagnostic features, then calibrate instruments and confirm the reference datum. Photograph the observation with a scale and north indication, record adverse as well as favorable evidence, and label samples so the record can be reconstructed. Preserve original orientation data before transformation. Where the first interpretation is uncertain, obtain an independent check rather than selecting a value just because it fits a familiar textbook category. Compare day-to-day conditions when moisture or stress changes could affect what is measured.

Quantitative reasoning. Choose a calculation proportional to the physical process instead of using a formula because it is available. Write the dimensions, units, reference directions and boundary assumptions before substituting numbers. Work with ranges for the parameters least constrained by field evidence. A sensitivity plot can reveal which parameters matter most, but its smoothness is not evidence that the geological variation is smooth. Every plotted result should be traced to the original observation, the simplifying constitutive law, and a check against an independent physical limit.

Interpretation and risk. In rock tunnel settings, a mistaken assessment of fault-related alteration may be more consequential under excavation sequence. Define a failure of the working hypothesis: for example, repeated water-level readings contradict the assumed flow direction, measured joint planes diverge from the mapped structural domain, or a test pit intersects a contact outside its expected depth interval. Explain the difference between an uncertainty that merely widens a construction cost range and one that introduces a new safety-critical failure mechanism. Assign new evidence collection to the latter first.

Alternative hypothesis exercise. Hypothesis A assumes hydraulic head and effective stress follows the dominant regional trend; hypothesis B assumes a local feature interrupts that trend. Draw a vertical section for both, label what is observed versus extrapolated, and compare the expected outcome of a borehole, scanline or geophysical traverse. Record what measurement would falsify either interpretation. The useful result is a testable prediction—not a forced choice between unverified assumptions.

Field-report exercise. Draft a one-page section called “Evidence and limits for Hydraulic head and effective stress.” Include the measurement conditions, the most consequential model uncertainty, the observation that would reduce it, and the implication for a civil-engineering decision. Explain why the quantities should not be interpreted as final design parameters without project-specific evidence and qualified review.

4.2 Darcy flow and fractured permeability

Core physical interpretation. Darcy flow in a representative porous medium is proportional to hydraulic conductivity, area and head gradient. Fracture apertures can produce highly anisotropic and scale-dependent behavior in rock.

Civil-engineering case. Two boreholes return very different packer-test conductivities because one intersects an open joint network.

Common error. A bulk equivalent conductivity hides localized preferential paths and is invalid for some turbulent fracture flows.

Recommended observational method. Distinguish matrix permeability, fracture transmissivity, test scale and hydraulic boundary conditions.

4.3 Recharge, storage and construction dewatering

Core physical interpretation. Groundwater storage and recharge control drawdown and rebound, but pumping near a site introduces three-dimensional transient flow and sometimes ground movement. Simple water-balance models are useful only as bounds.

Civil-engineering case. A basement dewatering scheme lowers hydraulic heads in adjacent compressible strata, risking settlement outside the excavation.

Common error. Do not use one-dimensional recharge arithmetic to estimate deep-well pumping capacity.

Recommended observational method. Construct monitoring baselines and a water budget before design of temporary works.

4.4 Dissolution, chemical attack and groundwater quality

Core physical interpretation. Carbonate dissolution can create karst cavities; sulfate-rich groundwater can affect susceptible concrete; acidic water may transport metals. Chemistry and geology must be interpreted together.

Civil-engineering case. A road embankment above limestone experiences sinkhole development following changes in drainage pathways.

Common error. Surface absence of collapse features does not exclude buried karst, and chemistry alone cannot map voids.

Recommended observational method. Combine mapping, historical records, targeted geophysics and confirmatory drilling before revising the model.

§4.4 Integration of the two interactive laboratories

Integration of the two interactive laboratories

This unit uses Lab 07 and Lab 08. Both are stand-alone offline models. Start with the default case, predict the direction of change, perform an input sweep, and export the result. Critically compare the model to field observations: which assumptions are physically justified and which idealizations are merely convenient?

A laboratory is not complete until the student has submitted the predicted trend, the observed numerical response with units, a physical explanation, an estimate of sensitivity, and a paragraph on why the result cannot be applied directly to a real project.

§4.5 Synthesis case: competing geological explanations

Synthesis case: competing geological explanations

A team is examining a bridge approach affected by buried channel lenses. The investigation was conducted while seasonal river stage was relatively quiet, creating a risk that the observed state is not representative of the critical construction condition. The team has a preliminary map, two discrete observations and a short monitoring record. Neither spatial continuity nor transient behavior is fully constrained.

Construct three competing cross-sections: one with laterally continuous units, one with an abrupt structural boundary, and one with a buried erosional or weathering feature. For each cross-section, list the predicted drill response, the predicted groundwater response, and the construction problem that would arise if it were true. Rank only the value of information, not a numerical probability unsupported by data. Identify the most efficient next test and the observations that would cause you to revise the construction method.

Finally write a limitations paragraph for a geotechnical or engineering-geology report. This paragraph must say what was measured, what was inferred, the dates and groundwater conditions, and which assumptions need checking during construction. Separate a local observation from a regional geological generalization and make all units consistent.
