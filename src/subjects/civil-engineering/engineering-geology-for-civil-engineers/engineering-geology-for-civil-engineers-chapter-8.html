§8.1 Theoretical Foundations: Seismic Geology, Active Faulting, Ground Shaking and Site Decisions

Engineering geological risk is the intersection of geological process, exposure, consequence and uncertainty. A good final report separates what is known, what might happen, what evidence would change the assessment and which decisions require licensed specialists.

§8.2 Outcomes and a practical engineering question

Outcomes and a practical engineering question

By the end of this unit, a student should be able to translate the geologic observations in seismic geology, hazards and engineering decisions into a documented alternative ground model, quantify at least one relevant sensitivity, and explain what additional evidence would change the decision. The goal is not memorization of terms but a defendable sequence: observation → interpretation → process model → engineering implication → uncertainty.

A usable field record contains the datum, location, measurement method, orientation or depth, date, observer, evidence strength and confidence. A drawing without those attributes is difficult to audit when field conditions change.

§8.3 Four connected lessons

Four connected lessons

8.1 Seismic sources, site response and liquefaction

Core physical interpretation. Earthquakes impose cyclic demands on soils and rock masses; liquefaction screening requires stratigraphy, effective stress, groundwater and site-specific resistance information. Simplified cyclic stress ratio is only a demand calculation.

Civil-engineering case. A riverside bridge foundation crosses saturated young sand with variable fines and a nearby buried channel.

Common error. Never infer liquefaction safety from CSR alone; resistance evaluation is a separate calibrated procedure.

Recommended observational method. Document the ground-motion scenario, stress reduction assumptions and field tests needed for full evaluation.

From field observation to a working ground model. Begin by recording what could actually be seen or measured when investigating seismic sources, site response and liquefaction. In a rock tunnel, the team may encounter fault-related alteration while excavation sequence changes boundary conditions. Divide the record into physical evidence, a preferred interpretation, and at least one plausible competing explanation. For example, a curved reflectivity anomaly, a change in drilling return or a wet joint might be consistent with multiple geological situations. A good model therefore predicts several independent observables rather than treating one indirect signal as a unique material label.

Scale and engineering decision. The scale of the question for seismic sources, site response and liquefaction must match the scale of the decision. A hand specimen can establish mineral type or intact strength but cannot prove the continuity of a weak layer over a bridge footprint. Conversely, a regional map can reveal structural trends but rarely resolves a narrow fracture zone at a tunnel face. Define whether the decision concerns a meter-scale foundation, a tens-of-meters roadcut, or a kilometre-long alignment, then select mapping, boring, laboratory testing and monitoring accordingly. State the range of plausible boundary positions and explain how each affects stability, excavation, drainage and the construction sequence.

Measurement protocol. For seismic sources, site response and liquefaction, first state the expected range and diagnostic features, then calibrate instruments and confirm the reference datum. Photograph the observation with a scale and north indication, record adverse as well as favorable evidence, and label samples so the record can be reconstructed. Preserve original orientation data before transformation. Where the first interpretation is uncertain, obtain an independent check rather than selecting a value just because it fits a familiar textbook category. Compare day-to-day conditions when moisture or stress changes could affect what is measured.

Quantitative reasoning. Choose a calculation proportional to the physical process instead of using a formula because it is available. Write the dimensions, units, reference directions and boundary assumptions before substituting numbers. Work with ranges for the parameters least constrained by field evidence. A sensitivity plot can reveal which parameters matter most, but its smoothness is not evidence that the geological variation is smooth. Every plotted result should be traced to the original observation, the simplifying constitutive law, and a check against an independent physical limit.

Interpretation and risk. In rock tunnel settings, a mistaken assessment of fault-related alteration may be more consequential under excavation sequence. Define a failure of the working hypothesis: for example, repeated water-level readings contradict the assumed flow direction, measured joint planes diverge from the mapped structural domain, or a test pit intersects a contact outside its expected depth interval. Explain the difference between an uncertainty that merely widens a construction cost range and one that introduces a new safety-critical failure mechanism. Assign new evidence collection to the latter first.

Alternative hypothesis exercise. Hypothesis A assumes seismic sources, site response and liquefaction follows the dominant regional trend; hypothesis B assumes a local feature interrupts that trend. Draw a vertical section for both, label what is observed versus extrapolated, and compare the expected outcome of a borehole, scanline or geophysical traverse. Record what measurement would falsify either interpretation. The useful result is a testable prediction—not a forced choice between unverified assumptions.

Field-report exercise. Draft a one-page section called “Evidence and limits for Seismic sources, site response and liquefaction.” Include the measurement conditions, the most consequential model uncertainty, the observation that would reduce it, and the implication for a civil-engineering decision. Explain why the quantities should not be interpreted as final design parameters without project-specific evidence and qualified review.

8.2 Erosion, sediment and climate interactions

Core physical interpretation. Erosion depends on climate forcing, soil, topography, cover and land management. RUSLE is an average annual soil-loss screening relation and is not an event-scale debris-flow or rockfall prediction model.

Civil-engineering case. Road building removes vegetation from a steep catchment and accelerates sediment deposition at culvert inlets.

Common error. A modeled annual soil-loss number does not predict the location of a specific landslide.

Recommended observational method. Compare alternative control measures and validate with field evidence and watershed records.

8.3 Uncertainty, risk register and investigation value

Core physical interpretation. Geologic models contain epistemic uncertainty from incomplete observation and natural spatial variability. Decision-making improves when uncertainty is tied to specific construction consequences and testable alternatives.

Civil-engineering case. A retaining wall site may contain either competent rock or a buried filled channel; the cost of one additional boring can be compared with redesign risk.

Common error. Do not report a precise numerical probability without adequate calibration or a defensible prior.

Recommended observational method. State competing models, likelihood evidence, consequences, monitoring triggers and the next discriminating measurement.

8.4 Communicating a defensible engineering-geology report

Core physical interpretation. An effective report gives a reproducible site description, quality-controlled logs, model drawings, groundwater conditions, uncertainties, limitations and engineering implications. Distinguish public hazard data from local design evidence.

Civil-engineering case. A procurement team needs to compare two alignments and must know which hazards are confirmed, inferred or only plausible.

Common error. A visually attractive cross-section can mislead if its boundary confidence and vertical exaggeration are concealed.

Recommended observational method. Use an evidence table, uncertainty register, revision history and independent technical review before decisions.

§8.4 Integration of the two interactive laboratories

Integration of the two interactive laboratories

This unit uses Lab 15 and Lab 16. Both are stand-alone offline models. Start with the default case, predict the direction of change, perform an input sweep, and export the result. Critically compare the model to field observations: which assumptions are physically justified and which idealizations are merely convenient?

A laboratory is not complete until the student has submitted the predicted trend, the observed numerical response with units, a physical explanation, an estimate of sensitivity, and a paragraph on why the result cannot be applied directly to a real project.

§8.5 Synthesis case: competing geological explanations

Synthesis case: competing geological explanations

A team is examining a rail alignment affected by rapidly alternating lithology. The investigation was conducted while drainage obstruction was relatively quiet, creating a risk that the observed state is not representative of the critical construction condition. The team has a preliminary map, two discrete observations and a short monitoring record. Neither spatial continuity nor transient behavior is fully constrained.

Construct three competing cross-sections: one with laterally continuous units, one with an abrupt structural boundary, and one with a buried erosional or weathering feature. For each cross-section, list the predicted drill response, the predicted groundwater response, and the construction problem that would arise if it were true. Rank only the value of information, not a numerical probability unsupported by data. Identify the most efficient next test and the observations that would cause you to revise the construction method.

Finally write a limitations paragraph for a geotechnical or engineering-geology report. This paragraph must say what was measured, what was inferred, the dates and groundwater conditions, and which assumptions need checking during construction. Separate a local observation from a regional geological generalization and make all units consistent.
