Plasticity, Uncertainty and Structural Model Verification

Comprehensive university textbook unit covering fundamental theory, mathematical derivations, numerical modeling, and design validation of Plasticity, Uncertainty and Structural Model Verification. Developed for undergraduate civil engineering study.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Structural Analysis II study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§8.1 Conceptual overview

Conceptual overview

A linear elastic model answers a narrower question than a performance or collapse assessment. Plastic hinges can redistribute bending after local yielding, but the mechanism is highly dependent on cross-section class, lateral restraint and ductility. Stiffness degradation and connection slippage also change predicted deformation. Quantifying uncertainty adds a second layer: rather than treating a single E or load as perfectly known, one can explore response sensitivity to plausible parameter variation. Yet Monte Carlo samples drawn from invented distributions do not constitute a measured reliability model. A good computational report therefore includes assumptions, governing equations, units, verification benchmarks, reproducible inputs, mesh checks and a clear limitation statement.

§8.2 Learning outcomes

Learning outcomes

After working through this unit you should be able to: (1) construct a physically consistent idealization, (2) express the relevant force, stiffness and displacement variables using compatible units, (3) solve a demonstrative problem by hand or a transparent numerical method, (4) interpret response patterns and their sensitivity to input parameters, and (5) identify what the simplified model cannot prove. In this unit the most important observation is not merely a numerical answer but the connection between structural behavior and the assumed support or constitutive conditions.

§8.3 Unit engineering case — Model-audit exercise

Unit engineering case — Model-audit exercise

A contractor submits a continuous-beam analysis with a smooth deflected-shape plot and a single stated maximum moment. Demand the stiffness matrix, support assumptions, input load cases and a reaction summary. Compare an interior deflection against the exact uniform-load beam formula, and run mesh refinement at a fixed nonnodal coordinate. Add a deterministic parameter-uncertainty plot while warning that arbitrary probability distributions cannot establish failure risk. Finally document which results have passed verification and which assumptions remain to be validated physically.

Investigation sequence

Begin by preparing a diagram that names every member, support, load, imposed movement and unknown. Choose an independent coordinate system and assign a sign to every generalized force. Derive a minimal reference calculation before using a computational model. Then run a sensitivity study in which only one physical assumption changes at a time. Record both the predicted movement and the reaction or internal-force counterpart. Check conservation of force and moment where the physical model supports those checks. Finally compare the calculation with a plausible measurement or with a more complete independent model. Label the evidence as a verification benchmark, an assumption or a genuinely observed fact.

Suggested written report

A useful report has a one-paragraph engineering question, a table of parameter values with units, an explicit statement of boundary conditions, the governing equations, a readable figure of the system, a table of numerical results, at least one independent verification, and a conclusion that identifies unverified assumptions. State what happens when the governing input doubles, when stiffness increases, and when a support is relaxed or stiffened. Explain whether the observed changes follow from algebraic scaling or from a change in load path. Keep the original units in the file even if a graph displays a converted unit.

§8.4 Plastic hinge and mechanism concepts

8.1 Plastic hinge and mechanism concepts

Learning target and physical interpretation

A fully plastic section may carry a plastic moment capacity Mp if sufficient ductility and stability are available. A collapse mechanism forms when enough hinges develop to turn the ideal structure into a kinematic mechanism.

Governing relation

For an ideal fixed-fixed beam with central point load, a symmetric three-hinge mechanism gives Pcollapse=8Mp/L by virtual work.

Worked engineering interpretation

For Mp=200 kN·m and L=8 m, the ideal fixed-fixed central-load mechanism reaches 200 kN, whereas a simple-span central hinge mechanism reaches 100 kN.

What the idealization omits

The rigid-perfectly-plastic result does not contain a load–deflection curve: a plotted straight line to collapse is only illustrative.

Verification and interpretation of results

Write the virtual displacement and hinge rotation pattern, then confirm external work equals internal plastic dissipation.

§8.5 Sensitivity and parameter uncertainty

8.2 Sensitivity and parameter uncertainty

Learning target and physical interpretation

Calculated responses are conditional on selected loads, support stiffness and material properties. Parameter sensitivity identifies which inputs most affect deformation and which are relatively benign.

Governing relation

For a simple-span beam with central force, δ=PL³/(48EI); logarithmic sensitivities are +1 to P, +3 to L, −1 to E and −1 to I.

Worked engineering interpretation

A 1% span increase changes the leading-order elastic deflection by approximately 3% when all other variables are held fixed.

What the idealization omits

Sampling an elastic formula with arbitrary normal distributions does not provide a calibrated failure probability, confidence interval or design reliability index.

§8.6 Beam element interpolation and convergence

8.3 Beam element interpolation and convergence

Learning target and physical interpretation

Finite-element convergence is a property of a sequence of models and quantities of interest. Beam elements with cubic displacement interpolation may reproduce particular nodal values accurately but still miss interior curvature or displacement.

Governing relation

Hermite beam field v(x)=N1v_i+N2θ_i+N3v_j+N4θ_j; exact uniform-load deflection for simple span is v(x)=−w x(L³−2Lx²+x³)/(24EI).

Worked engineering interpretation

At x=0.37L, an interior coordinate that does not always coincide with a node, varying the mesh reveals interpolation error that would be hidden by examining only a nodal midspan value.

What the idealization omits

A converged displacement is not proof of converged extreme shear or stress, which may exhibit stronger sensitivity to loading representation or discontinuities.

Verification and interpretation of results

§8.7 Verification versus validation

8.4 Verification versus validation

Learning target and physical interpretation

Verification asks whether the equations are solved correctly. Validation asks whether the selected equations and assumptions describe the physical system sufficiently for the intended decision. Both are needed.

Governing relation

A useful error metric is ε_rel=|Q_num−Q_ref|/max(|Q_ref|,Q_floor), with a declared floor to avoid division by near-zero reference values.

Worked engineering interpretation

A numerical model can pass a reaction-balance check perfectly while still be invalid for a deep beam if shear deformation is neglected.

What the idealization omits

Reporting a visually smooth graph as evidence of physical correctness confuses graphical polish with conservation laws and governing mechanics.

Extension 1 — Flexibility matrix energy and reciprocity

For multiple redundants, a force-method model is most transparent when generalized coordinates are defined before releasing a support. Consider two unknown reactions X1 and X2, paired with two displacements Δ1 and Δ2. Apply one unit reaction at coordinate 1 to the released primary structure and measure movement at both coordinates. That gives the first column of the flexibility matrix. Apply a unit reaction at coordinate 2 to obtain the second column. For conservative, linear elastic behavior with well-matched force and displacement coordinates, Maxwell–Betti reciprocity predicts f12=f21. The matrix is often symmetric positive definite for a stable set of independent coordinates, because the internal strain energy under nonzero generalized loading must be positive. If it is nearly singular, one redundant may not be independent or the primary structure may be nearly a mechanism.

Physical compatibility requires the displacement induced by real loads plus the displacement due to each redundant to equal the actual constrained movement. This rule distinguishes settlement from an external applied load. A spring support imposes neither zero displacement nor an arbitrary known force; it contributes a reaction proportional to its own movement. A very stiff spring approaches rigid-restraint behavior only in a limiting sense. If the spring has a gap or a one-sided bearing, the force law is piecewise and a conventional symmetric linear formulation may no longer apply over all loading cases.

An independent energy check can use U=(1/2)XᵀfX. Under consistent units, this energy must be nonnegative for an elastic stable primary structure. If one redundant is a moment and another is a force, the matrix entries carry different physical units, and changing coordinate scales may improve numerical conditioning. However, rescaling cannot repair a mistaken structural release. Another useful observation is that the response to twice a load is twice the response only while material and support conditions remain linear. No amount of matrix algebra can justify extending a constant-stiffness prediction through contact loss or yielding.

In the parallel-spring laboratory, compatibility is simply equality of movement. For the propped cantilever, compatibility is zero final tip movement. These appear to be different calculations because one uses a spring law and the other a beam-flexibility expression, but both are instances of the same force–displacement relationship. An engineering report should draw that conceptual link and state which physical displacement is actually being restrained. A clean numerical reaction is not enough unless the assumed bearing movement is plausible.

Extension 2 — Deriving beam stiffness from energy

A two-node beam element can be developed from a displacement interpolation rather than memorizing the 4×4 matrix. Let the local coordinate ξ=x/L run from zero at the left node to one at the right node. The transverse displacement is represented by the cubic Hermite field v=N1v1+N2θ1+N3v2+N4θ2, where N1=1−3ξ²+2ξ³, N2=L(ξ−2ξ²+ξ³), N3=3ξ²−2ξ³ and N4=L(−ξ²+ξ³). These functions ensure that the prescribed nodal translations and slopes are reproduced exactly. Differentiating the field twice gives curvature, and the Euler–Bernoulli bending energy is U=(1/2)∫EI(vʺ)²dx. Differentiation of U with respect to nodal displacements produces the beam stiffness matrix.

The origin of the terms 12EI/L³, 6EI/L², 4EI/L and 2EI/L is consequently mechanical rather than arbitrary. Translational stiffness scales as L⁻³, coupling translation to rotation scales as L⁻², and purely rotational stiffness scales as L⁻¹. This explains why a long beam may be considerably more flexible in transverse displacement even when its local rotational properties change more modestly. A uniform distributed load must be integrated against the same shape functions to form consistent nodal actions. Using only half the total load at each end reproduces total vertical force but misses the generalized moments and can therefore change the solution.

The exact deflection of a simply supported beam under a uniform load is quartic in x. A single cubic element cannot reproduce that exact interior field for arbitrary x, even if some nodal quantities are recovered accurately with a consistent load vector. The distinction between nodal accuracy and interior-field accuracy explains why refinement matters. In a continuous beam, adjacent elements share the interior rotation; the common joint rotation enforces displacement continuity and no relative kink at an ideal rigid connection. If the joint is a real hinge, the rotational continuity constraint must be released at that member end, with care to preserve translation compatibility.

Slope–deflection and moment distribution emerge from the same stiffness relations after vertical displacements are fixed and only rotations remain free. A hand analysis might be faster for one interior joint. A matrix solver is more reliable for many supports, settlements or changing loads, provided DOF indexing, end releases and consistent nodal loads are checked independently. When comparing the two methods, specify which member end moment is positive and draw both clockwise action directions. A result cannot be called wrong solely because one convention reverses the displayed end-moment sign.

Extension 3 — Frame transformation and rigid-body consistency

A frame element is evaluated most naturally in local coordinates aligned with its own axis. Axial extension appears in the local longitudinal displacement components, while transverse bending and end rotation appear in the remaining degrees of freedom. For an element at angle α to the global x-axis, the direction cosines c=cosα and s=sinα map global translations into local axial and transverse components. An appropriate block-diagonal transformation maps the two ends consistently and leaves the scalar in-plane rotation unchanged. The transformed global element stiffness is Tᵀk_local T, not Tk_local Tᵀ when T is defined as global-to-local.

This distinction is easy to test. A horizontal member subjected to a global horizontal relative movement should respond in axial stiffness EA/L. Rotating the same member by ninety degrees makes a global vertical relative movement axial. A global rigid translation of both ends must cause zero internal strain and force. For a frame, a small rigid-body rotation should likewise produce zero internal deformation in a consistent linearized kinematic representation when all nodal translations and rotations follow that rigid-body motion. If the element reports a large internal force under such a test, the transformation or DOF order may be incorrect.

The assembled structure must also be stable after applying supports. A planar frame node has three degrees of freedom; a base fixity removes two translations and its rotation. A pin support removes its translations but not its rotation. A moment release belongs to a member end and is not always equivalent to releasing a whole joint coordinate shared by other members. For example, three beams meeting at one physical pin may still share translations while allowing appropriate individual end rotations. Misrepresenting the releases can introduce spurious stiffness or a mechanism. The resulting matrix singularity is a diagnostic clue, not something to hide by adding enormous stiffness to a random diagonal element.

In the portal-frame laboratory, both fixed bases carry reactions that balance the lateral roof load. Because the roof beam joins both column tops rigidly, it also carries moments. Equal and opposite joint actions should reconcile when every local member force is transformed back to the global system. An analyst who checks only horizontal force equilibrium may miss a wrong sign in a member moment. It is better to test global reaction sums, moment equilibrium, strain-energy positivity, rigid-body modes and one simplified hand case. When all these hold, the solver has stronger evidence of correct implementation, though real boundary fixity and member properties still need validation.

Extension 4 — From settlement to contact and construction staging

A prescribed support settlement creates a special kind of loading: the restraint moves, while the structure is forced to follow at the contact. The associated reaction is not known in advance. After the global stiffness matrix is partitioned into free and constrained coordinates, the imposed displacement vector contributes −K_fc u_c to the free-coordinate right-hand side. This is why simply assigning a vertical point load in place of settlement is a different problem. For a continuous beam, a lowered interior support may induce both positive and negative end moments that change the bending pattern even if applied external loading remains unchanged.

The linear calculation assumes that all prescribed support constraints stay active. A roller or bearing capable of compression only cannot transmit arbitrary tension. If the solver predicts tensile contact pressure at a bearing, the active set of supports may change. Removing that bearing changes both the stiffness matrix and the deformation compatibility conditions. A separate contact analysis would alternate between calculating force under a selected set of active restraints and checking that all reactions obey contact inequalities. A smooth graph from a fixed-support model conceals such a change if the program has no contact logic.

Temperature introduces another compatibility problem. For a segment that expands freely by αΔTL, restraining its actual extension produces an axial force. For two serial segments with fixed end-to-end distance, the same force passes through both members and their free extensions add. Their mechanical extensions, force times axial flexibility L/(EA), sum with the free thermal extensions to meet the prescribed overall displacement. A bar that is warmed uniformly but remains completely free to expand has no axial force in this idealization. This difference is important when explaining why expansion joints and bearings can reduce thermal restraint actions.

Construction sequencing complicates both examples. A beam may be supported at one elevation during casting and at another after removal of temporary props. Some dead load may be carried before continuity is established, whereas later live loads act on the final continuous structure. Likewise, concrete shrinkage and creep depend on age and restraint history. A final geometry alone cannot capture these histories. A staged analysis must document the time each element and support becomes active, the loads applied at that stage, and the material properties appropriate to it. For every calculated moment redistribution, ask whether it comes from imposed movement, a new load path, changed stiffness or altered contact. Without that distinction, the numerical values have little diagnostic meaning.

Extension 5 — Structural stability beyond a single Euler member

The elegant Euler formula describes an ideal linear bifurcation of a perfectly straight prismatic member. In a real frame, load-induced internal forces can alter the global tangent stiffness before any individual member reaches its isolated pinned-pinned critical load. Geometric stiffness depends on axial force distribution and deformation mode. An ideal linearized stability analysis searches for a load multiplier at which det(K_elastic−λK_geometric)=0, with sign convention chosen so compression reduces restoring stiffness. The eigenvector describes the mode that loses incremental stiffness. This is a frame property, not merely a collection of independent column formulas.

A sway frame can exhibit global instability because the beam-column joints move laterally together. Bracing may dramatically increase the lowest critical multiplier by providing a stiff lateral load path, even when it barely changes individual member EI. Conversely, a nominally fixed connection that is flexible in practice can reduce rotational restraint and effective member buckling capacity. Effective-length factors approximate these end restraints under particular ideal conditions; they should not be guessed from a diagram. The inverse-square dependence on effective length means that even modest uncertainty in bracing length can have substantial consequences.

Second-order analysis ordinarily distinguishes local member curvature (P–δ) from larger frame story translation (P–Δ). Both can matter. In an iterative equilibrium solution, the force acting through displaced geometry alters the moment, which changes deformation, which further changes moment. A first-order correction M+PΔ computed once may underestimate the fully converged effect when the applied axial load is near a stability limit. It is useful to carry out increasing-load steps and monitor not only drift but also tangent stiffness and convergence. Loss of numerical convergence is not, by itself, proof of physical collapse, because solver tolerances and constitutive modeling affect it.

The educational buckling lab presents Euler Pcr and a simple amplifier 1/(1−P/Pcr). This is intentionally a sensitivity illustration, not an implementation of geometric stiffness analysis or a certified beam-column strength method. Initial crookedness, residual stress, inelasticity, connection slip and member imperfections all influence real behavior. To turn an ideal stability benchmark into a design assessment requires selecting appropriate analysis procedures and resistance provisions from the governing standards, with independent checking by a qualified engineer.

Extension 6 — Moving loads: response influence and envelopes

A bending-moment diagram shows moment along the structure for one specified loading arrangement. An influence line instead chooses one response quantity—such as left bearing reaction or bending moment at a fixed section—and varies the position of a unit load. This difference matters when examining vehicles crossing a bridge. For a simply supported span of length L, the right reaction under a unit load at coordinate x is x/L. The left reaction is (L−x)/L. A moment influence line for section a is triangular with maximum ordinate a(L−a)/L when the unit load lies at the section. Multiplying an ordinate by a real axle force gives that axle's contribution to the chosen response.

An axle group can be treated as a sum of moving point loads as long as the assumed linear superposition is valid. When the first axle is on the span but the second has not yet entered, the sum must include only the first. The same care is needed when the vehicle exits. For each vehicle position, moment equilibrium determines support reactions; the section moment is then assembled from the loads located to one side of the section. Because the relative axle positions are fixed by the vehicle geometry, extreme moment is not necessarily produced when the heaviest axle alone sits at the section. Searching the vehicle position over the allowed range is a straightforward numerical envelope calculation.

For an indeterminate continuous beam, support moments and reaction influence lines are no longer the simple triangular forms associated with determinate spans. A point force acting inside a beam finite element should be distributed to generalized nodal load coordinates through the beam shape functions, not rounded to the nearest node. Nearest-node allocation creates nonphysical jumps as the axle crosses the mesh. Even with correct consistent loads, the chosen mesh must be refined until the specific response envelope is sufficiently stable for the purpose of the calculation.

Real highway bridge design adds complexities absent from the classroom train: truck and tandem arrangements defined by a bridge code, multiple loaded lanes, load factors, dynamic amplification, wearing surface, lateral deck distribution, fatigue spectra, influence surfaces for two-dimensional systems and potentially nonlinear bearings. The lab's value is that it makes response superposition and equilibrium visible. A proper output report should identify the axle placement yielding the extreme, the fixed section being evaluated, whether each axle is on or off the bridge, and whether the output is a reaction, bending moment or shear. These quantities should not be mixed.

Extension 7 — Modes, frequency response and numerical integration

A two-degree-of-freedom shear building provides a small matrix eigenproblem with interpretable mode shapes. Its mass matrix represents floor inertia; its stiffness matrix reflects resistance to relative story drifts. Solving det(K−ω²M)=0 yields two circular frequencies. The corresponding vectors express relative floor movement, not absolute displacement amplitude. Any nonzero multiple of an eigenvector is the same mode. A customary normalization is to set the roof component to one, although mass normalization is often more useful in modal superposition. For linear undamped systems with suitable mass and stiffness properties, distinct modes are orthogonal under the mass matrix: φ_iᵀMφ_j=0 for i≠j.

The lower frequency commonly represents an in-phase sway-like motion, whereas the upper mode exhibits relative reversal between floors. How much each mode contributes under a particular excitation depends on the excitation direction and modal participation. Higher modes can make a small contribution to roof displacement while still influencing acceleration or story shear. Therefore, a statement that the first mode dominates every response is too broad. A full structural vibration model also needs rotational inertia, torsion and possibly three-dimensional interactions.

For one generalized coordinate, the governing damped motion is m ü+c u̇+ku=p(t). The circular natural frequency is √(k/m), and viscous damping ratio is c/(2√(km)). A harmonic input may be amplified near resonance; in steady state the dimensionless displacement amplification is 1/√((1−r²)²+(2ζr)²), where r is forcing frequency divided by natural frequency. At resonance r=1, this ratio becomes 1/(2ζ) for ζ>0. That result characterizes a linear steady-state solution, while a numerical simulation initiated from rest also contains transient motion.

Newmark average acceleration calculates the next displacement, velocity and acceleration from the previous step while enforcing dynamic equilibrium. It is unconditionally stable for the classical linear system under standard assumptions but not infinitely accurate. If the time increment is too large, the oscillation phase and amplitude may be distorted. The correct numerical experiment is to refine the time step and compare the result for a defined output such as peak displacement, phase delay or energy balance. Browser animation alone is not evidence of convergence. An actual floor-vibration study would additionally require a credible forcing spectrum, damping estimate, comfort criteria and mode identification from physical or calibrated model data.

Extension 8 — Engineering model auditing and epistemic limits

Verification is the process of checking that the mathematical model has been implemented and solved consistently. A stiffness matrix should have the expected symmetry and rigid-body modes before supports are applied. Its restrained solution should reproduce force and moment equilibrium. A small exactly solvable beam or truss can serve as a benchmark. Mesh refinement tests whether the reported quantity approaches a stable value. Browser interaction tests identify broken sliders and graphs but cannot independently establish the mechanical correctness of the solver.

Validation is a different question. A model of a deep shear-sensitive beam might solve Euler–Bernoulli equations perfectly and yet be inappropriate for the physical beam. An ideal fixed column base can strongly underpredict sway if the actual foundation rotates. A selected Young modulus might not represent a cracked reinforced-concrete section, and a flexible diaphragm may not behave as a rigid plane. These are physical-model questions that need measurement, test evidence, design-standard guidance and independent expert judgment, not just smaller numerical residuals.

Uncertainty studies should declare which quantities are random and why their distributions are credible. Varying a nominal load and modulus with arbitrary Gaussian coefficients of variation demonstrates mathematical sensitivity but does not establish a probability of structural failure. Real reliability analysis needs evidence-based tails of distributions, correlations, resistance and demand models, limit-state definitions and decision thresholds. A reproducible random seed makes an educational simulation easier to audit; it does not make its prior assumptions scientifically measured.

An engineering submission needs traceable units, versions of input geometry and software, analysis and load cases, support conditions, material properties, solver tolerances, verification examples, independent peer review, applicable standards and an explicit acceptance criterion. Where a result is unusually sensitive to small changes in material properties or restraints, that sensitivity should be highlighted rather than averaged away. If an educational model is taken outside its tested assumptions, it should be withdrawn from decision-making until the new case is validated. The purpose of these laboratories is to teach structural reasoning and the discipline of checking a model—not to create a shortcut around qualified structural design.

Lab 01 — interpretation and independent reasoning

The common displacement of two parallel springs can be derived without solving a matrix. Every branch connects the same node to an immovable support, so both branches experience the same change in length. Multiply that change by each spring stiffness and sum the member reactions. In the default example, a 100 kN force acting against 40 kN/mm aggregate stiffness produces 2.5 mm displacement. This is enough information to recover both branch forces independently. If the first spring is softened while the second stays unchanged, the total stiffness falls, increasing the common displacement, but the softer branch also attracts a smaller fraction of the applied force. These two effects must be distinguished. The branch-force sum remains 100 kN because no other load path is modeled. For a real elastomeric bearing, stiffness depends on geometry, confinement, aging and sometimes loading rate. The ideal spring model intentionally omits those complexities.

Lab 02 — interpretation and independent reasoning

The propped cantilever exposes the power of a carefully selected redundant. Remove the roller at the right tip to obtain a stable cantilever. A uniformly distributed downward load produces downward free-tip movement wL^4/(8EI). A hypothetical upward tip reaction produces opposite movement RL^3/(3EI). Compatibility requires their sum to vanish because the actual prop prevents vertical tip motion. The result is R=3wL/8, leaving 5wL/8 at the fixed support. The fixed support moment is not zero; it must close moment equilibrium for the full load and prop. At w=12 kN/m and L=6 m, R=27 kN and the fixed end takes 45 kN of vertical force. In a nonuniform girder, piecewise EI changes the flexibility integrals, so substituting a single average rigidity can be a poor shortcut. In a lift-off situation a roller cannot pull downward, so a negative calculated contact reaction would require a new unilateral contact model.

Lab 03 — interpretation and independent reasoning

A continuous beam is statically indeterminate because a middle vertical support adds a redundant reaction. With three simple supports, the rotations remain possible but are shared across member ends because the beam itself is continuous. The two Euler–Bernoulli beam elements contribute their stiffness to one common interior rotation. Computing each span as an independent simply supported beam destroys continuity and erases the hogging moment over the middle support. In the default 5 m and 7 m example, asymmetric loading produces an interior rotation and three support reactions that must sum to 106 kN of gravity load. The plot depicts only cubic displacement interpolation across each element; a uniform load produces a quartic exact deflected shape between nodes. Thus nodal rotations and reactions can be useful while the drawn displacement curve may still need mesh refinement. Comparing those different accuracy levels is part of the lab, not a reason to hide the limitation.

Lab 04 — interpretation and independent reasoning

Moment distribution can be understood as solving the same joint equilibrium as a small stiffness matrix. Consider a middle joint B joining two members whose outer ends A and C are rotationally fixed. Each connected member contributes rotational stiffness 4EI/L at B. Fixed-end loading moments initially leave a nonzero sum at B. A balancing rotation generates additional end moments proportional to the individual stiffnesses, and the net correction must cancel the unbalanced moment. The well-known distribution factors are simply each stiffness divided by their sum. When both spans have the same EI and length, the correction splits evenly; different span lengths shift the split even before changing distributed loads. Far-end moments also change because rotation at one end affects the other end through bending continuity. A physical drawing should label near and far ends and moments using one explicit convention. Do not interpret equal and opposite joint actions as separate externally applied moments.

Lab 05 — interpretation and independent reasoning

A three-member pin-jointed truss provides a transparent matrix example with only two unknown displacements. The top node can move horizontally and vertically; each bar resists the projection of that motion onto its own axis. A bar inclined by angle θ contributes horizontal stiffness proportional to cos²θ and vertical stiffness proportional to sin²θ, with coupling terms proportional to sinθ cosθ. The central vertical member mainly adds vertical resistance. In a symmetric arrangement, the horizontal coupling of left and right diagonal bars cancels, so the top displacement under purely vertical load has essentially zero horizontal component. The reported diagonal member axial forces need not equal half the applied vertical force because only their vertical components contribute to vertical equilibrium. The middle member also carries load. Summing raw axial force magnitudes, instead of their resolved components, would incorrectly imply a violation of equilibrium. The lab assumes pinned nodes, no flexural rigidity, and common linear elastic behavior.

The rigid-jointed portal frame extends truss analysis by adding bending rotation at each node. Its three members connect a left column, a roof beam and a right column, with their bases fixed. Lab 07 — interpretation and independent reasoning

Settlement converts what might look like an ordinary loading problem into one governed by known displacement. The middle beam support is assigned a downward movement while end supports remain vertically fixed. Even with the external distributed load set to zero, an elastic continuous beam can develop shears and bending moments because its supported profile is no longer straight. In stiffness terms, the vector of prescribed displacements is multiplied by the constrained-to-free stiffness block and transferred into the reduced equilibrium equation. Lab 13 — interpretation and independent reasoning

An ideal plastic collapse mechanism is a work balance, not a nonlinear finite-element load–deflection calculation. A fixed-fixed beam with a concentrated force at midspan can develop plastic hinges at the two fixed ends and at the center, provided ductile sections can sustain the required rotations without local or lateral buckling. If each hinge resists the fully plastic moment Mp, internal plastic work is the sum of moment times hinge rotation. For the symmetric mechanism this yields central-load collapse P=8Mp/L. Appendix E — Numerical conditioning and force recovery

Additional technical note — numerical conditioning and force recovery

A small linear residual does not always imply a small error in the displacement solution. When a stiffness matrix is ill-conditioned, a tiny perturbation to a support spring or a nearly redundant restraint can produce a comparatively large change in displacement. The matrix condition number summarizes how solution error may amplify under perturbation, but it depends on DOF scaling and the units assigned to different displacement coordinates. A rotation in radians and a translation in meters should not be compared as if they had the same numerical magnitude without physical interpretation. When diagnosing a nearly singular frame, inspect connectivity and released DOFs first, rather than adding arbitrary diagonal stiffness until the solver stops warning.

Member force recovery deserves a separate check from nodal displacement recovery. For a beam with distributed loading, the local element end forces require subtracting or adding consistent fixed-end load actions according to the chosen convention. Simply multiplying the local stiffness by the end-displacement vector may omit member actions induced directly by applied UDL. This can generate a bending-moment chart that is smooth but missing its proper fixed-end contribution. A sound diagnostic compares the sum of all member end shears with the equivalent applied load and verifies that the two end moments and shears satisfy member equilibrium about one end.

An engineering model should expose the solver tolerance, the residual norm, the physical units and at least one independent benchmark. The comparison ought to be made for a clearly identified output, such as middle support reaction, roof lateral drift or an interior point deflection, rather than for an unspecified statement that two graphs look similar. If a load is moved through a beam finite-element model, convergence is required for the full response envelope—not only for one arbitrarily chosen axle location. These checks make model results more trustworthy as educational calculations, though they still cannot substitute for validation of the physical structure.
