§7.1 Theoretical Foundations: Environmental Assessment, Monitoring and Soil/Groundwater Remediation

Running case. A brownfield near a floodplain is being redeveloped for housing and light industry.

Unit product. Create a conceptual site model, impacts register and phased investigation/remediation proposal.

§7.2 Unit orientation

Unit orientation

This unit asks what can and cannot be concluded from observations in the running case. Begin by listing state variables and the time basis, identifying which quantities have been measured and which are assumptions. The engineering task is to distinguish evidence of transport, transformation, dilution and removal, and to prevent an untested model from becoming a safety claim. Compare at least two approaches with the same functional requirements, and include one sensitivity or uncertainty test in the written submission.

§7.3 Impact assessment and alternatives

7.1 Impact assessment and alternatives

An environmental impact assessment identifies activities, receptors, impact pathways, mitigation and monitoring before decisions become irreversible. The no-project alternative can still carry environmental and social consequences; the task is comparison, not an automatic defence of construction. Screening and scoping ensure the study addresses significant effects rather than merely producing long lists.

Differentiate direct, indirect and cumulative impacts. A road project can affect habitat through runoff, noise, traffic growth and land-use change even if the construction footprint is small. Describe baseline data quality and uncertainty, involve affected groups, and distinguish reversible construction impacts from permanent changes.

Governing relationship and units

Residual risk = likelihood × consequence is only a screening construct; units and scales require explicit definitions.

Worked context

A proposed wastewater plant reduces river pollution but may create odour, truck traffic and energy demand. Compare alternative sites and process configurations while keeping equal discharge performance and receptor protection.

§7.4 Quantitative exposure and risk screening

7.2 Quantitative exposure and risk screening

Hazard identifies inherent ability to cause harm; risk concerns probability and consequence under a defined exposure. Conservative assumptions are appropriate for screening but should be reported rather than concealed. A hazard quotient exceeding one signals a need for more evaluation, not proof that illness will occur; below one is not a universal certificate of safety.

Specify exposure duration, body mass, intake, absorption, toxicity value, competing routes and sensitive populations. Include a realistic high-exposure scenario and a central scenario. Regulatory toxicity values and acceptable thresholds vary by pollutant and jurisdiction and must come from current authoritative sources.

Governing relationship and units

CDI=(C×IR×EF×ED)/(BW×AT).

Worked context

An illustrative drinking-water exposure concentration of 0.01 mg/L, intake 2 L/day and body mass 65 kg gives 3.08×10⁻⁴ mg/kg/day for daily exposure. The engineering decision requires compound-specific toxicology and actual sampling.

§7.5 Contaminated-site characterization and cleanup

7.3 Contaminated-site characterization and cleanup

Contaminated soil and groundwater are heterogeneous. Sampling near a source can miss preferential flow paths, and a seemingly clean well may simply be screened above the plume. Remediation options include removal, containment, pump-and-treat, soil vapour extraction, bioremediation and monitored natural attenuation, each appropriate only under particular hydrogeology and chemical behaviour.

Use a conceptual site model that connects source history, soil layers, groundwater gradient, measured chemical distribution and receptors. Establish data quality objectives and a staged investigation. Successful cleanup must be judged by validated endpoints and long-term monitoring, not merely the visible removal of contaminated surface soil.

Governing relationship and units

Advective travel time ≈ distance × effective porosity / Darcy flux, for a simplified saturated homogeneous path.

Worked context

A plume that travels 0.2 m/day could cross a 400 m distance in around 2,000 days absent retardation and dispersion. Sorption and biodegradation may slow particular constituents, but unknown preferential pathways can undermine this simple estimate.

§7.6 Compliance systems and environmental auditing

7.4 Compliance systems and environmental auditing

Compliance is jurisdiction-specific: a WHO recommendation, a project specification and a legally binding discharge permit are not interchangeable. Environmental management systems assign responsibilities, document controls, monitor outcomes and provide corrective action. A passed spot sample does not establish compliance with an annual average or a continuous-monitoring requirement.

Create a permit register listing each parameter, location, measurement method, averaging period, reporting date and response trigger. Verify calibration and laboratory accreditation; preserve version history and review changes in regulations. Investigate deviations with causal analysis rather than assuming that a temporary dose increase will cure a systemic operating problem.

Governing relationship and units

Compliance metric must match the rule: e.g., valid daily mean, annual load, or an event count.

Worked context

An effluent with five low daily readings and one severe overflow cannot be represented fairly by a simple average alone. Permit event reporting may apply independently of normal concentration limits.

Unit assessment

  2. Explain how different sampling times or locations could reverse the conclusion in this scenario: A brownfield near a floodplain is being redeveloped for housing and light industry.
  3. Choose two uncertainties and quantify which has the larger effect on the recommendation. Show a reproducible parameter table.
  4. Use one of the four governing relationships to calculate a low, central and high case, then explain why a numerical result is not a permit limit.
  5. Design an independent field or bench experiment that discriminates between a transport limitation and a reaction limitation.
  6. Write a corrective-action plan for equipment failure or a surprising monitoring result; identify response time and independent verification.
  7. Construct a single-page public explanation of the issue without concealing unresolved uncertainty.
  8. Critique a proposal that reports percent removal but omits residual streams, peak-flow capacity and monitoring quality.

Assessment policy: A numerical answer without units, documented assumptions, and interpretation is incomplete. Explain what additional observation or regulation would be needed to authorize real-world action.
