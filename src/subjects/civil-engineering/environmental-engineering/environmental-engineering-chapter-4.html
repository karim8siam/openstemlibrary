§4.3 Wastewater characterization and preliminary treatment

4.1 Wastewater characterization and preliminary treatment

Sanitary wastewater varies over a day and can receive wet-weather infiltration, industrial discharges or shock loads. Flow equalization can protect downstream biological processes, but it does not remove the total mass of pollutants. Screens and grit chambers protect pumps and downstream equipment; they are not substitutes for primary sedimentation or biological conversion.

Build a flow and load profile using hourly data, not only an average concentration. Evaluate BOD, COD, soluble and particulate fractions, total suspended solids, nitrogen forms, phosphorus, pH, alkalinity and potential toxicants. For industrial inputs, check inhibition and chemical compatibility before assuming that additional aeration will solve every performance problem.

Governing relationship and units

L_BOD (kg/day)=Q(m³/day)×S(mg/L)×0.001.

Worked context

At 12,000 m³/day and 220 mg/L BOD, the influent load is 2,640 kg/day. Doubling flow with rainwater while halving BOD concentration gives the same computed load, but different hydraulics may still overload clarifiers.

§4.4 Activated sludge and biological reaction control

4.2 Activated sludge and biological reaction control

Activated sludge couples microbial reactions in an aeration reactor with biomass separation in a final clarifier. Food-to-microorganism ratio, sludge retention time, dissolved oxygen, substrate concentration and return sludge flow are related but not interchangeable. Low soluble effluent BOD can coexist with high total BOD when clarifier solids escape.

The Monod equation relates specific growth to substrate under particular conditions; real plants contain mixed microbial populations and multiple growth limits. Monitor settleability, mixed-liquor suspended solids, wasting rates and effluent turbidity, and reconcile solids inventories. Oxygen delivery, temperature and toxicity may be decisive even when a simple growth curve predicts sufficient activity.

Governing relationship and units

μ=μ_max S/(K_s+S); F/M≈QS₀/(VX).

Worked context

A reactor with Q=5,000 m³/day, influent biodegradable substrate 180 mg/L, V=2,500 m³ and biomass 2,800 mg/L has F/M≈0.129 day⁻¹. Whether that is appropriate depends on process type, substrate basis and solids characterization.

§4.5 Nitrogen and phosphorus transformations

4.3 Nitrogen and phosphorus transformations

Nitrification oxidizes reduced nitrogen and consumes alkalinity and oxygen; denitrification reduces nitrate under anoxic conditions when a suitable electron donor is present. Neither process is captured by simply multiplying total nitrogen by a generic removal efficiency. Biological phosphorus removal depends on cycling anaerobic and aerobic conditions and on carbon availability.

Draw nitrogen species and reaction zones through the treatment train. Distinguish ammonia toxicity, nitrate discharge limits, and total-nitrogen mass balance. A low ammonia effluent may still have high nitrate. Chemical phosphorus precipitation shifts material into sludge; nutrient recovery requires a recovery pathway and quality assurance beyond simply precipitating a solid.

Governing relationship and units

Removed nutrient load (kg/day)=0.001Q(C_in−C_out).

Worked context

At 8,000 m³/day, reducing total phosphorus from 6 to 1 mg/L removes about 40 kg/day from the water stream. The phosphorus remains in biomass, precipitates or another residual stream until final handling.

§4.6 Biosolids, sludge handling and process resilience

4.4 Biosolids, sludge handling and process resilience

Wastewater treatment creates residual materials whose risk depends on contaminants, pathogens, stability and end-use conditions. Thickening reduces volume primarily by changing water content, whereas stabilization reduces readily degradable organics and odour potential. Anaerobic digestion may recover biogas, but energy benefit must be balanced against heating, gas cleaning, leaks and solids management.

For each residual stream, track dry-solids mass, moisture content, destination and legal status. Wet mass is a poor comparison of sludge production because water fraction varies. Consider equipment redundancy, power failure, storm bypass, return flows from dewatering, worker exposure and odour management when comparing treatment alternatives.

Governing relationship and units

Dry-solids mass = wet mass × solids fraction.

Worked context

A sludge stream of 100 t/day at 2% dry solids contains 2 t/day of solids. After thickening to 6% solids at unchanged dry-solids mass it occupies about 33.3 t/day, not one-third of the solids mass.
