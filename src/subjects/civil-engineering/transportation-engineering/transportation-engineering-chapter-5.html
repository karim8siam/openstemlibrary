§5.1 Learning objectives

Learning objectives

Learn to characterize the mechanisms, assumptions and observational evidence connecting flexible pavement composition and wheel loading, cumulative axle loading and pavement life, rigid pavements and joint behavior, maintenance, condition and lifecycle decisions. Demonstrate unit consistency, interpret safety and access alongside vehicle throughput, and compare model predictions against field observations before making recommendations.

§5.2 How to study this unit

§5.3 Flexible pavement composition and wheel loading

Lesson 5.1: Flexible pavement composition and wheel loading

Physical and planning meaning

Flexible pavements distribute wheel loads through layered bound and unbound materials. Rutting, fatigue cracking, moisture damage and thermal cracking have distinct mechanisms. A thicker asphalt layer is not automatically a complete remedy; drainage, subgrade condition and construction quality may control performance.

Governing relationship

For equal contact pressure p, contact area A = wheel load P/p.

Interpreted worked case

A 40 kN single tire load acting at nominal 700 kPa has estimated contact area 0.0571 m². Approximating the patch as circular gives radius about 0.135 m. A real tire has a noncircular contact-pressure distribution and dynamic tire forces.

Investigating an assumption

Compare a poorly drained thick asphalt pavement with a thinner pavement over a well-drained stable base. Which field evidence would distinguish likely distress causes?

Important limitations

The contact-pressure approximation is not a mechanistic–empirical pavement design procedure.

Alternative design comparison

Compare at least two interventions against the same baseline. Record whether each changes demand, available capacity, exposure, pavement condition, human behavior or monitoring quality. An alternative with the largest improvement in one performance indicator is not necessarily superior when its cost, emissions, access impacts and failure modes are considered.

§5.4 Cumulative axle loading and pavement life

Lesson 5.2: Cumulative axle loading and pavement life

Physical and planning meaning

Load equivalency factors convert mixed axle traffic into a reference damage scale. The fourth-power law is sometimes used as a rough teaching approximation but not as a universal calibrated law. Different pavement materials and failure modes produce different damage exponents and factors.

Governing relationship

Illustrative relative damage D = (P/P_ref)^n, commonly using n = 4 only for sensitivity demonstrations.

Interpreted worked case

If a reference load is 80 kN, doubling the simplified axle load to 160 kN increases fourth-power damage index by sixteen times. This must not be read as a reliable real-vehicle life prediction.

Investigating an assumption

Explore exponent n from 3 to 5: how different would fleet-composition conclusions be for heavy trucks?

Important limitations

Regulatory axle configuration, axle spacing, dual tires and locally calibrated design models are essential.

Sensitivity and uncertainty

Vary one uncertain parameter while keeping others fixed, then vary a second parameter jointly. A steep response indicates that the result should be reported as a range rather than a single number. Do not equate a sensitivity exercise with a statistical confidence interval; uncertainty also includes model structure and measurement bias.

§5.5 Rigid pavements and joint behavior

Lesson 5.3: Rigid pavements and joint behavior

Physical and planning meaning

Concrete slab pavements resist wheel loading partly through bending stiffness; temperature changes produce curling and warping that can interact with wheel stresses. Joints accommodate movements and must maintain load transfer. Faulting, pumping and cracking signal interactions among the slab, subbase, water and traffic.

Governing relationship

Slab bending stiffness per unit width D = Eh³/[12(1−ν²)] for idealized linear elastic plate behavior.

Interpreted worked case

Doubling slab thickness h increases idealized flexural rigidity eightfold while leaving material properties unchanged. The formula alone does not tell you whether slab stresses will satisfy any fatigue criterion.

Investigating an assumption

When would dowel bars improve performance? Distinguish load transfer at transverse joints from steel reinforcement used for crack distribution.

Important limitations

Plate models ignore many layered-system, corner loading and nonlinear contact effects.

Measurement protocol

Specify which observations distinguish the proposed engineering relationship from a coincidental correlation. Record station location, observation interval, vehicles or users included, calibration checks and missing data. A correct equation evaluated on a biased dataset can create a less defensible conclusion than a rough estimate accompanied by an explicit uncertainty interval.

§5.6 Maintenance, condition and lifecycle decisions

Lesson 5.4: Maintenance, condition and lifecycle decisions

Physical and planning meaning

Condition surveys describe cracking, rut depth, roughness, skid resistance and drainage. A condition index is a management tool, not a physical law. The timing of maintenance matters because small interventions on a sound road can be more effective than waiting until full reconstruction is unavoidable.

Governing relationship

Present value PV = Σ cost_t/(1+r)^t when costs occur at year t.

Interpreted worked case

A road agency compares an immediate treatment of 1 million cost units with a 1.3 million treatment due in five years. At 5% annual discount, the future cost has PV ≈ 1.019 million. This simple comparison ignores performance, residual value and user delays.

Investigating an assumption

Create a lifecycle decision that includes construction emission, user delay, periodic maintenance and climate risk instead of only agency cost.

Important limitations

Use real condition observations and consistent analysis horizons. A lower discounted agency cost need not mean a socially preferable outcome.

Dimensional audit

Before computing, rewrite every quantity in one coherent system of units. A time rate needs a time denominator; a spatial density needs a road-length denominator; a percentage must become a decimal before entering an algebraic equation. Unit checking can reject many plausible-looking but incorrect answers without any specialist software.

§5.9 Extended pavement case: a freight corridor with seasonal flooding

Extended pavement case: a freight corridor with seasonal flooding

A highway carrying heavy trucks develops wheel-path rutting and cracking one year after major resurfacing. A simple design memo initially attributes the problem to unexpectedly high axle loads and requests thicker asphalt. A stronger investigation compares truck counts, axle configurations, aggregate quality, density tests, subgrade moisture and surface drainage before deciding on the repair. Rutting in the asphalt layer, rutting in the unbound base and deformation in the subgrade can look similar at the pavement surface but require different corrective actions. Cores, deflection measurements and trench observations help locate the deforming layer.

The fourth-power relationship is useful for demonstrating the possibility of disproportionate heavy-vehicle damage. With a reference axle of 80 kN, an axle of 120 kN has a damage index of 1.5^4 = 5.0625 under that specific mathematical approximation. An axle of 160 kN gives sixteen times the reference index. These are not calibrated equivalent single-axle load factors and cannot by themselves establish pavement life. Different axle groups and pavement failure mechanisms need appropriate design factors. Repeating one rough exponent for every vehicle and road can substantially distort a project forecast.

Groundwater observations may be equally important. Inspect edge drains, outlets, sealed shoulders, culverts and seasonal water-table elevation. Wet granular base layers can lose stiffness; poor drainage can increase pumping or moisture damage. Survey crossfall and ponding locations during storms rather than assuming the road is well drained because it looks dry during a site visit. Short-lived resurfacing gains often reflect treating symptoms without correcting moisture or structural failure. Construction records should establish whether specified compaction and binder quality were achieved.

A rigid concrete option changes the problem, rather than eliminating it. With ideal plate rigidity proportional to the cube of slab thickness, increasing thickness from 200 to 220 millimetres increases elastic plate stiffness by a factor (220/200)^3 ≈ 1.331 if modulus and Poisson ratio remain constant. It does not imply a 33% improvement in fatigue life or durability. Joints, load transfer, thermal gradients, soil support and water under the slab must still be evaluated.

Compare three strategies: drainage rehabilitation with local base repairs, strengthened flexible pavement and a reconstructed concrete pavement. Use an equal analysis horizon and include construction costs, traffic disruption, periodic maintenance, embodied emissions, heavy vehicle demand uncertainty and probable storm exposure. A cheapest-initial-cost decision may transfer risk to future road users and maintenance budgets. A reconstruction option could also impose significant present-day access costs on businesses. Explain the evidence that would cause you to change your preferred option.

Your project report should contain a distress map, a targeted sampling plan, a table distinguishing damage mechanisms from visual symptoms, a transparent axle sensitivity graph and a lifecycle comparison. Do not claim that the simplified 16-fold damage value predicts actual service life. Document exactly which elements require a jurisdictionally adopted mechanistic–empirical or other approved pavement design method.

Lesson 5.4: Maintenance, condition and lifecycle decisions

Physical and planning meaning

Condition surveys describe cracking, rut depth, roughness, skid resistance and drainage. A condition index is a management tool, not a physical law. The timing of maintenance matters because small interventions on a sound road can be more effective than waiting until full reconstruction is unavoidable.

Governing relationship

Present value PV = Σ cost_t/(1+r)^t when costs occur at year t.

Interpreted worked case

A road agency compares an immediate treatment of 1 million cost units with a 1.3 million treatment due in five years. At 5% annual discount, the future cost has PV ≈ 1.019 million. This simple comparison ignores performance, residual value and user delays.

Extended pavement case: a freight corridor with seasonal flooding

A highway carrying heavy trucks develops wheel-path rutting and cracking one year after major resurfacing. A simple design memo initially attributes the problem to unexpectedly high axle loads and requests thicker asphalt. A stronger investigation compares truck counts, axle configurations, aggregate quality, density tests, subgrade moisture and surface drainage before deciding on the repair. Rutting in the asphalt layer, rutting in the unbound base and deformation in the subgrade can look similar at the pavement surface but require different corrective actions. Cores, deflection measurements and trench observations help locate the deforming layer.

The fourth-power relationship is useful for demonstrating the possibility of disproportionate heavy-vehicle damage. With a reference axle of 80 kN, an axle of 120 kN has a damage index of 1.5^4 = 5.0625 under that specific mathematical approximation. An axle of 160 kN gives sixteen times the reference index. These are not calibrated equivalent single-axle load factors and cannot by themselves establish pavement life. Different axle groups and pavement failure mechanisms need appropriate design factors. Repeating one rough exponent for every vehicle and road can substantially distort a project forecast.

Groundwater observations may be equally important. Inspect edge drains, outlets, sealed shoulders, culverts and seasonal water-table elevation. Wet granular base layers can lose stiffness; poor drainage can increase pumping or moisture damage. Survey crossfall and ponding locations during storms rather than assuming the road is well drained because it looks dry during a site visit. Short-lived resurfacing gains often reflect treating symptoms without correcting moisture or structural failure. Construction records should establish whether specified compaction and binder quality were achieved.

A rigid concrete option changes the problem, rather than eliminating it. With ideal plate rigidity proportional to the cube of slab thickness, increasing thickness from 200 to 220 millimetres increases elastic plate stiffness by a factor (220/200)^3 ≈ 1.331 if modulus and Poisson ratio remain constant. It does not imply a 33% improvement in fatigue life or durability. Joints, load transfer, thermal gradients, soil support and water under the slab must still be evaluated.

Compare three strategies: drainage rehabilitation with local base repairs, strengthened flexible pavement and a reconstructed concrete pavement. Use an equal analysis horizon and include construction costs, traffic disruption, periodic maintenance, embodied emissions, heavy vehicle demand uncertainty and probable storm exposure. A cheapest-initial-cost decision may transfer risk to future road users and maintenance budgets. A reconstruction option could also impose significant present-day access costs on businesses. Explain the evidence that would cause you to change your preferred option.

Your project report should contain a distress map, a targeted sampling plan, a table distinguishing damage mechanisms from visual symptoms, a transparent axle sensitivity graph and a lifecycle comparison. Do not claim that the simplified 16-fold damage value predicts actual service life. Document exactly which elements require a jurisdictionally adopted mechanistic–empirical or other approved pavement design method.
