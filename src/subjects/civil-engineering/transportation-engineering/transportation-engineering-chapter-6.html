Learning objectives

Learn to characterize the mechanisms, assumptions and observational evidence connecting rainfall on road surfaces and runoff pathways, ditches, channels and hydraulic conveyance, culverts and road–stream crossings, construction staging and work-zone operations. Demonstrate unit consistency, interpret safety and access alongside vehicle throughput, and compare model predictions against field observations before making recommendations.

§6.3 Rainfall on road surfaces and runoff pathways

Lesson 6.1: Rainfall on road surfaces and runoff pathways

Physical and planning meaning

A paved roadway is a compact catchment with high direct runoff during rainfall. Sheet flow travels across crossfall to gutters, channels and inlet structures. Blocking a low point can produce ponding even when the pavement itself remains structurally sound.

Governing relationship

Rational screening equation Q = 0.00278 C i A, where i is mm/h and A is hectares.

Interpreted worked case

With C = 0.85, i = 60 mm/h and A = 2 ha, Q ≈ 0.2836 m³/s. This assumes a matching design intensity and catchment time of concentration.

Investigating an assumption

Examine how blocked inlets, climate-driven rainfall change, and upstream impervious areas alter the design problem.

Important limitations

The rational method is primarily a peak-flow screening approach for suitably small catchments, not a full storm hydrograph.

Sensitivity and uncertainty

Vary one uncertain parameter while keeping others fixed, then vary a second parameter jointly. A steep response indicates that the result should be reported as a range rather than a single number. Do not equate a sensitivity exercise with a statistical confidence interval; uncertainty also includes model structure and measurement bias.

Three distinct evidence-based investigations

Establish a baseline for person throughput and bus headway variability; compare that baseline with an alternative centred on transit priority coupled with safer stops. Your written answer must separate improvements that follow from a formula from improvements that are only plausible hypotheses. Address conditions in which the intervention would fail.

§6.4 Ditches, channels and hydraulic conveyance

Lesson 6.2: Ditches, channels and hydraulic conveyance

Physical and planning meaning

Roadside ditches convey runoff and sometimes intercept shallow seepage. Their shape, side slopes and surface roughness determine carrying capacity under an assumed steady water level. Erosion risk is strongly connected to mean velocity and local soil or lining properties.

Governing relationship

Manning Q = (1/n)AR^(2/3)S^(1/2), with hydraulic radius R=A/P.

Interpreted worked case

For a rectangular channel 2 m wide and 0.5 m deep, A=1 m² and wetted perimeter is 3 m. For n=0.03, S=0.002, the flow estimate is about 0.716 m³/s.

Investigating an assumption

Compare a vegetated ditch with a smoother lined ditch: which might have higher capacity, and what are the tradeoffs for erosion and treatment?

Important limitations

Manning estimates do not prove the channel is erosion-resistant, self-cleaning or safe in a flood.

Measurement protocol

Specify which observations distinguish the proposed engineering relationship from a coincidental correlation. Record station location, observation interval, vehicles or users included, calibration checks and missing data. A correct equation evaluated on a biased dataset can create a less defensible conclusion than a rough estimate accompanied by an explicit uncertainty interval.

§6.5 Culverts and road–stream crossings

Lesson 6.3: Culverts and road–stream crossings

Physical and planning meaning

A culvert can be controlled by conditions at its entrance or outlet. A simple full-flow Manning calculation may provide a benchmark but not an inlet-control, tailwater or overtopping assessment. Sediment transport, fish passage and debris blockage also affect the choice and orientation of a crossing.

Governing relationship

For a full circular culvert A=πD²/4 and R=D/4 in Manning flow.

Interpreted worked case

With D=0.9 m, n=0.013 and slope 0.004, an idealized full-flow Manning estimate is about 1.19 m³/s. The real hydraulic capacity may be lower when entrance losses or tailwater dominate.

Investigating an assumption

How would the project change if the stream is a fish migration corridor or prone to large debris in monsoon floods?

Important limitations

Do not substitute full-flow Manning output for a jurisdiction-specific culvert design check.

Dimensional audit

Before computing, rewrite every quantity in one coherent system of units. A time rate needs a time denominator; a spatial density needs a road-length denominator; a percentage must become a decimal before entering an algebraic equation. Unit checking can reject many plausible-looking but incorrect answers without any specialist software.

§6.6 Construction staging and work-zone operations

Lesson 6.4: Construction staging and work-zone operations

Physical and planning meaning

Construction on operating roads creates temporary capacity, safety and accessibility constraints. Work-zone planning must account for workers, drivers, pedestrians, cyclists, emergency access and the construction process. A plan should link temporary traffic management, communication, monitoring and incident response.

Governing relationship

Temporary bottleneck queue increase rate = arrivals − service when positive.

Interpreted worked case

An approach carrying 900 veh/h is reduced to one lane with estimated work-zone capacity 700 veh/h. Over a 90-minute closure, a simplified queue builds by 300 vehicles if demand and service are constant. The physical queue length cannot be deduced without vehicle spacing assumptions.

Investigating an assumption

Identify a closure window that minimizes total person delay rather than only private-car delay, considering freight and buses.

Important limitations

Traffic control details must follow the legally applicable current manual; an educational sketch is not a compliant work-zone plan.

Alternative design comparison

Compare at least two interventions against the same baseline. Record whether each changes demand, available capacity, exposure, pavement condition, human behavior or monitoring quality. An alternative with the largest improvement in one performance indicator is not necessarily superior when its cost, emissions, access impacts and failure modes are considered.

§6.9 Extended hydraulic case: roadway inundation and a culvert bottleneck

Extended hydraulic case: roadway inundation and a culvert bottleneck

A low-lying road repeatedly floods at a stream crossing during the rainy season. Residents report that the road became more frequently impassable after upstream land development. A first screening exercise estimates runoff from a paved 2.5 hectare contributing area with runoff coefficient 0.85 and rainfall intensity 70 mm/h. The rational equation Q=0.00278CiA gives approximately 0.414 m³/s for that modeled local catchment. This is a peak-rate estimate for a selected design intensity, not a storm hydrograph. It also excludes any substantial upstream river discharge that may pass through the same crossing.

Suppose the existing culvert is a circular pipe with 0.9 metre internal diameter, Manning roughness 0.013 and bed slope 0.004. Under an idealized full-flow assumption its area is πD²/4 ≈ 0.636 m² and hydraulic radius is D/4=0.225 m. Applying Manning gives a benchmark flow of approximately 1.14 m³/s. Comparing this with 0.414 m³/s might appear to show plenty of capacity. That conclusion is unwarranted without an inlet, outlet and tailwater analysis. Flow can be restricted by approach geometry, submerged outlet, debris, a damaged entrance, silt accumulation or high downstream water level.

The next field campaign should measure catchment boundaries, culvert barrel dimensions, inlet geometry, trash accumulation, surveyed upstream and downstream invert elevations, high-water marks and possible overtopping routes. Photograph erosion around the approach road and identify the elevation of vulnerable homes. Time of concentration must be estimated from realistic flow paths before selecting rainfall intensity from an intensity-duration-frequency relationship. For a changing climate, discuss the difference between historical rainfall records and future hazard scenarios. Report the source and uncertainty of all return-period inputs.

Consider a second culvert, an improved entrance, a larger waterway opening, a bypass path and improved debris maintenance as possible interventions. A larger culvert might lower upstream water but increase downstream velocity and scour. A protected natural channel may be important for ecological connectivity and fish passage. Raising the roadway can reduce interruption while diverting floodwater elsewhere; impacts on nearby land cannot be excluded from the scope of analysis. The decision should compare flooding of the infrastructure and flooding created or worsened by the infrastructure.

For a work-zone scenario, assume temporary lane closures reduce service by 250 vehicles per hour while arrival demand remains fixed for two hours. A simple fluid-queue estimate suggests a net buildup of 500 vehicles, but this ignores diversion, reduced demand and queue spillback. The real construction plan must include safe pedestrian and emergency access and applicable temporary traffic control standards. Hydraulics and traffic operations are coupled parts of one construction problem.

Submit a causal diagram of why the road floods, separate calculations for local runoff and idealized culvert conveyance, a list of critical missing information and a multi-criteria alternatives table. State explicitly why neither Manning's equation nor the rational formula can independently certify a culvert design. A convincing engineering recommendation should be safe under the most consequential plausible failure modes, not merely under the default calculator settings.

Extended hydraulic case: roadway inundation and a culvert bottleneck

A low-lying road repeatedly floods at a stream crossing during the rainy season. Residents report that the road became more frequently impassable after upstream land development. A first screening exercise estimates runoff from a paved 2.5 hectare contributing area with runoff coefficient 0.85 and rainfall intensity 70 mm/h. The rational equation Q=0.00278CiA gives approximately 0.414 m³/s for that modeled local catchment. This is a peak-rate estimate for a selected design intensity, not a storm hydrograph. It also excludes any substantial upstream river discharge that may pass through the same crossing.

Suppose the existing culvert is a circular pipe with 0.9 metre internal diameter, Manning roughness 0.013 and bed slope 0.004. Under an idealized full-flow assumption its area is πD²/4 ≈ 0.636 m² and hydraulic radius is D/4=0.225 m. Applying Manning gives a benchmark flow of approximately 1.14 m³/s. Comparing this with 0.414 m³/s might appear to show plenty of capacity. That conclusion is unwarranted without an inlet, outlet and tailwater analysis. Flow can be restricted by approach geometry, submerged outlet, debris, a damaged entrance, silt accumulation or high downstream water level.

The next field campaign should measure catchment boundaries, culvert barrel dimensions, inlet geometry, trash accumulation, surveyed upstream and downstream invert elevations, high-water marks and possible overtopping routes. Photograph erosion around the approach road and identify the elevation of vulnerable homes. Time of concentration must be estimated from realistic flow paths before selecting rainfall intensity from an intensity-duration-frequency relationship. For a changing climate, discuss the difference between historical rainfall records and future hazard scenarios. Report the source and uncertainty of all return-period inputs.

Consider a second culvert, an improved entrance, a larger waterway opening, a bypass path and improved debris maintenance as possible interventions. A larger culvert might lower upstream water but increase downstream velocity and scour. A protected natural channel may be important for ecological connectivity and fish passage. Raising the roadway can reduce interruption while diverting floodwater elsewhere; impacts on nearby land cannot be excluded from the scope of analysis. The decision should compare flooding of the infrastructure and flooding created or worsened by the infrastructure.

For a work-zone scenario, assume temporary lane closures reduce service by 250 vehicles per hour while arrival demand remains fixed for two hours. A simple fluid-queue estimate suggests a net buildup of 500 vehicles, but this ignores diversion, reduced demand and queue spillback. The real construction plan must include safe pedestrian and emergency access and applicable temporary traffic control standards. Hydraulics and traffic operations are coupled parts of one construction problem.

Submit a causal diagram of why the road floods, separate calculations for local runoff and idealized culvert conveyance, a list of critical missing information and a multi-criteria alternatives table. State explicitly why neither Manning's equation nor the rational formula can independently certify a culvert design. A convincing engineering recommendation should be safe under the most consequential plausible failure modes, not merely under the default calculator settings.
