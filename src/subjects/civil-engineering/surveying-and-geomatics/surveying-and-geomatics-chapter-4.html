Traverse Computations, Error Propagation and Compass Rule Adjustment

Comprehensive university unit covering foundational theory, design formulations, laboratory evidence, and step-by-step solved problems in Traverse Computations, Error Propagation and Compass Rule Adjustment.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Surveying and Geomatics study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§4.1 The central engineering problem

The central engineering problem

Traverse coordinates accumulate from lengths and directions. A closed polygon provides two independent closure components, and angular closure is an additional check.

Bowditch corrections are a practical length-proportional approximation under specified observation characteristics; rigorous least squares is preferable for heterogeneous and high-order control.

Control networks should resist gross errors through redundancy, geometric strength and independently occupied checks; a mathematically small residual does not guarantee a correct datum.

§4.2 Four lesson sequence

Four lesson sequence

Lesson 4.1: Traverse azimuths and coordinate increments

Learning target and core concept

A traverse converts measured lengths and azimuths into component movements. The first-order computations are ΔE=L sinθ and ΔN=L cosθ for north-referenced azimuths. Be careful with quadrants, magnetic versus grid directions, clockwise sign conventions and angular wraparound. For a closed traverse the summed coordinate increments should be zero, except measurement error and network limitations.

Worked numerical or method example. At L=200 m and azimuth 120°, ΔE=173.205 m and ΔN=−100.000 m. The negative northing increment means travel southward despite positive distance. Validate with an independent sketch and reverse bearing of 300°.

Field context: Industrial estate traverse

A four-leg control traverse includes bearings crossing 360°. Normalize azimuths and use correct sine/cosine conventions. The computed coordinate closure and independent angle closure should be recorded separately, including the source of initial orientation.

Required engineering artifact. A point-by-point coordinate ledger and annotated plot showing north/east increments with signed directions.

Lesson 4.2: Closing errors and the Bowditch rule

Learning target and core concept

The Bowditch, or compass, rule distributes east and north misclosures to legs in proportion to leg length. It is historically useful for traverses with comparable distance and angle quality, but should not be mistaken for a universal maximum-likelihood estimator. The linear correction to a leg is −f_E L_i/ΣL and −f_N L_i/ΣL; after application the corrected coordinates close algebraically.

Worked numerical or method example. For total traverse length 800 m, a 200 m leg gets 25% of each coordinate correction. If east misclosure is +0.040 m, that leg receives −0.010 m east correction; this operation alone says little about whether the original control datum was correct.

Field context: Perimeter loop correction

One leg crosses a busy roadway and was observed under poorer conditions. Bowditch corrections allocate by length, not by this uncertainty. Compare the convenient method with weights reflecting observed precision and identify circumstances where the two diverge.

Required engineering artifact. A table of raw misclosure, length ratios, corrections and corrected point coordinates, accompanied by an explicit approximation caveat.

Lesson 4.3: Weighted adjustment and least-squares reasoning

Learning target and core concept

Survey adjustment combines observations with a functional model and a stochastic model. Assign realistic standard deviations before computing weights; an arbitrary large weight can hide a bad measurement. Weighted means provide a transparent first example, but multi-dimensional networks need equations, Jacobians, covariance propagation and normalized residual testing. Check the condition number when control geometry is weak.

Worked numerical or method example. If independent point estimates are 100.010±0.010 m and 99.990±0.020 m, weights are 10000 and 2500. The weighted mean is 100.006 m, and formal standard uncertainty is 1/√12500≈0.00894 m.

Field context: Two independent control occupations

Two crews estimate one benchmark coordinate with different variances. A weighted mean is useful only if estimates share a compatible reference, have unbiased errors and their correlations are negligible. Document whether both relied on the same base station.

Required engineering artifact. A weighted result, its formal uncertainty, and a qualitative assessment of correlation risk with an outlier reobservation plan.

Lesson 4.4: Area computations, network design and QA

Learning target and core concept

Parcel area from planar coordinates uses the shoelace sum; its sign records vertex orientation. It must not be interpreted as ellipsoidal land area unless appropriate corrections or geodesic algorithms are used. Non-closed or self-intersecting boundaries can produce misleading algebraic results. Control geometry should have independently verifiable points with substantial angular diversity; narrow traverses magnify lateral uncertainty.

Worked numerical or method example. For quadrilateral (0,0),(20,0),(24,10),(0,10), area equals 220 m² by the shoelace formula, not 200 m². The skewed top edge changes the area and should appear in the plan sketch.

Field context: Irregular land parcel

A cadastral polygon has a notch and one repeated corner point. First verify vertex order and simple polygon geometry; the shoelace identity alone will happily return an algebraic area for a self-intersecting path.

§4.4 Extended field note: why a network is more than a closed polygon

Extended field note: why a network is more than a closed polygon

Traverse surveying provides the computational bridge between instrument directions and mapped positions. For a north-referenced azimuth θ, east increment is L sinθ and north increment is L cosθ. The sign is determined by the quadrant; an angle of 120 degrees moves the point eastward and southward. When a field book mixes grid azimuth, magnetic direction and local orientation without explicit conversions, the forward calculations can be individually flawless while the final network is rotated or reflected.

A closed traverse provides a useful algebraic control: the vector sum of coordinate increments should be zero after returning to the starting point, subject to measurement errors. Yet the magnitude of closure is only a diagnostic. Errors may cancel in pairs, and all points may be shifted by a common control error without affecting the closure. A second loop or external tie increases redundancy. Better network geometry, using wider azimuth diversity and avoiding unnecessarily elongated triangles, also makes it easier to distinguish different error sources.

The compass or Bowditch rule distributes north/east closure in proportion to line length. For four sides with length proportions 0.2, 0.3, 0.1 and 0.4, the first receives 20 percent of each total coordinate correction. This allocation is computationally transparent and appropriate as an illustrative approximation when observation quality is roughly comparable. It is not derived from the full covariance structure of mixed angle and distance observations. If a short line has a poorly observed angle while a long line has high-quality instruments, the length-proportional rule can conceal the actual uncertainty allocation.

Weighted estimation introduces the stochastic side of network adjustment. Two independent estimates with standard deviations σ1 and σ2 can be combined using reciprocal variance weights. The combined estimate shifts toward the more precise value; the formal uncertainty shrinks if errors are independent and unbiased. But if both observations depend on one incorrect control coordinate or one shared reference station, naïve independence assumptions overstate confidence. For a genuine multi-point network, least squares needs a measurement model, linearization where required, covariance treatment and statistical diagnostics. Even a small adjustment residual does not prove that the adopted datum is correct.

An area computation likewise requires geometry beyond arithmetic. The shoelace formula calculates the oriented planar area from ordered vertices, not the legal or geodesic area of land by itself. A reversed vertex order changes sign; a self-intersecting polygon can yield a deceptive algebraic sum. Test simple-polygon validity, control quality, units and projection distortion. For any official boundary report, professional authority and land records are essential evidence in addition to a coordinate-area calculation.

Capstone analysis: an urban control network with mixed reliability

A redevelopment site contains two closed traverses sharing an initial monument. One loop is measured through open corridors with long high-quality EDM lines; the other crosses streets with short sights, vehicle vibration and difficult target identification. If a team blindly applies Bowditch correction to both and compares only relative closure, the mathematical treatment may conceal unequal observation quality. Plot the geometry and observation network before making any adjustment. Note which control points are shared: a common error in the initial monument can move both loops together while preserving individual closure.

For a simple illustrative 420 m loop with a 40 mm east closure, a 120 m leg receives −11.43 mm east correction under the length-proportional rule. This is not the standard error of the corrected point. A rigorous adjustment would use angle and distance functional equations and their uncertainty model, not just length fractions. Explain what instrument repetition, calibration, target-centering observations and redundant routes would support the weights. Compare the corrected route against a control point excluded from the adjustment so that external accuracy can be assessed separately from fit.

A proposed parcel boundary is represented by a skewed quadrilateral. Before calculating its area, verify vertex order and ensure its edges do not self-intersect. For coordinates (0,0), (20,0), (24,10), (0,10), decomposition into rectangle and triangle gives 200+20=220 m², matching the shoelace result. The agreement verifies computation for the chosen coordinates but says nothing about whether they describe a lawful land boundary. The source surveys, monuments and applicable cadastral authority still matter.

The final network memo should provide raw observations, initial orientation, east and north misclosure, adjustment model, corrected coordinates, residuals, independent control checks and legal qualifications. A result can be operationally adequate for a temporary site sketch yet inadequate for foundations or legal property definition. Separate those uses in the acceptance statement. Students should learn to prioritize the risk of a confidently wrong reference over an attractive small residual.
