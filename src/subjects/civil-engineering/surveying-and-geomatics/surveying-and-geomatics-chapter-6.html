Mapping Technologies, Photogrammetry and LiDAR Scanning

Comprehensive university unit covering foundational theory, design formulations, laboratory evidence, and step-by-step solved problems in Mapping Technologies, Photogrammetry and LiDAR Scanning.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Surveying and Geomatics study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§6.1 The central engineering problem

The central engineering problem

Digital photogrammetry extracts geometric information from overlapping images tied to control; scale and elevation errors are sensitive to camera calibration and network geometry.

Lidar point density is not the same as verified vertical accuracy. Data processing, classification, independent checkpoints and a documented uncertainty report are needed for terrain products.

GIS operations may silently change units, projections and spatial resolution. Metadata should preserve original frames, transformations, sources and dates.

§6.2 Four lesson sequence

Four lesson sequence

Lesson 6.1: Image scale and ground sampling distance

Learning target and core concept

Ground sampling distance is an approximate pixel footprint, not independently verified map accuracy. It depends on camera pixel pitch, focal length, flying height above ground, image geometry and surface relief. Photogrammetric products also depend on overlap, lens distortion, tie-point stability, independent ground control and bundle adjustment. A low nominal GSD cannot compensate for weak ground control.

Worked numerical or method example. Using altitude 120 m above ground, pixel pitch 2.4 µm and focal length 24 mm, approximate GSD=120×0.0024/24=0.012 m per pixel, or 1.2 cm/pixel. This is nominal image sampling, not a positional accuracy claim.

Field context: Drone survey of clay quarry

The site has a 40 m height range and a narrow flight corridor. A constant-height nominal GSD underestimates sampling variability, and image texture is poor on uniform surfaces. Plan overlap, ground checks, camera calibration and relief-aware processing.

Required engineering artifact. A table of predicted GSD across elevation zones and an explanation of why pixel count alone cannot certify elevation accuracy.

Lesson 6.2: Stereo photogrammetry and height

Learning target and core concept

Stereo image geometry uses parallax differences to infer elevations when overlapping images and camera orientations are calibrated. Simplified vertical stereo equations are helpful for sensitivity checks, but actual processing uses bundle adjustment and 3D intersection. Baseline and focal length must be expressed in compatible units; poorly conditioned geometry or sparse tie points increases vertical uncertainty.

Worked numerical or method example. In an ideal vertical stereo model with B=120 m, f=0.100 m and measured parallax p=0.020 m, object range Z=Bf/p=600 m. If the camera height above datum is 700 m, derived object height is 100 m; small p errors can strongly change Z.

Field context: Stereo image block over bridge

Sparse tie points occur on water and moving vehicles. The simple parallax-height relation remains a useful sensitivity exercise, but wrong focal length or weak exterior orientation can create systematic bridge deck errors.

Lesson 6.3: Lidar acquisition and elevation quality

Learning target and core concept

Lidar sensors estimate ranges from laser observations and positioning/orientation information. Nominal point density can be approximated from pulse rate, platform velocity and swath width, but scanning distribution, overlaps, filtering and occlusion change the delivered density. Absolute vertical accuracy must be checked against independent ground checkpoints. Dense point clouds can be systematically biased by poor boresight calibration.

Worked numerical or method example. For a nominal 200 kHz pulse rate, 50 m/s flight speed and 400 m effective swath, mean acquisition rate is 200000/(50×400)=10 pulses/m² before losses and overlap. This is not an achieved ground-point density.

Field context: Airborne lidar floodplain

The specification calls for bare-earth elevation modeling under seasonal vegetation. High total pulse density does not guarantee enough ground returns. Identify independent terrain checkpoints, overlapping swath checks, withheld classification samples and spatial bias maps.

Required engineering artifact. A pulse-rate density calculation, a plan for achieved-ground density measurement, and an independent accuracy report template.

Lesson 6.4: Remote sensing, GIS integration and map projections

Learning target and core concept

Raster and vector GIS layers should each carry a named CRS, axis order and units. Reprojection changes geometry numerically, while georeferencing establishes the link between imagery and control. Resampling a 1 m raster to 0.25 m does not create new terrain knowledge. Terrain models need explicit breaklines and void handling; a smooth appearance can hide geometric error.

Worked numerical or method example. A 1000 m ground line recorded in grid coordinates at combined scale factor 0.99982 has nominal grid length 999.82 m. Ignoring the 0.18 m difference may be tolerable for a small sketch but unacceptable for some long precision alignments.

Field context: Municipal GIS layer integration

§6.4 Extended field note: samples, point clouds and map accuracy

Extended field note: samples, point clouds and map accuracy

A photogrammetric image pixel is a sampling element. For an ideal nadir-facing camera, nominal GSD scales approximately with above-ground altitude and pixel pitch and inversely with focal length. A 120 m flight height, 2.4 micrometre pixel and 24 mm focal length give about 1.2 cm nominal ground sampling. This figure does not establish absolute mapping accuracy. Lens calibration, camera orientation, image overlap, ground control, tie-point quality, relief and bundle adjustment influence the final product. At different terrain elevations, the same flight altitude relative to a fixed datum produces different above-ground heights and therefore different GSD.

Stereo images support parallax-based height recovery. The idealized relationship range Z=Bf/p indicates why a shorter baseline or smaller measured parallax can create strong height sensitivity. The actual software estimates orientation and positions from many image correspondences and control constraints, often through bundle adjustment. Moving objects, specular water, deep shadows and repeated textures complicate this inference. The correct student question is not “how many pixels do I have?” but “which physical surfaces and landmarks can be reconstructed with independent control?”

Lidar uses range measurements associated with platform position and orientation. An acquisition pulse-density calculation is only a planning estimate: PRF divided by the product of platform speed and swath width. Actual spatial distribution varies with scanning geometry and overlap, and multiple returns do not imply multiple independent pulses. Vegetation can make total-return counts large while ground-point density remains low. A good bare-earth elevation model needs appropriate classification, swath calibration, checkpoints and terrain breaklines. Independent absolute and relative validation should be reported, rather than solely repeating the point-cloud processing software's internal residual.

GIS integration can create another hidden error layer. Resampling a 1 m DEM to 0.25 m cells increases the number of pixels but does not invent new measurements. An attractive hillshade or smooth contour image is not evidence of accuracy. Layer alignment depends on coordinate reference systems, projection parameters, axis conventions, datum realizations and transformations. The same site can be described by different coordinate systems with different distortions; high-quality geospatial metadata are necessary for interoperable use.

For project acceptance, create a holdout checkpoint plan that spans land cover and elevation conditions and was not used to build or adjust the product. Compute statistics on external vertical and horizontal differences, describe sample size and distribution, and clearly separate nominal sampling, relative precision and absolute accuracy. Current ASPRS and USGS lidar specifications provide useful examples of how accuracy classes are documented, but their specific requirements should not be transposed uncritically to every country, dataset or construction contract.

Capstone analysis: integrating drone imagery, lidar and survey ground control

A municipality commissions an updated surface model for a drainage basin with steep embankments, tree cover and low-texture water surfaces. The imaging operator proposes 1 cm nominal ground sampling and a high lidar pulse density as evidence of survey accuracy. An engineering reviewer must distinguish nominal acquisition resolution from verified positional quality. Compute ground sampling distance as a function of flight height above terrain, pixel pitch and focal length; show how different terrain relief changes the effective height and therefore the sample footprint. Then identify camera calibration and ground control requirements.

The same review should consider a stereo geometry model. Parallax sensitivity can be dramatic when the chosen baseline and image geometry produce small measured disparities. A simplified scalar height relation is instructive but not a replacement for calibrated bundle adjustment and independent 3D checkpoints. Weak or repetitive image texture can cause false correspondence even if mathematical residuals look small. A high-density collection of wrong tie points is not reliable terrain evidence.

For lidar, calculate an ideal pulse density from pulse rate, flight velocity and swath width. Then request the actual return classification, ground-point density, acquisition overlap, swath consistency and independent vertical checkpoints. In a wooded floodplain, canopy returns may overwhelm bare-ground observations. A smooth interpolated DEM in an obscured area may reproduce the processing assumptions more faithfully than the real ground. Require maps of voids, poor visibility and interpolation limits. The accepted terrain model needs appropriate breaklines at levees, drains and walls.

Finally, verify geospatial integration. Document every layer's CRS, axis order, map projection and reference epoch. A high-resolution orthophoto could appear aligned with a GIS layer in one neighborhood yet exhibit a systematic shift elsewhere. The handoff should include raw acquisition summaries, processing versions, independent checkpoint RMSE with sample count and terrain distribution, and clear limitations. The student must not treat prettier pixels or more points as an alternative to sound geodetic control.
