Differential Leveling, Optical Instruments and Earth Curvature

Comprehensive university unit covering foundational theory, design formulations, laboratory evidence, and step-by-step solved problems in Differential Leveling, Optical Instruments and Earth Curvature.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Surveying and Geomatics study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§3.1 The central engineering problem

The central engineering problem

A leveling circuit is a chain of backsight and foresight readings referenced to stable marks. The algebraic sum of height differences should match an independently established benchmark difference within project tolerance.

GNSS ellipsoid heights and leveling orthometric heights are not interchangeable. Conversion using a specified geoid model is an approximation whose uncertainty must be recorded.

Closure adjustment distributes the observed discrepancy according to the measurement design, but it does not repair a wrong benchmark, a changed instrument height or unstable turning points.

§3.2 Four lesson sequence

Four lesson sequence

Lesson 3.1: Differential leveling and field books

Learning target and core concept

Differential leveling estimates elevations through backsight-minus-foresight increments. Use consistent notation: a backsight to a known point provides height of instrument; the foresight to a turning point gives its elevation. A turning point must be stable, repeatably occupied and unambiguous. Arithmetic checks on totals detect some transcription failures but cannot detect wrong benchmark identity.

Worked numerical or method example. If benchmark A is at 102.350 m, BS=1.245 m and FS=1.875 m, the next point is H_B=102.350+1.245−1.875=101.720 m. Recording the FS as 1.785 m causes a 90 mm error despite a plausible-looking elevation.

Field context: Waterworks benchmark transfer

Two stable benchmarks sit on opposite sides of a trench and the level instrument must move several times. Label every turning point unambiguously. Avoid mixing instrument heights with staff readings, and compute closure before elevating any critical pipeline invert.

Required engineering artifact. A complete field-book reduction with ΣBS, ΣFS, rise/fall and independent closure, along with a correction narrative if a staff value is transposed.

Lesson 3.2: Curvature, refraction and reciprocal leveling

Learning target and core concept

A long sight line departs from a level surface due to Earth curvature, and atmospheric refraction bends the light ray. Their net effect can be modeled approximately as a function of squared sight distance, with a variable refraction coefficient. Balanced backsight and foresight distances minimize certain effects, but over water the refraction can be unstable. Reciprocal leveling can reduce many systematic effects across an inaccessible crossing.

Worked numerical or method example. Using R=6,371,000 m, d=1,000 m and assumed refraction coefficient k=0.13, the combined magnitude is (1−k)d²/(2R)≈0.0683 m. The sign depends on the height-difference correction convention used; specify it before applying a number.

Field context: Reciprocal leveling across estuary

Sight lines cross warm water. Refraction varies in time and with height above the surface. Use near-simultaneous reciprocal observation sets from both ends, maintain independent geometry checks and log weather trends. Do not claim a universal fixed atmospheric refraction coefficient.

Required engineering artifact. A sensitivity plot for distance and k plus a commentary on why the model may understate uncertainty during unusual meteorological conditions.

Lesson 3.3: Profiles, cross-sections and contour interpretation

Learning target and core concept

Longitudinal profiles locate elevation along a planned centerline; cross-sections describe transverse shape, drainage and earthwork implications. Chainage is an along-alignment coordinate, not always the straight-line distance. Interpolated contours assume a surface model, and should not be treated as exact features across cliffs, retaining walls or inaccessible voids. Record breaklines and independent terrain checks.

Worked numerical or method example. If road centerline elevations are 110.2 m at chainage 0 m and 112.6 m at chainage 80 m, a linear segment has grade (2.4/80)×100=3.0%. The assumption of linear grade should not be extended across a vertical curve without station-specific geometry.

Field context: Drainage corridor topographic model

A smooth DEM interpolated across a concrete drain boundary produces a falsely low embankment and incorrect runoff path. Collect breaklines, photograph inaccessible edges and compare the modeled surface against independent checkpoints.

Required engineering artifact. Longitudinal and cross-section profiles with flagged interpolation gaps, a surface provenance note and an explicit limit on volume estimates.

Lesson 3.4: Level-loop misclosure and adjustments

Learning target and core concept

A level loop is redundant only if the closure reference is independently established. The unadjusted loop misclosure is the sum of BS−FS minus the known endpoint elevation difference. Simple length-weighted adjustment assumes error contributions roughly follow route length; other field designs may require station-weighted or rigorous network adjustment. Corrections reduce algebraic closure but do not prove all observations are free of gross errors.

Worked numerical or method example. For ΣBS−ΣFS=2.046 m and known rise 2.014 m, f=+0.032 m. With 2 km and 3 km run segments, corrections of −0.0128 m and −0.0192 m distribute the observed closure in proportion to distance.

Field context: Road bridge leveling loop

A four-leg circuit connects trusted end marks but has a closure beyond the field tolerance. Length-proportional adjustment can force algebraic closure, yet it is incorrect to publish the result without resolving the possible blunder.

Required engineering artifact. A closure distribution table for a passing case and a separate stop/reobserve decision tree for a failing case.

§3.4 Extended field note: vertical control and physically meaningful elevation

Extended field note: vertical control and physically meaningful elevation

A leveling route is best understood as a sequence of temporary height relationships anchored at known vertical control. A backsight adds to the height of a previously known point, and a foresight subtracts from the height of instrument to obtain the next elevation. This bookkeeping method is simple, but a successful level transfer depends on stable turning points, correctly identified staffs, suitable instrument geometry and controlled observation conditions. A turning point that settles between foresight and backsight breaks the assumed common elevation. Arithmetic sums alone cannot diagnose that event.

Use a field-book audit with each set-up and its raw readings. At a minimum, compute sum of backsights, sum of foresights, their difference, expected benchmark elevation change and misclosure. If an observed route has a +32 mm closure while the end-control difference is independently known, subtract that discrepancy using a justified weighting scheme only after determining it is within acceptance limits. A five-kilometre loop with a short high-noise section may not warrant the same correction allocation as a uniformly observed loop. There is no universal closure allowance detached from instrument class, route method, local practice and project requirements.

Long sight lines introduce curvature and refraction. The Earth departs from a flat horizontal plane and the light ray may bend through vertically varying air. Over an estuary, the atmospheric gradient can change within hours or minutes. A classroom expression involving squared distance and a constant refraction coefficient illustrates sensitivity but should not be interpreted as a complete prediction. Reciprocal leveling creates two geometries to reduce several correlated effects, yet it still demands timing, stable targets and quality checks. If reciprocal observations disagree beyond project tolerance, document the disagreement and repeat in more favorable conditions.

Digital terrain interpretation introduces vertical questions at another scale. Profiles and cross-sections can support drainage and earthwork decisions, but they inherit every error and omission in the source points. If surveyors miss the edge of a culvert headwall, a triangulated surface may create an artificial gentle slope; a contour plot may appear reasonable but produce the wrong flow direction. A breakline should capture the boundary, and a field sketch should identify the structural feature. Do not replace inaccessible areas with a fabricated smooth surface without labeling interpolation and limitations.

Lastly, distinguish vertical reference types. A GNSS solution commonly supplies ellipsoid height, while a project may use orthometric height derived through a geoid model or a local leveling network. The relation H=h−N is straightforward only when frames, epochs and models are compatible. Every elevation transfer ought to name its vertical datum or explicitly state that it is an arbitrary site-relative height. This discipline prevents a few centimetres of apparently harmless notation error from becoming a major invert or flood-protection error.

Capstone analysis: transferring sewer invert elevations across a canal

An urban sanitation project must connect two pipe networks across a canal. The invert design relies on a consistent vertical datum, while a contractor's GNSS results use ellipsoid heights. A separate level route transfers orthometric elevations from a benchmark on the west bank. Begin by confirming the benchmark's physical stability, published or project datum, time of observation and relationship to the proposed works. Do not merely write “metres above sea level” when the controlling surface is undefined.

The level party observes backsights and foresights around the canal and obtains a +32 mm misclosure against independently known end-control difference. The numerical size may or may not be acceptable. Investigate the full set-up records, line lengths, turning points and weather before adjusting. If the project method permits length-proportional correction, two successive route lengths of 2 km and 3 km allocate 12.8 mm and 19.2 mm of the opposite signed correction, respectively. Show adjusted values separately from original readings and check that cumulative corrections reproduce the known endpoint difference.

Across the water, refraction may change rapidly. Use reciprocal leveling or a shorter alternate route where available, recording observation time and meteorological context. A constant-k curvature/refraction calculation predicts a scale but not a reliable site correction by itself. Plot the influence of sight distance on the modeled effect, then discuss whether a 50 percent shorter line truly guarantees a fourfold field improvement when atmospheric variability and other errors are present. The model's quadratic behavior addresses only one error mechanism.

Finally, reconcile GNSS and leveling heights using a documented geoid and vertical datum. If an ellipsoid height is converted using a geoid model incompatible with the design's frame or epoch, the numerical formula can be executed perfectly and still yield a wrong invert elevation. The project deliverable should have a vertical control register, raw field-book, misclosure report, GNSS conversion record, independent checks and a signed list of unresolved risks. The educational exercise cannot accept a real sewer slope or certify the design.
