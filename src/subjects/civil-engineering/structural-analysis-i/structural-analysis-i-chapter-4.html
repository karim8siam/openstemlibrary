Frames, Cables and Three-Hinged Arches

Comprehensive university textbook unit covering fundamental theory, mathematical derivations, numerical modeling, and design validation of Frames, Cables and Three-Hinged Arches. Developed for undergraduate civil engineering study.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Structural Analysis I study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§4.1 Unit purpose

Unit purpose

Frames and arches introduce thrust, bending and geometric coupling in ways that a straight beam diagram cannot capture. Hinges may make a system determinate, yet they also create meaningful kinematic behavior. Cables share some visual similarity with arches but carry a fundamentally different internal resultant.

§4.2 Field scenario

Field scenario

Consider a short pedestrian crossing with two possible concepts: a three-hinged arch and a suspended cable. Compare geometry, support horizontal action, span-clearance implications and whether the member can resist an arbitrary load reversal.

§4.3 Topics and worked reasoning

Topics and worked reasoning

Topic 4.1: Frame equilibrium and internal force

A plane rigid-jointed frame can carry axial force, transverse shear and bending moment in the same member. A three-hinged frame or arch gains an additional internal hinge condition that can permit a global equilibrium solution without compatibility analysis. When isolating part of a frame at an internal hinge, the hinge transmits force but no resisting couple; the two parts have equal and opposite interface forces. A conventional beam-only model can miss large horizontal thrust or member axial forces when a structure contains inclined elements or rigid knees.

Engineering practice: Use clearly labelled joint and member free-body diagrams. For any inclined member, transform the global force components into local axial and transverse axes before sketching internal diagrams. Check the moment at a true pin: it should be zero in the simple idealization. A moment at a member center cannot be inferred from the center geometry alone; it follows from the external actions and constraints.

Fully evaluated study example

A three-hinged parabolic arch has horizontal span 8.0 m, crown rise f=2.5 m, and vertical point load 24 kN at 2.40 m from the left. Its vertical left reaction is 16.800 kN. The equivalent beam moment at crown is 28.800 kN·m; horizontal thrust is 11.755 kN. The crown hinge carries zero moment by construction. The large thrust sensitivity to small f can govern foundation assumptions even when the arch is statically determinate.

Reasoning and interpretation

Which member action becomes dominant at a rigid knee? Start by writing the physical reason, then use an equation or laboratory parameter sweep to support the claim. Compare the result against an extreme case: zero applied load, negligible rise, very long span, perfectly fixed restraint, or load at a support, whichever is physically appropriate. In a valid limiting case, the response should approach a predictable behavior. When a calculation fails an extreme-case check, investigate modeling assumptions before blaming numerical precision.

Topic 4.2: Three-hinged arch thrust

For a three-hinged arch with horizontal springing supports, span L, crown rise f and a crown hinge at midspan, the vertical end reactions match those of the corresponding simply supported beam under identical vertical loads, provided no applied horizontal actions. The crown hinge enforces zero bending moment there, so horizontal thrust H equals the simply supported beam moment at midspan divided by f. For a point load P at x=a, the beam crown moment is R_A L/2-P max(0,L/2-a). The arch section bending moment is M_arch(x)=M_beam(x)-H y(x), where y(x) is the arch ordinate above springing level.

Engineering practice: Test a symmetric point load and an off-center point load. Show that the arch moment at the crown is exactly zero while nonzero bending can remain elsewhere. If the rise is reduced at fixed span and load, required horizontal thrust increases. Do not interpret this idealized thrust as a safe bearing or foundation design without checking support capacity and movement.

Topic 4.3: Cable mechanics and sag

A perfectly flexible cable carries tension but no bending moment. Under a vertical loading uniformly distributed along the horizontal projection, the ideal cable shape is parabolic. With horizontal span L, sag f and load intensity w per horizontal meter, horizontal tension component H=wL²/(8f), while the support vertical reaction is wL/2 for symmetric loading; resultant support tension is sqrt(H²+(wL/2)²). These formulas do not apply unchanged to a cable whose dominant uniform load is its own weight measured along cable length, which leads to a catenary.

Engineering practice: Explore the tension penalty of small sag. Doubling sag reduces H by half if load intensity and span remain fixed, but greater sag may conflict with clearance requirements. Compare parabolic arc-length estimates, total vertical loading, and support forces. Explain why a cable cannot resist an upward normal point load in the same way as a flexural beam.

Topic 4.4: Curved members and equilibrium

Curved members may experience strongly varying local axial and shear components even when global force resultants are simple. A three-hinged arch is determinate because the crown hinge supplies an additional moment condition. In a two-hinged arch the horizontal thrust generally depends on deformation and stiffness, requiring compatibility. Locally, a small element of curved structure transfers normal force, shear and bending according to the selected tangent orientation, with sign conventions that must be consistent along the curve. Centerline geometry is therefore an essential part of the analysis and should be recorded explicitly.

Engineering practice: For a parabolic arch y=4 f x(L-x)/L squared, calculate the slope at a selected section and transform global horizontal and vertical force resultants into tangent and normal components. Compare two arches having the same span and loading but different rise. State which changes are directly controlled by statics and which require stiffness assumptions.

§4.4 Deep dive and misconception repair

Deep dive and misconception repair

Curved systems clinic: an arch is not an inverted cable in every respect

A three-hinged arch and a hanging cable can share a parabolic appearance but resist loads by fundamentally different mechanisms. The arch is a compression-dominated structural element that can also carry shear and bending. A perfectly flexible cable carries tension only and must adopt a geometry compatible with the applied loading. A cable of fixed geometry under changed loading can redistribute shape substantially, whereas the first-order arch analysis generally holds its centerline geometry fixed.

The three-hinged arch's crown condition provides a particularly transparent force solution. First calculate reactions for an equivalent simply supported beam under the same vertical loading. Next evaluate beam bending moment at the crown. Because the arch has an internal hinge with zero transmitted moment, the horizontal thrust multiplied by rise must cancel the equivalent beam moment at that location. This gives H=Mbeam(crown)/f. Finally calculate any other section's arch moment by subtracting H times the arch ordinate from the equivalent beam moment.

This relation illustrates a geometric trade-off. A shallow arch has small rise f. If the equivalent crown moment is fixed, horizontal thrust must be large. More rise can reduce thrust but may increase overall height, change clearance and alter member lengths. Neither choice can be judged from the thrust formula alone. Soil capacity, footing restraint, construction sequence, environmental actions and material stability all matter in design practice.

Load mismatch and arch bending

For a parabolic arch under a full-span uniform load per horizontal length, an ideal funicular compression line can align well with the arch geometry, producing low bending when supports and loading match the assumptions. A concentrated off-center load destroys that exact match, leaving nonzero bending even though crown moment is constrained to zero. This distinction explains why a geometry that seems efficient for one load case may still experience flexure under real varying traffic or asymmetric snow.

The cable equation H=wL²/(8f) requires that w be uniform per horizontal projected distance. If w is instead a constant self-weight per cable arc length, the exact equilibrium shape is a catenary. In a shallow cable the parabolic approximation may be close, but the difference becomes relevant for large sag. One must not use the parabolic formula as a general exact solution for any cable material or loading.

Support horizontal action has an engineering consequence: foundations must supply the balancing thrust or an explicit tension tie must carry it. A three-hinged arch with a tie is not mechanically the same as an untied arch thrusting into the abutments. The tie changes the global force path. When presenting a computed H, label it 'horizontal thrust in this idealized arch' rather than 'safe foundation load'.
