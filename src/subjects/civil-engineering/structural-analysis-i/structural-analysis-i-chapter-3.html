Pin-Jointed Trusses and Displacement

Comprehensive university textbook unit covering fundamental theory, mathematical derivations, numerical modeling, and design validation of Pin-Jointed Trusses and Displacement. Developed for undergraduate civil engineering study.

§3.1 Unit purpose

Unit purpose

Pin-jointed trusses convert global actions into axial force patterns. The geometry creates load paths, so a small angle change can strongly alter compression and tension. Later, virtual work allows displacements to be estimated without solving a full finite-element model.

§3.2 Field scenario

Field scenario

Compare a shallow triangular roof truss with a deep one. Use identical load, steel modulus and chord area, but change the apex rise. Quantify the diagonal force penalty of reducing depth before considering buckling or joint details.

§3.3 Topics and worked reasoning

Topics and worked reasoning

Topic 3.1: Truss idealization and nodal loads

A classical pin-jointed truss carries axial forces in its straight members when loads and reactions act at the ideal joint centers and connections transmit negligible bending. The model assumes members are primarily two-force elements, so the internal resultant is parallel to the member axis. Real gusset plates and eccentric connections may create secondary moments. Loads delivered between joints by a deck require local beam action and cannot be applied to the truss as nodal loads without an appropriate load-transfer model. The sign convention tension positive, compression negative makes method-of-joints equations easier to interpret.

Engineering practice: Start by checking global reactions, then isolate a joint with no more than two unknown independent member forces. Maintain accurate direction cosines for diagonal bars. After obtaining member forces, reassemble joint equilibrium at another node as a check. A member reported as zero-force under one load case may carry substantial force under a different load distribution.

Fully evaluated study example

A symmetric triangular truss with span 6.0 m, apex rise 2.5 m and downward apex load 28 kN has diagonal angle theta=arctan(2h/L)=39.81°. Each inclined bar carries compression magnitude P/(2 sin theta)=21.869 kN; the bottom tie carries tension P/(2tan theta)=16.800 kN. Increasing rise at fixed span lowers those member-force magnitudes. This exact equilibrium result assumes pin joints, symmetric geometry and a load applied at the apex only; off-center deck loading requires a different joint-load pattern.

Reasoning and interpretation

Is a load truly applied through a pin joint? Start by writing the physical reason, then use an equation or laboratory parameter sweep to support the claim. Compare the result against an extreme case: zero applied load, negligible rise, very long span, perfectly fixed restraint, or load at a support, whichever is physically appropriate. In a valid limiting case, the response should approach a predictable behavior. When a calculation fails an extreme-case check, investigate modeling assumptions before blaming numerical precision.

Topic 3.2: Joint and section equilibrium

The method of joints uses two force-equilibrium equations at each isolated planar joint. The method of sections cuts through selected members and applies three independent rigid-body equilibrium equations to one piece; it is efficient when only a few internal forces are required. Both approaches depend on correct free-body isolation and on the premise that member end moments can be neglected. A zero-force-member inference is only valid for its specified joint geometry and loading. In a symmetric triangular truss under an apex load P, the two diagonals carry compression magnitude P/(2 sin theta), and the bottom tie carries tension P/(2 tan theta).

Engineering practice: Demonstrate these results using apex vertical equilibrium and one support joint horizontal equilibrium. Explain how the diagonal force grows as the truss becomes shallow: sin theta approaches zero. Compare an apex load with an unequal load at a lower joint; symmetry ceases and the simple closed-form forces cannot be reused unmodified.

Topic 3.3: Truss deformations and axial stiffness

For small deformation a bar of length L_i, axial force N_i, area A_i and Young modulus E_i changes length by delta_i=N_i L_i/(A_i E_i). A system displacement at a chosen joint can be calculated using virtual work: Delta=sum over members N_i n_i L_i/(A_i E_i), with n_i the member axial force due to a unit virtual force in the desired displacement direction. The units of the unit virtual force must be handled consistently. This result is a linear elastic energy expression and is not a substitute for buckling, connection slip or geometric nonlinear analysis.

Engineering practice: Compute an apex displacement for a triangular truss under a vertical point load. Identify which members have the highest contribution to displacement rather than simply choosing the longest member. Change the cross-sectional area of the tie and observe the effect on vertical movement. The bottom tie is not irrelevant to vertical stiffness even though its axis is horizontal.

Topic 3.4: Load placement and truss influence

A bridge truss often receives wheel loads through floor beams at panel points. For an ideal linear determinate truss, the member-force response to a moving unit load can be constructed from joint-load cases and interpolation through the floor system. A moving force applied directly at an arbitrary point along an axial member would violate the nodal-loading truss idealization unless that member is explicitly modeled as a beam. Influence ordinates should therefore reflect where and how the load enters the structure. Compression reversals under different load positions may become important for stability and detailing.

Engineering practice: Apply unit loads successively to several panel joints, solve the target diagonal force, and assemble an ordinate table. Explain where the largest magnitude occurs and whether the critical load position differs for tension and compression. When using a linear influence diagram between panel points, state that this is a floor-beam distribution assumption and not a continuous point force directly applied to the truss chord.

§3.4 Deep dive and misconception repair

Deep dive and misconception repair

Truss clinic: force equilibrium versus displacement

A truss can have internally large forces while its overall external reaction system looks modest. In a shallow triangle under a force at the apex, compression in the diagonals is amplified by the inverse of the diagonal sine. To resist a fixed downward force, shallow diagonals need large axial compression because only a small portion of each axial force contributes vertical support. The bottom chord then ties the spreading horizontal components together. This explains mechanically why the bottom member carries tension, even though the applied force is purely vertical.

An internal force is not a displacement. A strong member may carry high tension but stretch very little when its area and elastic modulus are large. Conversely, a relatively small axial force can create considerable extension in a long, thin member. To calculate the vertical movement of the apex, virtual work weights each member's real axial force by the virtual axial force produced by a unit apex load. Both the elastic stiffness EA/L and the force transmission geometry enter the result.

Reversal and load cases

Suppose the same triangular truss is subjected to an upward force at the apex. In a geometrically linear, pin-connected model with no buckling or slack elements, all axial forces reverse signs. The formerly compressed diagonals enter tension while the tie enters compression. This mathematical sign reversal does not prove that the member system can resist the reversed load safely: a slender tie designed only for tension may be inadequate in compression, and pin or connection details may not restrain the required behavior. The engineering analysis must distinguish the equations of ideal linear response from a viable built structure.

When loads originate at points along a bridge deck, floor beams, stringers and connection details transmit the actions into truss panel points. Treating a wheel as an arbitrary nodal force without specifying the deck system can misrepresent both local member bending and global truss response. A basic panel-point model remains valuable when the load path is clearly described and the omitted local actions are checked elsewhere. The intended response, not modeling fashion, decides the right level of detail.

Verification by a second joint

After solving the two diagonal forces from apex equilibrium, isolate the left support joint. The horizontal component of the diagonal compression must balance tension in the bottom tie. The vertical component must match the left support reaction. At the right support, the corresponding horizontal and vertical balances should also close. Three separate joint checks guard against an incorrect diagonal angle or reversed member orientation. A reliable numerical truss solver applies these residual checks to every assembled joint, not only to the support equations.

In an axial member, the elastic force-deformation relation N=EAΔL/L assumes uniform axial response, a constant cross section and small extension. It cannot estimate bending due to eccentric connections, local member instability or postbuckling. When using virtual work, keep the load and virtual-force units consistent: a virtual force of 1 kN is not numerically the same as a unit force of 1 N. The displacement must ultimately have dimensions of length.
