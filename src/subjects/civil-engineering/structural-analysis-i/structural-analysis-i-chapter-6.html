Moving Loads and Influence Lines

Comprehensive university textbook unit covering fundamental theory, mathematical derivations, numerical modeling, and design validation of Moving Loads and Influence Lines. Developed for undergraduate civil engineering study.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Structural Analysis I study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§6.1 Unit purpose

Unit purpose

Moving actions convert an otherwise static equilibrium model into a placement optimization problem. The influence-line method distinguishes the structural transfer mechanism from the moving vehicle schedule. It is used here as an introduction to quasistatic bridge response rather than a substitute for code-specified traffic-load models.

§6.2 Field scenario

Field scenario

Compare a single heavy maintenance vehicle with a two-axle vehicle crossing the same beam. The largest support reaction and the largest midspan moment may occur at different vehicle positions; record both.

§6.3 Topics and worked reasoning

Topics and worked reasoning

Topic 6.1: What an influence line represents

A shear or moment diagram shows variation along a member when the load position is held fixed. An influence line instead shows variation of one specified response coordinate as a unit load moves through candidate positions. For a simple beam, the left reaction ordinate under a unit downward force at x=z is 1-z/L. At a section c, the moment influence ordinate is z(L-c)/L for z no greater than c and c(L-z)/L for z at least c. These piecewise linear relations follow directly from statics and should be distinguished from spatial bending-moment curves.

Engineering practice: Choose a response target before drawing an influence line and state whether it is a reaction, shear, moment or displacement. For a selected section the moment influence line reaches its maximum at the section. Check the dimensions: a unit-force moment influence ordinate has dimensions of length; multiplying by an actual wheel force gives moment.

Fully evaluated study example

For a simply supported span 8.0 m, a moving 24 kN point load causes a left reaction of 7.500 kN when positioned at x=5.50 m. The influence ordinate for left reaction is 0.3125. At a chosen section c=L/2, the bending-moment influence ordinate at the load position is 1.2500 m, giving section moment 30.000 kN·m. This is one position in a moving-load search, not the maximum over all possible positions.

Reasoning and interpretation

Is the plot a spatial diagram or a load-position influence line? Start by writing the physical reason, then use an equation or laboratory parameter sweep to support the claim. Compare the result against an extreme case: zero applied load, negligible rise, very long span, perfectly fixed restraint, or load at a support, whichever is physically appropriate. In a valid limiting case, the response should approach a predictable behavior. When a calculation fails an extreme-case check, investigate modeling assumptions before blaming numerical precision.

Topic 6.2: Moving concentrated forces and vehicle trains

For a linear system, the response due to multiple wheel forces equals the sum of axle forces multiplied by their influence ordinates at the current axle positions. Wheel positions outside the span contribute zero. As a vehicle travels, different axle configurations may control different sections and response types. To identify a critical position, one may evaluate candidate axle alignments with influence line breakpoints or scan a finely spaced position grid and then refine around the best value. Grid scans provide approximations rather than mathematically exact maxima unless supplemented by analytic checks.

Engineering practice: For a two-axle vehicle with loads P1 and P2 separated by d, sweep the lead axle through the bridge and track section moment at c. Mark which axle is critical and whether the peak occurs when an axle coincides with c. Vary axle spacing to see when one or both axle forces fit on the span. Record scan resolution so a reported numerical peak can be reproduced.

Topic 6.3: Influence envelopes and distribution

An envelope is the collection of largest and smallest possible response values over permitted vehicle positions or load patterns. It should preserve sign, especially for shear where the positive and negative extrema can both be important. For a moving uniform load, placing loading over favorable influence regions maximizes certain responses; unfavorable regions may be left unloaded when the load is genuinely variable. Structural members receiving deck loads through transverse systems also require a model of how the deck distributes wheel forces to girders and joints.

Engineering practice: Build a moment envelope at multiple sections by sweeping vehicle position. Compare envelope maxima to the fixed-load moment diagram at one arbitrary vehicle position. The envelope cannot be inferred from a single snapshot. Discuss how vehicle path restrictions and axle group geometry constrain the admissible positions.

Topic 6.4: Critical positions and decision accuracy

Critical positions differ between shear and flexure, between support reaction and section response, and between single-axle and multi-axle loading. The correct criterion is a response functional evaluated over allowed load placements, not merely the visually largest axle. Discretized scanning introduces positional error, while oversimplified lane distribution introduces modeling error. It is useful to separate numerical convergence from uncertainty about actual traffic loading. Dynamic amplification and code-specified load models are outside a basic quasistatic influence-line calculation unless explicitly incorporated.

Engineering practice: Repeat the same vehicle scan with a coarse and fine positional step and examine the change in the maximum result. If the maxima differ appreciably, refine further. Check at axle–section coincidence positions analytically. Explain why a mathematically precise answer to a poorly justified vehicle model may still be a poor engineering decision.

§6.4 Deep dive and misconception repair

Deep dive and misconception repair

Influence clinic: where the load is and what response is measured

An influence line should be named by its response coordinate. 'Moment at section c as a function of wheel position z' has a clear meaning; 'the beam's moment influence line' without specifying a section does not. This precision separates the response function from the variable moving action. It also explains why a simple-beam influence line for a support reaction is triangular while the influence line for a moment at an interior section has two linear branches meeting at that section.

For a beam of span L, let a unit force move to z. The left reaction is (L-z)/L. At a section c to the left of z, the local bending moment equals that reaction times c. At a section c to the right of z, subtract the force times (c-z). Simplification gives the two branches of the moment influence function. The peak ordinate for a positive unit load occurs at z=c and equals c(L-c)/L. This result does not depend on EI because the structure is statically determinate.

When a two-axle vehicle moves across the span, each axle contributes its force times the ordinate under its own position. The two loads have a fixed spacing, so their positions are not independent variables. A search that moves each axle separately into its own favorable location invents an impossible vehicle configuration and overestimates the combined maximum. The optimization must preserve axle spacing, axle order and the restriction that forces outside the span make no contribution.

Fine-grid scanning is approximate

A spreadsheet or browser simulation can scan positions in small increments and retain the largest computed response. This is reliable as an exploratory numerical method if its resolution and scope are reported. If the grid step is too large, it may miss a peak occurring when one axle aligns exactly with the observed section. A useful improvement is to evaluate the positions at which an axle crosses the section, an axle enters or exits the span, and any stationary points of a smooth response region.

An envelope shows the maximum permitted response at each structural coordinate after considering the entire set of allowed vehicle positions. The line drawn through envelope values generally does not correspond to any single vehicle placement. It is therefore incorrect to interpret an envelope as an instantaneous bending-moment diagram. Negative shear envelopes require separate treatment since peak positive and peak negative values can govern different checks.

Real bridges require additional factors outside this quasistatic demonstration: vehicle lateral distribution, impact or dynamic amplification where applicable, traffic load models, fatigue, composite interaction, local deck action and code requirements. The influence line still provides the conceptual foundation for understanding which positions matter and why a fixed-load sketch is insufficient.
