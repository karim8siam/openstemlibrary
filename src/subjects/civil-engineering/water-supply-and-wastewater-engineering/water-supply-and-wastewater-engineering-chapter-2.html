§2.1 Distribution systems, storage and pumping

Unit 2: Distribution systems, storage and pumping

Engineering problem

Link conservation of mass and energy to the behavior of pumps, pipes and tanks under realistic operating constraints.

System context. A regional utility and its engineering consultant must keep household access, source protection, operating budget, plant controls, maintenance, public-health risk and the downstream environment in a single decision record. This unit focuses on mass conservation, hydraulic gradients, energy and motor selection, time-dependent storage. A calculated capacity or ideal removal percentage only answers a narrow question; it never certifies operation.

What to carry into the next unit

Treat assumptions as named data rather than invisible constants. Where a model omits fluctuating inflow, temperature, maintenance, unserved customers or instrument uncertainty, state the omission before drawing conclusions. The case studies show how apparently plausible outputs can fail after a changed boundary condition.

Lesson sequence

  • Lesson 1: [Continuity and network topology](lessons/unit-02-lesson-01.md)
  • Lesson 2: [Pipe energy losses and pressure limits](lessons/unit-02-lesson-02.md)
  • Lesson 3: [Pump curves, efficiency and system operating points](lessons/unit-02-lesson-03.md)
  • Lesson 4: [Service reservoirs, transients and emergency storage](lessons/unit-02-lesson-04.md)

Operating envelope

The linked lessons address Continuity and network topology; Pipe energy losses and pressure limits; Pump curves, efficiency and system operating points; Service reservoirs, transients and emergency storage. Separate predictable day-to-day variation from rare hazardous events. A well-built model should expose its domain: the range of flow, concentration, temperature and operating condition over which its assumptions are defensible. Utility investigations often require repeat observations during dry weather and storms, and distribution or collection networks need spatially distributed data rather than a single inlet measurement.

Structured investigation

Choose one real or synthetic utility. Sketch a flow schematic with three explicitly labeled boundaries: incoming quantity and quality, transformed or stored inventory, and outgoing quantity and quality. Provide at least one mass or energy balance, justify the time horizon, and make a quantitative uncertainty or sensitivity comparison. The main practical limitation to discuss is water density and viscosity are approximated as constant.

Conditions to challenge

  • Water density and viscosity are approximated as constant.
  • An imposed Darcy friction factor does not respond to temperature or turbulence.
  • A quadratic pump model can misrepresent efficiency and cavitation.

§2.2 Continuity and network topology

Unit 2 · Lesson 1: Continuity and network topology

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, explain mass conservation using a defensible system boundary; identify the measurements needed to test a conceptual calculation; and distinguish an illustrative formula from an engineering acceptance decision.

Core reasoning

At every steady-state junction, algebraic inflow equals outflow plus known demands. This remains true regardless of pipe material or which pressure solver is used; loops add nonlinear headloss equalities and demand allocations.

Unlike a textbook exercise that starts by offering all required parameters, a real engineering team normally starts with incomplete measurements. The first analytical task is to decide which boundary encloses the operation being investigated. For continuity and network topology, confirm whether the accounting period is hourly, daily, seasonal, or lifetime; whether the reported figures refer to flow, mass or concentration; and where streams enter, leave or are temporarily stored. If a reservoir is refilling, the local flow balance must include a storage term. If a laboratory result is a concentration, multiplying by the simultaneous volumetric flow gives a load; the two quantities answer different planning questions.

Data quality determines what can be inferred. Field instruments need locations and calibration dates. Laboratory measurements need sampling protocols, preservation details and timestamps. Maintenance logs and operator observations often explain anomalies that disappear from a monthly mean. When a parameter is only an estimate, calculate sensitivity before arguing for a larger structure or a more powerful pump. A high flow multiplier cannot rescue a mislabeled unit, and an efficient machine cannot remove contaminants that its process is not designed to target.

Worked engineering investigation

At a junction receiving 45 L/s from one main and 20 L/s from another, serving 18 L/s locally, the outward flow is 47 L/s if storage is not changing.

Boundary check. Ask what the example deliberately omits. For this lesson, omissions may include future demand, seasonal variation, upstream losses, biological variability, energy constraints or monitoring gaps, depending on the equipment. Label these rather than appending an unspecified “safety factor.” A missing constraint is not equivalent to random uncertainty: some constraints must be investigated directly before the calculation is relevant.

Field and design interpretation

Choose flow orientations, write signed balances and test whether inferred negative flows simply indicate reverse direction rather than a numerical failure.

§2.5 Service reservoirs, transients and emergency storage

Core reasoning

Equalization storage balances time-varying delivery against time-varying consumption. Fire reserve, outage reserve, unusable dead storage and transient pressure control are distinct provisions and must not be conflated.
