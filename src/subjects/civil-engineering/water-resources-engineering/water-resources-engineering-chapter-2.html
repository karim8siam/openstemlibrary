§2.1 Theoretical Foundations: Water Allocation Rules, Multi-Sector Trade-Offs and Priorities

Unit 02 — Water Allocation

Unit overview. Allocation is a constrained distribution of scarce, variable water among uses with different reliability, timing, quality and social consequences. Domestic systems need public-health reliability; irrigation withdrawals may be deferrable for a few days but not at a critical growth stage; industrial cooling can return most of an intake at a warmer temperature; rivers need water to sustain ecosystems. A mass balance is necessary but insufficient for allocation: physical capacity, priorities, quality limits, downstream obligations and institutional legitimacy also matter. Optimization models reveal tradeoffs only when their objective functions and constraints honestly represent agreed priorities.

Field problem. Consider two water districts and a downstream reach in an extended dry spell. One district has high economic value per cubic metre but a second district needs a minimum sanitation supply. Maximizing declared monetary benefit without mandatory minimum-service and ecological constraints could produce an ethically and legally unacceptable result. Test a hierarchy of hard requirements and flexible economic optimization; identify which binding constraint causes each unmet demand.

Four topics. Competing Uses, Basin Planning, Optimization, Water Rights Contexts.

Before starting: Sketch a control volume and record which terms describe depths, rates, stored volumes, concentrations, entitlements or monetary values. Identify the boundary, the timescale, the user groups affected and at least one observation whose absence would make a numerical conclusion unreliable. Explain why seasonal variability matters more than a single annual figure for some decisions.

5. Competing Uses

Core concept. Water service objectives may conflict in timing and quality. Minimum household allocations, ecosystem releases, agricultural diversions and industrial cooling requirements require different reliability definitions. Ranking by gross economic output alone undervalues public health and ecological connectivity.

Analysis workflow. Create a table of essential minimums, requested supplies, consumptive fractions and return-flow quality. Allocate scarce monthly supply first under explicit hard constraints and then compare optional allocations.

Engineering interpretation. An allocation that is efficient in the model may be impossible without legal authority and enforceable withdrawal records.

Evidence and measurement. Work with an illustrative alluvial agricultural plain where the available record includes upstream abstraction registers. Begin by naming the controlled variable, the response variable and the geographic location of each observation. Check the date, measurement frequency, applicable units and any uncertainty statement. Where a record is missing or derived from a different location, describe the interpolation explicitly rather than silently assuming that it is representative. Separate a model output from an observation. A discrepancy may reveal a missing process, incorrectly measured inputs, model-structure error or a unit conversion mistake.

From conservation to prediction. A transparent first calculation should reconcile a mass, energy or financial balance as appropriate to the physical mechanism. Convert input quantities into coherent dimensions and display intermediate steps, particularly when mixing depth, area, volume, discharge and time. If a relationship uses a calibrated coefficient, record the period and conditions that support it. Specify an observable metric and the unit in which it would be reported. A useful metric permits comparisons across dates without changing the spatial boundary or its denominator.

Scenario comparison. Compare a baseline in which current practice continues against an alternative with a different capacity, demand, governance rule or environmental reserve. Where possible, change only one assumption at a time, then carry out a second experiment in which multiple uncertain inputs vary together. Explain what each comparison isolates and what interactions remain hidden. Before using a numerical method, distinguish a conservation equation from an empirical relation and a legally mandated constraint. Only conservation identities hold without calibration.

Scope and limitations. Do not interpret a screening model as an approved infrastructure design. Confirm whether physical boundary conditions, environmental constraints, public-health requirements and downstream effects appear in the calculation. A conceptual model that omits return flows, aquifer exchange, water quality or operational failures may overestimate usable supply. Run at least two alternative parameter sets, recording how the predicted outcome changes. A sensitivity experiment identifies leverage but does not by itself assign a probability to uncertainty.

6. Basin Planning

Core concept. A basin plan extends beyond a single structure. It should represent upstream and downstream reaches, seasonal connectivity, urban growth, drought pathways, ecosystem needs and the organizations responsible for each decision. Boundaries follow hydrology but institutions follow jurisdictions; coordination resolves the mismatch.

Analysis workflow. Map the river network and locate demand nodes, storage nodes, gauges and quality constraints. For each node state what enters, leaves and returns. Test whether two different subbasin summaries close to the same downstream total.

Engineering interpretation. Consultation without clear decision authority can create a plan that appears inclusive but cannot be implemented.

Evidence and measurement. Work with an illustrative semi-arid tributary where the available record includes ecological monitoring series. Begin by naming the controlled variable, the response variable and the geographic location of each observation. Check the date, measurement frequency, applicable units and any uncertainty statement. 7. Optimization

Core concept. An allocation problem can minimize shortage or maximize defined benefits subject to supply, minimum flow, capacity and timing constraints. With linear values per water unit and a single budget, a greedy priority order can solve a simplified case; more realistic storage networks require linear or nonlinear programming.

Analysis workflow. Translate a narrative into decision variables with physical units. Evaluate the feasible set before computing a nominal optimum; then change one constraint and record the shadow tradeoff in physical terms.

Engineering interpretation. A mathematical optimum is only valid for the assumed objective and feasible set, not automatically a socially legitimate policy.

Evidence and measurement. Work with an illustrative small coastal settlement where the available record includes reservoir operating logs. 8. Water Rights Contexts

Core concept. Water rights may arise from permits, customary practice, riparian access or other jurisdiction-specific systems. A hydrological model cannot determine entitlements from physical availability alone. Cross-border and cross-district disputes require a clear legal source and enforceable measurement.

Analysis workflow. Separate legal requirements, contracts, announced policy priorities and assumptions in the allocation documentation. Build hypothetical conflicts without stating that a general textbook rule overrides local statutes.

Engineering interpretation. Where records are weak, a permit cap may not translate into actual conservation without meters and enforcement.

§2.2 Unit application and simulation protocol

Unit application and simulation protocol

Laboratory 03 — Allocation under Minimums

Purpose and physical meaning. This laboratory isolates one process within water-resources engineering. It is intentionally a limited analytical or bookkeeping model rather than a digital twin of any real basin. The objective is to predict how an output responds to controlled input changes, reproduce the calculation independently and recognize the boundary beyond which the model loses credibility. Use the graph to explore variation, not as evidence of real-world accuracy.

Equation and units. Essential served = min(A,E); optional served = min(max(A−E,0), R). Inputs are available water in ML/day, essential minimum in ML/day, and other requests in ML/day. Units matter because a factor of 1,000 can arise when litres are confused with cubic metres, or when a daily rate is used directly in a monthly balance. Write out the conversion before comparing scenarios.

Default experiment. Start with the provided values: Available water = 70 ML/day, Essential minimum = 25 ML/day, Other requests = 65 ML/day. Write a verbal prediction of the first output. Make a hand calculation for the default setting wherever the equation permits it; for a time series, reproduce the first step and check that the total storage change equals the accumulation of fluxes. Only then compare with the displayed model output.

Sensitivity investigation. Move the first slider down to 20 ML/day and up to 110 ML/day, holding the other two at their defaults. Examine the direction and shape of the response, and inspect whether the graph is discontinuous at a physical constraint such as zero storage or an allocation cap. Repeat for the second and third inputs. Export the curve to CSV and estimate a finite-difference sensitivity from adjacent rows; specify the unit of the derivative.

Boundary and validation. A single-period priority allocation assuming all water is physically interchangeable, with no spatial conveyance, quality or institutional restrictions. A credible validation task would compare the predicted process with a data record measured under comparable conditions and quantify deviations against uncertainties. Do not tune an input to force agreement and then claim independent verification using the same observations. Distinguish a passing arithmetic check from a validated hydrological model.

Challenge. At which supply value does the essential minimum become unmet? Explain why the priority rule must be established outside the numerical model. Report how the answer changes under an extreme but physically plausible input combination, and identify one omitted mechanism that would be important before real implementation. In your conclusion distinguish observations, model assumptions and proposed decisions. If the simulation becomes insensitive to an input, determine whether a maximum/minimum constraint has become active or an equation is coded incorrectly.

Student deliverables. Include a hand calculation, a three-row baseline/low/high scenario table with units, the exported response plot, a one-paragraph discussion of parameter sensitivity, and a one-paragraph critique of model applicability. Name the specific monitoring records and institutional approvals a responsible engineer would seek before designing a project.

§2.3 Applied project

Applied project

Project 02 — Water Allocation: basin decision memorandum

Decision setting. Consider two water districts and a downstream reach in an extended dry spell. One district has high economic value per cubic metre but a second district needs a minimum sanitation supply. Maximizing declared monetary benefit without mandatory minimum-service and ecological constraints could produce an ethically and legally unacceptable result. Test a hierarchy of hard requirements and flexible economic optimization; identify which binding constraint causes each unmet demand.

Project mandate. Assume a local water commission must make a reversible decision within a limited financial and operational envelope. The project is not to design a real structure; it is to demonstrate reproducible calculations and show when the engineering team must request additional information. Assign a stakeholder team to challenge the baseline scenario and a second team to challenge the preferred intervention. Students should explicitly document disagreement rather than forcing the same preference through all models.

Required tasks.

  1. Define the physical system boundary and provide a map or schematic of inflow, outflow, demands and stores.
  2. Write an auditable water balance and an explicit list of observations needed to populate it.
  3. Compare a baseline, a dry-period stress case and a governance or engineering intervention.
  4. Quantify a service-reliability indicator together with one ecological or distributional consequence.
  5. State at least two risks not represented by the introductory classroom models.
  6. Propose a monitoring plan with observations, frequency, responsible parties and decision triggers.
  7. Prepare a transparent summary for people affected upstream and downstream, separating confirmed evidence from assumptions.

Method. Investigate all four syllabus topics of Unit 2: Competing Uses, Basin Planning, Optimization, Water Rights Contexts. Use the associated pair of simulations as teaching tools, independently verify at least three quantitative results, and do not insert those simulation outputs directly into a permit or construction drawing. When two assumptions point to opposite decisions, describe the range of plausible outcomes and identify which field measurement would most cheaply reduce uncertainty. An optimized number without enforceable operational responsibilities is not a complete solution.

Submission rubric. Technical closure (25%), evidence quality and assumptions (20%), scenario sensitivity and uncertainty (20%), environmental and equity impacts (15%), reproducibility of calculations and charts (10%), and clarity of recommendations (10%). A polished graph cannot compensate for an incorrectly defined basin boundary or missing feasibility constraint.

§2.4 synthesis and review

Unit 02 synthesis and review

As a final check, reconcile the data and assumptions across the four topics rather than treating them as isolated calculations. Prepare a source table showing which information is a measurement, which comes from a model and which expresses a policy decision. Compare what would happen at daily, seasonal and multiannual time scales. Identify one conflict between upstream and downstream objectives and one environmental consequence of the most attractive numerical alternative. Record the monitoring action that would most improve the evidence base. A good water-resources engineer must know what the calculations leave unresolved.
