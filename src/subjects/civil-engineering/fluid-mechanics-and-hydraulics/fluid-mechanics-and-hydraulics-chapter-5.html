§5.2 Conservation principles and engineering judgment

Conservation principles and engineering judgment

Mass, momentum and mechanical energy are the principal organizing principles of this unit. Their apparent simplicity hides the hardest practical question: which losses, forces, storage terms and boundary conditions belong inside the model? In a concrete roadside channel, a visually convincing plot may still be wrong if an outlet is submerged when the model assumes free flow. Start by drawing the geometry, marking the upstream and downstream reference elevations, identifying the expected flow regime and recording the source of every coefficient.

Dimensional checks expose many errors before they become design mistakes. Keep discharge in cubic meters per second when deriving SI power or forces; distinguish kPa from Pa and diameter from radius; label gauge and absolute pressure; and state whether the model uses a Darcy or a Fanning friction factor. Any reported precision should reflect input uncertainty, not the number of digits available in a computer.

An engineering investigation also has an ethical and operational dimension. If a floodplain bypass reach carries contaminated water or serves a public-facing facility, failure is not only an algebraic concern: unexpected overtopping, pressure loss, scour or cavitation can harm people and ecosystems. Use the small models here to discover sensitivities and build intuition. Actual selection, construction or operation requires qualified review, site data and applicable standards.

§5.3 Uniform flow and Manning resistance

Lesson 5.1 — Uniform flow and Manning resistance

Physical basis. Open channels carry a free surface whose position is part of the solution. Manning’s formula relates discharge to wetted area, hydraulic radius, slope and roughness under approximately uniform flow. Hydraulic radius is A/P, not geometric depth except for wide channels.

Representative relation: Q=(1/n)AR^(2/3)*sqrt(S).

Assumptions and failure modes. Roughness must be calibrated to actual channel condition, vegetation and flow depth. A single n can misrepresent compound sections or strongly nonuniform flow.

Investigation objective. Change channel width at constant slope and evaluate velocity and discharge sensitivity.

Why the model matters for an actual project

The decision in a grass-lined drainage swale depends on more than a single value of an unknown. Begin with an explicit sketch and a table listing the fluid state, channel or pipe geometry, the selected datum, boundary elevations and the intended output. Record the difference between a measured input and one chosen for an exploratory calculation. This distinction becomes essential when an optimistic coefficient produces a result that appears to satisfy a design target.

For uniform flow and manning resistance, the equation Q=(1/n)AR^(2/3)*sqrt(S) can be used as a starting point, not a universal law independent of context. First trace every term back to the conservation principle or constitutive relation that supplies it. Next imagine reversing the direction of flow, doubling the characteristic dimension or moving the gauge datum. Some predictions must change in a specific direction; if the spreadsheet or simulation does not respect that monotonicity, stop and inspect the setup.

How to verify the result

For a laboratory hydraulic flume, collect a timed volumetric collection measurement. A good verification uses at least one independently observable quantity, not the same calculation rearranged. Where a coefficient is empirical, report its source and the regime in which it was obtained. If the measured result differs from the model, examine datum errors, flow regime and equipment condition before altering a parameter merely to improve the fit.

Interpretation and limitations

Roughness must be calibrated to actual channel condition, vegetation and flow depth. A single n can misrepresent compound sections or strongly nonuniform flow. In addition, maintenance accessibility may vary with operating season or maintenance. A calculated response should therefore be accompanied by a bounded sensitivity study: change one influential variable, keep the others fixed, document whether the output rises or falls, and identify a physical observation that could falsify the assumed model. Engineers who cannot explain the direction of that change are not ready to use the output for selection.

§5.4 Critical flow and specific energy

Lesson 5.2 — Critical flow and specific energy

Physical basis. Froude number compares stream velocity to gravity-wave propagation speed. Subcritical and supercritical regimes react differently to downstream disturbances. Critical depth in a rectangular section satisfies y_c=(q²/g)^(1/3), where q is discharge per unit width.

Representative relation: Fr=V/sqrt(g*y); y_c=(q²/g)^(1/3).

Assumptions and failure modes. For nonrectangular channels use hydraulic depth A/T in Fr, not necessarily the measured vertical depth. Local constrictions alter the available specific energy.

Investigation objective. Find the two alternative depths corresponding to the same specific energy and explain why a control section may approach critical flow.

§5.5 Gradually varied profiles

Lesson 5.3 — Gradually varied profiles

Physical basis. Gradually varied flow balances bed slope and friction slope against the free-surface acceleration response. The differential equation dy/dx=(S0-Sf)/(1-Fr²) becomes singular near a critical control, so ordinary step methods should not simply march through a jump.

Representative relation: dy/dx=(S0-Sf)/(1-Fr²).

Assumptions and failure modes. Backwater calculation requires a valid boundary condition and flow regime. A sudden hydraulic jump is rapidly varying and uses momentum rather than the standard GVF energy approximation.

§5.6 Hydraulic jumps and energy dissipation

Lesson 5.4 — Hydraulic jumps and energy dissipation

Physical basis. A hydraulic jump converts rapid, shallow supercritical flow into deeper subcritical flow, often with severe turbulence. Momentum is approximately conserved across the short jump; energy is lost. For rectangular sections, sequent depth and energy loss have closed-form estimates.

Representative relation: y2=(y1/2)(sqrt(1+8Fr1²)-1).

Assumptions and failure modes. Sequent-depth relations assume hydrostatic pressure sufficiently far from the roller. Tailwater and channel geometry determine whether a stable jump forms in the desired location.

Investigation objective. Compute downstream depth and energy loss for a stilling basin inlet and assess downstream scour implications.

§5.7 applied investigation

Unit 5 applied investigation

Your group has been assigned a spillway tailrace. Develop a falsifiable hydraulic model, list all inputs in a measurement register, show how mass conservation is checked and identify a second method of checking the primary output. Compare a low-flow, baseline and high-flow condition. Discuss seasonal temperature change and operating schedule as two possible sources of disagreement. Provide a table of calculated values, explain whether results are stable under small input changes, and separate teaching-model conclusions from claims requiring field verification.

Decision memo

Prepare a two-page report containing the question, assumptions, data provenance, dimensional checks, one independent cross-check, sensitivity findings, limitations and a practical next measurement. Report uncertainty without inventing probabilities. Include a safe operating implication and identify exactly which engineering decision is not justified by the simplified model.

Review prompts

Explain why conservation is not equivalent to assuming zero losses. Distinguish an ideal law, an empirical coefficient, an instrument reading and a numerical approximation. Explain how a model can generate a precise value yet still be inappropriate for a culvert, pump, gate or open-channel condition.
