§4.1 Theoretical Framework: Torsion and Shaft Design

Prerequisite knowledge: force equilibrium, SI dimensional analysis, algebra and first-year calculus.

Learning objectives: state a model and its validity range, calculate physically meaningful engineering quantities, reconcile equations with a free-body diagram or deformation compatibility, and identify at least two failure modes excluded by the idealization.

§4.2 Unit context and engineering interpretation

Unit context and engineering interpretation

Torsion arises in drive shafts, fasteners, tools, bridge bearings and structural transfer elements. It is tempting to represent every twisting component by T r/J, but this is rigorously the classical circular-shaft result for linear elastic Saint-Venant torsion. A rectangular section can warp and concentrates stress differently. Thin-walled closed sections transmit shear flow around a cell, while open sections can have very different torsional rigidity. Recognition of the cross-section geometry is therefore the first modeling decision.

Circular-shaft torsional stiffness depends on the polar second moment, which is exceptionally sensitive to diameter. Designers sometimes enlarge a shaft to control rotation rather than peak shear stress. In a stepped shaft, the largest twist may originate in a long thin segment even when another segment carries the peak stress. A coupling or gear introduces local bearing and bending; simply adding a safety factor to nominal T r/J cannot replace a local connection model. The designer should distinguish global torque transmission from the local torque-transfer mechanism.

Power transmission introduces time. Constant mechanical power at a lower angular speed requires higher torque. A rotating shaft may repeatedly experience tension and compression at its outer surface under a stationary transverse load, creating a fatigue scenario absent from static torsional strength. Start-stop cycles, reversal and misalignment complicate the load spectrum. The models in this unit let students control one variable at a time, then identify the omissions in a real coupled drivetrain.

§4.3 Circular-shaft torsion in elasticity

Lesson 4.1 — Circular-shaft torsion in elasticity

Physical mechanism and governing idea

A circular shaft under Saint-Venant torsion develops shear stress that increases linearly with radius in the classical elastic solution. The polar second moment J describes geometric resistance. Torque-angle relationships contain G and J, while stress uses T r/J. These results require a sufficiently circular uniform shaft away from strongly disturbed ends.

Governing relationship

tau(r)=T r/J; theta=T L/(GJ)

Reading the equation: Start from a properly oriented free body or a measured constitutive response, identify the force or displacement controlled externally, and decide which geometric quantity belongs in the denominator. Check units before substituting numbers, and compare the sign of the result with the physical action.

Worked interpretive example

For a solid circular shaft J=pi d^4/32. Doubling diameter increases J sixteenfold.

What the simplified formula misses

Applying the circular-shaft equation to a thin rectangular bar is invalid; noncircular torsion produces warping and a different stress field.

§4.4 Hollow shafts and torque transmission

Lesson 4.2 — Hollow shafts and torque transmission

Physical mechanism and governing idea

Removing material near the center of a circular shaft can reduce mass with a smaller penalty in torsional stiffness than removing outer material. For concentric hollow circles, J=pi(do^4-di^4)/32. The maximum shear stress lies at the outer radius. Compare stress, twist, buckling and connection demands, not just mass efficiency.

Governing relationship

J = pi (do^4-di^4)/32; tau_max=T do/(2J)

Reading the equation: Start from a properly oriented free body or a measured constitutive response, identify the force or displacement controlled externally, and decide which geometric quantity belongs in the denominator. Check units before substituting numbers, and compare the sign of the result with the physical action.

Worked interpretive example

A 60 mm outer diameter and 40 mm inner diameter hollow shaft has a finite J controlled strongly by its outer diameter.

What the simplified formula misses

Thin-walled shafts can buckle locally, and torque transfer at keys or splines creates stress concentrations not captured by uniform shaft theory.

§4.5 Power, speed and torque

Lesson 4.3 — Power, speed and torque

Physical mechanism and governing idea

A motor shaft transports power as angular speed multiplied by torque in consistent SI units. At steady operating speed, T=P/omega. Starting and stopping events can produce torques exceeding the nominal rating. Shaft design must also account for bending from bearings and gears and fatigue from rotating load cycles.

Governing relationship

P=T omega; omega = 2 pi n/60

Reading the equation: Start from a properly oriented free body or a measured constitutive response, identify the force or displacement controlled externally, and decide which geometric quantity belongs in the denominator. Check units before substituting numbers, and compare the sign of the result with the physical action.

Worked interpretive example

A machine delivering 5 kW at 1500 rpm has a nominal torque of about 31.8 N m.

What the simplified formula misses

Rated power is not a complete load spectrum. Start-up, stall, vibration and impact factors must be established for engineering design.

§4.6 Angle of twist and compatibility

Lesson 4.4 — Angle of twist and compatibility

Physical mechanism and governing idea

A fixed shaft with stepped segments behaves like elastic torsional springs in series when torque is constant. The total twist is the sum T_i L_i/(G_i J_i). A parallel torque path, in contrast, distributes torque according to torsional stiffness when the shafts rotate through the same angle.

Governing relationship

theta_total = sum(T_i L_i/(G_i J_i))

Reading the equation: Start from a properly oriented free body or a measured constitutive response, identify the force or displacement controlled externally, and decide which geometric quantity belongs in the denominator. Check units before substituting numbers, and compare the sign of the result with the physical action.

Worked interpretive example

Two equal segments in series, one half as stiff as the other, do not share equal twist: the softer segment twists twice as much under the same torque.

What the simplified formula misses

An assembly with non-circular cross sections, splines or compliant couplings may need a more detailed model.

§4.8 Engineering case study: Machine drive shaft with coupling offset

Engineering case study: Machine drive shaft with coupling offset

A coupling transmits rated torque between a motor and a gearbox. The maintenance log shows vibration and bearing replacements. Nominal torsion equations give twist and shear for an ideal shaft, but misalignment adds cyclic bending and can shift fatigue hot spots to shoulders or keyways. In a shaft made of multiple diameters, peak torsion stress and maximum total angle of twist need not occur in the same segment.

Deliverable: Draw separate free bodies for torsion and transverse forces. List torque spectrum, shaft dimensions, connection details, bearing positions, alignment measurements and surface quality before discussing a practical retrofit.

Case-study discussion

Start by differentiating data from assumptions: measured loads and geometry should be separated from guessed restraints and material properties. Build one simple calculation whose result can be checked independently. List at least three mechanisms by which the idealization could fail, and prioritize them by whether they would change the engineering conclusion. Translate the numerical demand into a practical question about safety, serviceability, testing or maintenance. Keep a clear audit trail so a reviewer can follow the unit conversions, load path and model limitations.

§4.9 Calculation verification

Calculation verification

  • Recompute J directly from diameter rather than rounding a tabulated value too early.
  • If torque doubles while diameter and G are fixed, both shear and twist should double in the linear model.
  • Check positivity, zero-load behavior, symmetry, and asymptotic scaling whenever applicable.
  • Do not use a graph or slider response as the only evidence that an equation is correct.

Engineering case study: Machine drive shaft with coupling offset

A coupling transmits rated torque between a motor and a gearbox. The maintenance log shows vibration and bearing replacements. Nominal torsion equations give twist and shear for an ideal shaft, but misalignment adds cyclic bending and can shift fatigue hot spots to shoulders or keyways. In a shaft made of multiple diameters, peak torsion stress and maximum total angle of twist need not occur in the same segment.

Deliverable: Draw separate free bodies for torsion and transverse forces. List torque spectrum, shaft dimensions, connection details, bearing positions, alignment measurements and surface quality before discussing a practical retrofit.

Case-study discussion

Start by differentiating data from assumptions: measured loads and geometry should be separated from guessed restraints and material properties. Build one simple calculation whose result can be checked independently. List at least three mechanisms by which the idealization could fail, and prioritize them by whether they would change the engineering conclusion. Translate the numerical demand into a practical question about safety, serviceability, testing or maintenance. Keep a clear audit trail so a reviewer can follow the unit conversions, load path and model limitations.

Calculation verification

  • Recompute J directly from diameter rather than rounding a tabulated value too early.
  • If torque doubles while diameter and G are fixed, both shear and twist should double in the linear model.
  • Check positivity, zero-load behavior, symmetry, and asymptotic scaling whenever applicable.
  • Do not use a graph or slider response as the only evidence that an equation is correct.
