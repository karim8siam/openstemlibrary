§6.2 Unit context and engineering interpretation

Unit context and engineering interpretation

Beam stress and beam deflection are both governed by section stiffness, yet they answer different design questions. Flexural stress uses the section modulus I/c; deflection uses the full flexural rigidity EI. Changing beam depth alters I approximately with depth cubed in a rectangular section. The outer fiber stress therefore scales with depth squared under constant bending moment and constant width, whereas deflection changes more sharply. This explains why a modest depth increase can be valuable for long spans.

The famous elastic beam formulas rest on assumptions. Plane sections are assumed to remain plane and perpendicular to a small-deflection neutral axis, and shear deformation is omitted. These assumptions work best for long slender beams where bending is dominant. A deep timber beam, a sandwich panel with a soft core or a short composite member may require Timoshenko beam theory or a continuum model. Likewise, near a point load, a bearing plate creates three-dimensional contact stresses outside one-dimensional beam theory.

Use an independent approximate check whenever possible. For example, under a central point load, a simply supported beam has a triangular moment diagram. Integrating the curvature function twice with zero end deflections yields the familiar P L³/(48EI) midspan result. Under a uniform full-span load, the maximum becomes 5 w L⁴/(384EI). Both carry deflection units; both grow rapidly with span. A simulation must reproduce zero displacement at its ideal pin and roller, even when the line is graphically exaggerated for visibility.

§6.3 Bending stress and neutral axis

Lesson 6.1 — Bending stress and neutral axis

Physical mechanism and governing idea

Under classical elastic bending of a prismatic homogeneous beam, plane sections remain plane and longitudinal stress varies linearly through depth. For bending about a principal centroidal axis, sigma=-M y/I. The most highly stressed fibers lie far from the neutral axis. This is a local beam-section approximation, not a full stress analysis at a notch.

Governing relationship

sigma_x = -M y/I; S=I/c; sigma_max=|M|/S

Reading the equation: Start from a properly oriented free body or a measured constitutive response, identify the force or displacement controlled externally, and decide which geometric quantity belongs in the denominator. Check units before substituting numbers, and compare the sign of the result with the physical action.

Worked interpretive example

A section with twice the elastic section modulus carries half the bending stress under the same moment.

What the simplified formula misses

At large curvature, near abrupt supports, or in cracked composite sections, the elastic transformed-section assumptions need re-evaluation.

§6.4 Transverse shear through beam sections

Lesson 6.2 — Transverse shear through beam sections

Physical mechanism and governing idea

Shear stress is generally nonuniform across a beam section. The engineering VQ/(I b) formula connects transverse shear resultant to first area moment Q and local width b. In a rectangular section, shear peaks at the neutral axis and reaches zero at its top and bottom surfaces in the textbook idealization.

Governing relationship

tau = VQ/(I b); rectangular tau_max=1.5 V/A

Reading the equation: Start from a properly oriented free body or a measured constitutive response, identify the force or displacement controlled externally, and decide which geometric quantity belongs in the denominator. Check units before substituting numbers, and compare the sign of the result with the physical action.

Worked interpretive example

A rectangular beam carrying 10 kN shear over 5000 mm² area has average 2 MPa and ideal maximum about 3 MPa.

What the simplified formula misses

This approximation is not appropriate at all corners and supports, or when transverse shear deformation strongly affects the member.

§6.5 Deflection from curvature

Lesson 6.3 — Deflection from curvature

Physical mechanism and governing idea

Small-deflection Euler-Bernoulli theory approximates curvature by y double prime and relates it to M/EI with an adopted sign convention. Integrating curvature twice introduces constants set by support displacement and rotation conditions. The shape of the bending moment function determines the deflection profile.

Governing relationship

E I y(x)=M(x) with a consistent signed coordinate convention

Reading the equation: Start from a properly oriented free body or a measured constitutive response, identify the force or displacement controlled externally, and decide which geometric quantity belongs in the denominator. Check units before substituting numbers, and compare the sign of the result with the physical action.

Worked interpretive example

For a midspan point load on a simple beam, maximum downward deflection magnitude is P L³/(48EI).

What the simplified formula misses

The formula neglects shear deflection and geometric nonlinearity, so it becomes less accurate for deep, soft-shear, or strongly deflected beams.

§6.6 Serviceability and stiffness selection

Lesson 6.4 — Serviceability and stiffness selection

Physical mechanism and governing idea

Strength and serviceability are independent questions. A beam may meet bending stress limits but deflect excessively for partitions, finishes or equipment. Span has a cubic influence on point-load deflection and a fourth-power influence on uniform-load deflection. Support conditions and composite action also matter significantly.

Governing relationship

delta_max(point central)=P L³/(48EI); delta_max(UDL)=5wL⁴/(384EI)

Reading the equation: Start from a properly oriented free body or a measured constitutive response, identify the force or displacement controlled externally, and decide which geometric quantity belongs in the denominator. Check units before substituting numbers, and compare the sign of the result with the physical action.

Worked interpretive example

Doubling the span while preserving EI and central load increases elastic deflection eightfold.

What the simplified formula misses

Allowable deflection depends on application and design standard. A single span/360 limit is not universal and should not be imposed without context.

§6.8 Engineering case study: Deflection-sensitive laboratory equipment platform

Engineering case study: Deflection-sensitive laboratory equipment platform

A steel supporting beam meets an initial bending stress target, yet an optical instrument suffers vibration and alignment errors. Increasing section modulus can help strength; increasing EI helps static deflection. Natural frequencies and dynamic excitation require an additional dynamics model. Floor finishes, partition details and instrument requirements may impose serviceability limits stricter than generic rule-of-thumb span limits.

Deliverable: Compute static midpoint deflection for point and distributed loads, compare different member depths, and identify a suitable measured acceptance criterion. Explain why elastic static deflection alone cannot establish satisfactory vibration performance.

Case-study discussion

Start by differentiating data from assumptions: measured loads and geometry should be separated from guessed restraints and material properties. Build one simple calculation whose result can be checked independently. List at least three mechanisms by which the idealization could fail, and prioritize them by whether they would change the engineering conclusion. Translate the numerical demand into a practical question about safety, serviceability, testing or maintenance. Keep a clear audit trail so a reviewer can follow the unit conversions, load path and model limitations.

§6.9 Calculation verification

Calculation verification

  • Confirm that elastic deflection is zero at x=0 and x=L for ideal simple supports.
  • At fixed EI, test point load scaling with L cubed and uniform load scaling with L to the fourth power.
  • Check positivity, zero-load behavior, symmetry, and asymptotic scaling whenever applicable.
  • Do not use a graph or slider response as the only evidence that an equation is correct.
