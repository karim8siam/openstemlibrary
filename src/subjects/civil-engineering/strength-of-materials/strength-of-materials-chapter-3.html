§3.1 Theoretical Framework: Constitutive Response and Material Testing

Prerequisite knowledge: force equilibrium, SI dimensional analysis, algebra and first-year calculus.

Learning objectives: state a model and its validity range, calculate physically meaningful engineering quantities, reconcile equations with a free-body diagram or deformation compatibility, and identify at least two failure modes excluded by the idealization.

§3.2 Unit context and engineering interpretation

Unit context and engineering interpretation

Materials data require careful interpretation. Elastic modulus is a slope fitted over a strain interval, not simply the largest reported stress divided by elongation at fracture. Offset yield strength is defined using a prescribed offset strain and a measured elastic slope. Ultimate tensile engineering stress is the maximum test force divided by original specimen area, and it can differ greatly from true stress in the necked region. Percent elongation measured after fracture depends on gage length and specimen geometry, so it should not be compared blindly across standards.

A Poisson ratio describes how an initially uniaxially stressed isotropic sample contracts or expands transversely. Under a fully constrained plane-strain condition, the same elastic modulus and Poisson ratio produce a different relationship between applied stress and deformation. This is why a concrete pier confined by surrounding soil, an elastomer bonded to rigid plates, and an unconstrained tensile coupon cannot be modeled by one scalar spring stiffness without checking the state of stress. Anisotropic composites demand directional elastic constants instead of one E and nu.

Plastic strain is path dependent. In an ideal bilinear material the first loading branch is linear, and subsequent loading follows a reduced tangent stiffness. Unloading commonly follows a steeper elastic slope. The crossing of the unloaded line with the zero-stress axis illustrates residual strain. This simplified graph is valuable for seeing how permanent deformation develops, but it cannot predict damage, low-cycle fatigue, reverse yielding or rate-dependent creep. Appropriate model complexity follows the engineering question.

§3.3 Tensile testing and engineering curves

Lesson 3.1 — Tensile testing and engineering curves

Physical mechanism and governing idea

A tensile test measures force and extensometer displacement over a defined gage length. Engineering stress divides load by original area; engineering strain divides gage extension by original length. Elastic slope, offset proof strength, ultimate strength and fracture elongation answer different questions. A testing machine reports a curve only after calibration and careful specimen preparation.

Governing relationship

sigma_eng = F/A0; epsilon_eng = delta/L0

Reading the equation: Start from a properly oriented free body or a measured constitutive response, identify the force or displacement controlled externally, and decide which geometric quantity belongs in the denominator. Check units before substituting numbers, and compare the sign of the result with the physical action.

Worked interpretive example

A 20 kN test force acting on a 100 mm² original section means 200 MPa engineering stress.

What the simplified formula misses

Engineering stress differs from true stress when the area changes significantly. Post-necking curve interpretation needs more than a nominal conversion.

§3.4 Poisson ratio and multiaxial elastic response

Lesson 3.2 — Poisson ratio and multiaxial elastic response

Physical mechanism and governing idea

An axially stretched isotropic linear elastic specimen generally contracts laterally. The Poisson ratio relates transverse strain to axial strain under uniaxial stress. In three-dimensional elasticity, strain depends on all three normal stresses. Material properties must be assessed for the appropriate direction in composites and other anisotropic solids.

Governing relationship

epsilon_trans = -nu epsilon_axial; epsilon_x = (sigma_x - nu(sigma_y + sigma_z))/E

Reading the equation: Start from a properly oriented free body or a measured constitutive response, identify the force or displacement controlled externally, and decide which geometric quantity belongs in the denominator. Check units before substituting numbers, and compare the sign of the result with the physical action.

Worked interpretive example

An axial strain of 900 microstrain and nu=0.30 predicts a transverse strain of -270 microstrain in uniaxial linear elasticity.

What the simplified formula misses

The Poisson formula alone is not sufficient for a plane-strain constraint or a layered material that responds differently in different directions.

§3.5 Yield, plasticity and unloading

Lesson 3.3 — Yield, plasticity and unloading

Physical mechanism and governing idea

When load increases beyond initial linearity, permanent strain can develop. A bilinear tangent model idealizes strain hardening by assigning a second lower slope after the yield point. Unloading from plastic strain often occurs approximately along the elastic modulus, leaving a residual strain. That is why a bar can return to zero load without returning to its original length.

Governing relationship

Elastic: sigma=E epsilon; ideal bilinear after yield: sigma=fy+Et(epsilon-fy/E)

Reading the equation: Start from a properly oriented free body or a measured constitutive response, identify the force or displacement controlled externally, and decide which geometric quantity belongs in the denominator. Check units before substituting numbers, and compare the sign of the result with the physical action.

Worked interpretive example

For E=200 GPa and fy=250 MPa, the elastic yield strain is 0.00125.

What the simplified formula misses

A teaching bilinear model is not a calibrated constitutive law. Cyclic loading, Bauschinger effects and localization are omitted.

§3.6 Temperature, strain rate and property scatter

Lesson 3.4 — Temperature, strain rate and property scatter

Physical mechanism and governing idea

A strength value comes from a test population under specified conditions. Temperature, loading rate, specimen orientation, defects, processing route and manufacturing history may change measured strength and ductility. Designers need characteristic or specified properties appropriate to their code and product form, not the single highest laboratory result.

Governing relationship

Use a documented property distribution and the applicable design standard

Reading the equation: Start from a properly oriented free body or a measured constitutive response, identify the force or displacement controlled externally, and decide which geometric quantity belongs in the denominator. Check units before substituting numbers, and compare the sign of the result with the physical action.

Worked interpretive example

If two specimens yield at 242 and 258 MPa, reporting the average as a guaranteed minimum would be an unsupported inference.

What the simplified formula misses

The workbook simulations use illustrative materials. They are not material certification data or replacements for manufacturer design allowables.

§3.8 Engineering case study: Material substitution for a small lifting bracket

Engineering case study: Material substitution for a small lifting bracket

A project team considers replacing a steel component with aluminum to reduce mass. The two candidate materials may have different yield strengths, moduli, density and fatigue behavior. Equal dimensions do not imply equal stiffness or equal safety. If applied load is prescribed, elastic extension scales inversely with E; if assembly displacement is prescribed, reactions can increase with stiffness.

Deliverable: Create a decision matrix separating stiffness, strength, deformation limit, corrosion exposure, joining, cost and standard-compliant inspection. Do not assign a single winner until required limits and the actual load control method are known.

Case-study discussion

Start by differentiating data from assumptions: measured loads and geometry should be separated from guessed restraints and material properties. Build one simple calculation whose result can be checked independently. List at least three mechanisms by which the idealization could fail, and prioritize them by whether they would change the engineering conclusion. Translate the numerical demand into a practical question about safety, serviceability, testing or maintenance. Keep a clear audit trail so a reviewer can follow the unit conversions, load path and model limitations.

§3.9 Calculation verification

Calculation verification

  • Obtain E from the local elastic slope, not from ultimate strength divided by total elongation.
  • On a bilinear model, unload from two distinct peak strains and compare residual strain predictions.
  • Check positivity, zero-load behavior, symmetry, and asymptotic scaling whenever applicable.
  • Do not use a graph or slider response as the only evidence that an equation is correct.
