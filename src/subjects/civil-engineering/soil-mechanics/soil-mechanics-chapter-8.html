Chapter 8 • Theory & Derivations

Geotechnical Field Exploration, Testing and Uncertainty

Comprehensive university textbook unit covering fundamental theory, mathematical formulations, numerical modeling, and code-based design validation of Geotechnical Field Exploration, Testing and Uncertainty. Developed for undergraduate civil engineering study.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Soil Mechanics study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§8.1 Outcomes

Outcomes

After completing this unit the learner should be able to define the governing state variables, calculate representative quantities with correct units, explain when the idealized relation breaks down, interpret the corresponding laboratory models, and defend a field investigation or verification decision.

§8.2 Core lessons

Core lessons

Lesson 8.1 — Planning a ground investigation

Central question. A useful site investigation links engineering decisions to conceptual ground models rather than collecting isolated test results.

Governing relationships and definitions. Specify investigation objectives, strata continuity, groundwater monitoring, uncertainty and potential design consequences. Choose soundings and boreholes to investigate actual load paths and failure mechanisms.

Worked calculation / observation. A building footprint with possible fill over clay demands different investigation coverage from a long highway embankment crossing an alluvial valley. The depth of interest follows influence, not a universal fixed number.

Why interpretation matters. A borehole log records observations made through a particular sampling window. Drawing continuous layer boundaries between widely spaced boreholes is a geological interpretation, not a direct measurement. Show alternative connections where discontinuous lenses, fill pockets or abrupt contacts remain plausible.

Field case: A small site investigation with sparse borehole coverage. In a small site investigation with sparse borehole coverage, the engineer should first sketch the geometry, state the loading or groundwater history, and identify which of the measured quantities are direct observations. Next identify the controlling stress, volume, hydraulic or strength variable from this lesson. A prediction is only as defensible as the link between the laboratory variable and its field counterpart. For example, the stress relevant to settlement may be an effective stress after excess water pressure dissipates, whereas the load relevant to a temporary wall brace may be a short-term total-stress action. Mixing those questions can produce a plausible-looking but physically inconsistent answer.

From observation to a defensible model. A practical investigation has three stages. Observation: identify the features of a small site investigation with sparse borehole coverage that a field crew can actually record, such as layer thickness, water-table history, moisture state or deformation. Interpretation: select the idealized law described above, explain the chosen signs and units, and identify which soil parameters need independent tests. Prediction: evaluate a baseline and at least two credible alternatives. A single baseline is rarely an adequate basis for choosing a foundation treatment, drainage provision or construction method.

Verification practice. A useful checking sequence is dimensional analysis, limiting behavior and independent arithmetic. Insert the stated units before using a formula; a conductivity given in cm/s cannot be entered as m/s unchanged. Ask whether a computed stress or discharge tends toward zero when its driving load is removed. Then compare with a second hand calculation, a plotted numerical response or a bounding case. A graph from an interactive model provides feedback, not evidence that the assumed governing equation is correct for the site.

Reasoning exercise. Draw the physical control volume or stress path for a small site investigation with sparse borehole coverage. Label the measured variables, the inferred variables and the quantities that could change after construction. Which variable would be the cheapest to measure more accurately? Describe how two alternative assumptions could change the outcome in opposite directions. Finally explain which decision would be postponed until a more representative specimen, groundwater observation or construction-stage measurement becomes available.

Model boundary. Log coordinate, datum, ground elevation, drilling method, sampling depth and groundwater reading date. Record refusals and anomalies rather than deleting them. Present the result with an explicit number of significant digits justified by the inputs; do not confuse that numerical precision with geological certainty. Professional design requires the governing standard, site-specific investigation and independent review.

§8.4 Integrated design conversation

Integrated design conversation

Imagine that the site data are incomplete and construction will begin in phases. Construct two defensible interpretations of the subsurface conditions. For each, state the best estimate of the controlling material parameter and a credible low and high value. Explain how a field observation or laboratory test could reject one interpretation. The deliverable is not only a number but a documented chain from field evidence to calculation, checking, and the resulting construction choice.

§8.5 Applied dossier: sparse investigation and a misleading average

Applied dossier: sparse investigation and a misleading average

A proposed light industrial building sits on variable fill over natural clay. Three borings identify fill thicknesses of 1.1, 2.4 and 0.6 m. An average of 1.37 m is easy to compute, but it is not evidence of a uniform layer that is 1.37 m thick beneath every footing. The spatial transitions and origin of the fill are unknown. Before selecting one compressibility or strength parameter, the engineer should plot the borehole locations, encountered materials, sample elevations, groundwater observations and plausible alternative geological sections.

For one tube sample, advance during driving was 0.80 m and recovered length was 0.64 m. Sample recovery ratio is 0.64/0.80×100=80%. With an inside tube diameter of 75 mm and wall thickness 2 mm, the geometric area ratio is [(79²−75²)/75²]×100≈10.95%. These indices are useful descriptions of the sampling operation. Neither number alone proves that undisturbed stress history or fabric has been retained in the tube.

Two oedometer specimens from the same mapped clay unit report quite different compression indexes. Rather than average immediately, compare water content, specimen condition, sampling depth, extrusion notes and tested stress range. A mixed fill, fissured crust and deeper natural clay may have been conflated as one unit. Replicate testing of a critical horizon may be more valuable than a large number of unrelated index tests.

For a sensitivity exercise, assume friction angle measurements are approximately normal with a mean of 32° and standard deviation of 3°, solely for teaching. On a 25° dry cohesionless slope, compute FS=tanφ′/tan25° for each sampled φ′ using a fixed random seed. The resulting histogram or percentile curve tells you what that assumed distribution implies mathematically. It is not an estimate of site failure probability until the assumed statistical model and spatial representativeness have been validated.

A final report should therefore distinguish observed quantities, derived parameters, correlations and judgment. It should list decisions that remain insensitive to uncertainty, decisions that may change and the least expensive additional investigation that resolves each uncertainty. The alternative is false precision: an attractive graph whose narrow confidence band has been invented by unsupported assumptions.

§8.6 Technical methods notebook: traceability and uncertainty

Technical methods notebook: traceability and uncertainty

A field record is a chain of evidence. For each borehole, record coordinates and elevation datum, start and finish date, sampling method, test depths, recovery, weather and temporary or stabilized water readings. An apparent layer boundary in an interpolated profile should be labeled an interpretation between observations, not drawn as a physically measured continuous surface. If a proposed footing crosses a suspect fill-to-clay boundary, a targeted follow-up test may have more value than many additional tests in a well-characterized corner.

Recovery and area ratio. Recovery ratio is recovered sample length divided by advance length and is often reported as a percentage. A recovery over 100% is not impossible in a disturbed sample context, but it flags an inconsistency or special mechanism that should be explained rather than accepted without review. A thin-wall tube geometric area ratio may be defined as (Dout²−Din²)/Din²×100%, provided diameters refer to the cutting geometry being evaluated. Lower ratios generally reduce displacement for an otherwise comparable geometry, but no universal inference about sample quality follows from area ratio alone.

Disturbance is physical, not merely arithmetic. Stress relief during extraction, moisture loss, remolding, vibration, storage and extrusion alter soil structure and measured stress–strain behavior. A sample that is unsuitable for oedometer interpretation may remain useful for moisture or particle-size tests. Specify the data needed before choosing sampling equipment and handling procedures; otherwise tests may be precise but answer the wrong question.

Correlations need context. A field penetration count is affected by energy delivery, equipment, overburden and soil type. It cannot be treated as an intrinsic modulus without procedural corrections and locally relevant correlation evidence. Similar caution applies when predicting hydraulic conductivity from grain size or strength from Atterberg limits. Report estimated values and ranges, and distinguish empirical correlation error from actual subsurface variability.

Deterministic simulations of uncertainty. A fixed-seed Monte Carlo model produces the same sampled numbers for the same input parameters. That is useful for educational reproducibility. Suppose φ′ is assumed to follow a normal distribution with mean 32° and standard deviation 3°, and the slope has β=25°. The simulated spread in tanφ′/tanβ is a transformed spread from the assumed parameter distribution. It does not establish that friction angles are normal at the site, that values are spatially independent, or that the implied proportion below FS=1 estimates real-world landslide frequency.

An uncertainty register. Separate three categories. Measurement uncertainty includes instrument calibration, recovery and test repeatability. Model uncertainty includes simplifications in loading, drainage, stress distribution and failure geometry. Spatial uncertainty includes unobserved lenses and layer changes. A practical mitigation plan identifies which decision depends on which category and chooses follow-up work accordingly. Where outcomes are sensitive to groundwater conditions, a piezometer may outperform a second index test; where outcomes depend on the thickness of weak clay, better stratigraphic coverage may be essential.

Reviewable deliverable. Include a plan of investigation locations, logs, a longitudinal ground profile, groundwater readings by date, a table of chosen parameters with traceable sources, representative calculations, sensitivity plots and unambiguous remaining assumptions. Finish with what additional evidence would change the recommended design approach. The objective is not to remove all uncertainty, which is impossible in earth materials, but to make engineering decisions proportional to the quality and consequences of the available evidence.

Applied dossier: sparse investigation and a misleading average

A proposed light industrial building sits on variable fill over natural clay. Three borings identify fill thicknesses of 1.1, 2.4 and 0.6 m. An average of 1.37 m is easy to compute, but it is not evidence of a uniform layer that is 1.37 m thick beneath every footing. The spatial transitions and origin of the fill are unknown. Before selecting one compressibility or strength parameter, the engineer should plot the borehole locations, encountered materials, sample elevations, groundwater observations and plausible alternative geological sections.

For one tube sample, advance during driving was 0.80 m and recovered length was 0.64 m. Sample recovery ratio is 0.64/0.80×100=80%. With an inside tube diameter of 75 mm and wall thickness 2 mm, the geometric area ratio is [(79²−75²)/75²]×100≈10.95%. These indices are useful descriptions of the sampling operation. Neither number alone proves that undisturbed stress history or fabric has been retained in the tube.

Two oedometer specimens from the same mapped clay unit report quite different compression indexes. Rather than average immediately, compare water content, specimen condition, sampling depth, extrusion notes and tested stress range. A mixed fill, fissured crust and deeper natural clay may have been conflated as one unit. Replicate testing of a critical horizon may be more valuable than a large number of unrelated index tests.

For a sensitivity exercise, assume friction angle measurements are approximately normal with a mean of 32° and standard deviation of 3°, solely for teaching. On a 25° dry cohesionless slope, compute FS=tanφ′/tan25° for each sampled φ′ using a fixed random seed. The resulting histogram or percentile curve tells you what that assumed distribution implies mathematically. It is not an estimate of site failure probability until the assumed statistical model and spatial representativeness have been validated.

A final report should therefore distinguish observed quantities, derived parameters, correlations and judgment. It should list decisions that remain insensitive to uncertainty, decisions that may change and the least expensive additional investigation that resolves each uncertainty. The alternative is false precision: an attractive graph whose narrow confidence band has been invented by unsupported assumptions.

Technical methods notebook: traceability and uncertainty

A field record is a chain of evidence. For each borehole, record coordinates and elevation datum, start and finish date, sampling method, test depths, recovery, weather and temporary or stabilized water readings. An apparent layer boundary in an interpolated profile should be labeled an interpretation between observations, not drawn as a physically measured continuous surface. If a proposed footing crosses a suspect fill-to-clay boundary, a targeted follow-up test may have more value than many additional tests in a well-characterized corner.

Recovery and area ratio. Recovery ratio is recovered sample length divided by advance length and is often reported as a percentage. A recovery over 100% is not impossible in a disturbed sample context, but it flags an inconsistency or special mechanism that should be explained rather than accepted without review. A thin-wall tube geometric area ratio may be defined as (Dout²−Din²)/Din²×100%, provided diameters refer to the cutting geometry being evaluated. Lower ratios generally reduce displacement for an otherwise comparable geometry, but no universal inference about sample quality follows from area ratio alone.

Disturbance is physical, not merely arithmetic. Stress relief during extraction, moisture loss, remolding, vibration, storage and extrusion alter soil structure and measured stress–strain behavior. A sample that is unsuitable for oedometer interpretation may remain useful for moisture or particle-size tests. Specify the data needed before choosing sampling equipment and handling procedures; otherwise tests may be precise but answer the wrong question.

Correlations need context. A field penetration count is affected by energy delivery, equipment, overburden and soil type. It cannot be treated as an intrinsic modulus without procedural corrections and locally relevant correlation evidence. Similar caution applies when predicting hydraulic conductivity from grain size or strength from Atterberg limits. Report estimated values and ranges, and distinguish empirical correlation error from actual subsurface variability.

Deterministic simulations of uncertainty. A fixed-seed Monte Carlo model produces the same sampled numbers for the same input parameters. That is useful for educational reproducibility. Suppose φ′ is assumed to follow a normal distribution with mean 32° and standard deviation 3°, and the slope has β=25°. The simulated spread in tanφ′/tanβ is a transformed spread from the assumed parameter distribution. It does not establish that friction angles are normal at the site, that values are spatially independent, or that the implied proportion below FS=1 estimates real-world landslide frequency.

An uncertainty register. Separate three categories. Measurement uncertainty includes instrument calibration, recovery and test repeatability. Model uncertainty includes simplifications in loading, drainage, stress distribution and failure geometry. Spatial uncertainty includes unobserved lenses and layer changes. A practical mitigation plan identifies which decision depends on which category and chooses follow-up work accordingly. Where outcomes are sensitive to groundwater conditions, a piezometer may outperform a second index test; where outcomes depend on the thickness of weak clay, better stratigraphic coverage may be essential.

Reviewable deliverable. Include a plan of investigation locations, logs, a longitudinal ground profile, groundwater readings by date, a table of chosen parameters with traceable sources, representative calculations, sensitivity plots and unambiguous remaining assumptions. Finish with what additional evidence would change the recommended design approach. The objective is not to remove all uncertainty, which is impossible in earth materials, but to make engineering decisions proportional to the quality and consequences of the available evidence.
