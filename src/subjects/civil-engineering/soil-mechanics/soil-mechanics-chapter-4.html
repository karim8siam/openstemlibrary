Compressibility, Consolidation and Settlement Time Rates

Comprehensive university textbook unit covering fundamental theory, mathematical formulations, numerical modeling, and code-based design validation of Compressibility, Consolidation and Settlement Time Rates. Developed for undergraduate civil engineering study.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Soil Mechanics study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§4.1 Outcomes

Outcomes

After completing this unit the learner should be able to define the governing state variables, calculate representative quantities with correct units, explain when the idealized relation breaks down, interpret the corresponding laboratory models, and defend a field investigation or verification decision.

§4.2 Core lessons

Core lessons

Lesson 4.1 — One-dimensional compression and the e–log stress curve

Central question. Normally consolidated clays show a steeper virgin compression slope than the recompression slope observed before yield.

Governing relationships and definitions. For 1D logarithmic stress model Δe=Cc log10(σ′f/σ′0), settlement S=H Δe/(1+e0). If the path crosses preconsolidation, split it into Cr and Cc contributions.

Worked calculation / observation. With H=4 m, e0=0.9, Cc=0.25, σ′0=80 kPa and σ′f=160 kPa, S=4×0.25×log10(2)/1.9=0.158 m.

Why interpretation matters. Consolidation settlement and consolidation rate answer different questions. The compression index and stress path govern the eventual primary settlement in the model, while the drainage path and consolidation coefficient govern how rapidly it appears. Changing a rate parameter alone should not change the final settlement for the same assumed loading and compression properties.

Field case: A new tank founded on a compressible clay layer. In a new tank founded on a compressible clay layer, the engineer should first sketch the geometry, state the loading or groundwater history, and identify which of the measured quantities are direct observations. Next identify the controlling stress, volume, hydraulic or strength variable from this lesson. A prediction is only as defensible as the link between the laboratory variable and its field counterpart. For example, the stress relevant to settlement may be an effective stress after excess water pressure dissipates, whereas the load relevant to a temporary wall brace may be a short-term total-stress action. Mixing those questions can produce a plausible-looking but physically inconsistent answer.

From observation to a defensible model. A practical investigation has three stages. Observation: identify the features of a new tank founded on a compressible clay layer that a field crew can actually record, such as layer thickness, water-table history, moisture state or deformation. Interpretation: select the idealized law described above, explain the chosen signs and units, and identify which soil parameters need independent tests. Prediction: evaluate a baseline and at least two credible alternatives. A single baseline is rarely an adequate basis for choosing a foundation treatment, drainage provision or construction method.

Verification practice. A useful checking sequence is dimensional analysis, limiting behavior and independent arithmetic. Insert the stated units before using a formula; a conductivity given in cm/s cannot be entered as m/s unchanged. Ask whether a computed stress or discharge tends toward zero when its driving load is removed. Then compare with a second hand calculation, a plotted numerical response or a bounding case. A graph from an interactive model provides feedback, not evidence that the assumed governing equation is correct for the site.

Reasoning exercise. Draw the physical control volume or stress path for a new tank founded on a compressible clay layer. Label the measured variables, the inferred variables and the quantities that could change after construction. Which variable would be the cheapest to measure more accurately? Describe how two alternative assumptions could change the outcome in opposite directions. Finally explain which decision would be postponed until a more representative specimen, groundwater observation or construction-stage measurement becomes available.

Model boundary. This is an idealized primary consolidation estimate based on a representative laboratory path. Layering, three-dimensional stress bulbs and immediate settlements require separate assessment. Present the result with an explicit number of significant digits justified by the inputs; do not confuse that numerical precision with geological certainty. Lesson 4.2 — Preconsolidation and loading history

Central question. Maximum past effective stress influences whether new loads primarily cause recompression or virgin compression.

Governing relationships and definitions. OCR=σ′p/σ′0. If σ′f≤σ′p, use Cr; if σ′f>σ′p, split Δe=Cr log10(σ′p/σ′0)+Cc log10(σ′f/σ′p).

Worked calculation / observation. For σ′0=70, σ′p=140, σ′f=210 kPa, Cr=0.05 and Cc=0.28: Δe≈0.05 log10 2 + 0.28 log10 1.5 =0.0643. With H=3 m, e0=0.85, settlement≈0.104 m.

Why interpretation matters. The familiar e–log stress plot is often drawn as straight segments to interpret oedometer data. Real data are curved, and the transition near preconsolidation pressure may be gradual. In a field settlement prediction it is better to provide a range based on plausible interpretation than present a sharp yield stress as a physically exact threshold.

Lesson 4.3 — Rate of primary consolidation

Central question. Pore pressure dissipates with drainage, so settlement develops in time even after construction loading is complete.

Governing relationships and definitions. Time factor Tv=cv t/Hdr². For the ideal one-dimensional uniform initial excess pore pressure model, mean degree of consolidation U=1−(8/π²)Σ[exp(−(2m+1)²π²Tv/4)/(2m+1)²].

Worked calculation / observation. With cv=2×10⁻⁷ m²/s and a drainage length Hdr=2 m, at t=1 year Tv≈1.58. The ideal series predicts U close to 0.98; drainage assumptions control this result.

Why interpretation matters. The drainage length is not always equal to the layer thickness. If both top and bottom are draining, the longest path for the ideal symmetric model is half the thickness; with one impermeable boundary, it is the full thickness. That difference becomes squared in the time-factor relation, which is why boundary conditions often dominate timing predictions.

Lesson 4.4 — Secondary compression and monitoring

Central question. Creep may continue after most excess pore pressure dissipates and is especially important in organic deposits and some soft clays.

Governing relationships and definitions. One simplified secondary settlement relation is Sα=H Cα/(1+ep) log10(t2/t1), with consistent definition of the coefficient and time origin.

Worked calculation / observation. Take H=3 m, Cα=0.015, ep=0.75 and t2/t1=10: Sα≈25.7 mm. The observation period and coefficient definition matter as much as the arithmetic.

§4.5 Applied dossier: staged loading of a soft clay layer

Applied dossier: staged loading of a soft clay layer

A new storage facility is proposed above 5 m of compressible clay. The initial average effective vertical stress in the layer is estimated as 60 kPa, the preconsolidation pressure as 110 kPa and the post-construction stress as 180 kPa. Laboratory interpretation gives e0=0.95, Cr=0.05 and Cc=0.30. Because the new stress exceeds the preconsolidation breakpoint, calculate the void ratio decrease in two steps. Recompression contributes 0.05 log10(110/60)≈0.0132, and virgin compression adds 0.30 log10(180/110)≈0.0642. The combined Δe≈0.0774; the ideal one-dimensional primary settlement estimate is 5×0.0774/(1+0.95)≈0.198 m.

This number should be shown as a model estimate, not a precise prediction of footing movement. The stress increase is unlikely to be constant over the clay thickness, stiffness may vary with depth, and the laboratory sample may be disturbed. A layered calculation would integrate settlement over sublayers using depth-dependent stress increments and initial effective stresses. Differential movement also depends on foundation geometry and lateral variability.

Assume drainage can occur at both the upper and lower boundaries of the 5 m layer, giving a 2.5 m drainage path. With cv=1.5×10⁻⁷ m²/s, one year produces Tv≈(1.5×10⁻⁷×31,536,000)/(2.5²)≈0.757. The standard idealized Terzaghi series then predicts a substantial fraction of primary consolidation by that time. If the base turns out to be impervious, the drainage length doubles and the same Tv occurs after four times as much elapsed time. This quadratic sensitivity deserves more attention than minor rounding in settlement.

A staged construction proposal might include settlement plates, extensometers and piezometers. Hold points should be linked to monitored dissipation and observed movements, not solely a predicted number of days. The instrumentation record can also reveal that an assumed sand drainage boundary is actually discontinuous. This is a case where the observation plan is part of the engineering model and can change the schedule before a limit is exceeded.

§4.6 Technical methods notebook: settlement amount and settlement timing

Technical methods notebook: settlement amount and settlement timing

Compression is a stress-path calculation. A normally consolidated idealized oedometer line gives Δe=Cc log10(σ′f/σ′0). For a soil with overconsolidated initial state, the total compression may combine recompression and virgin compression. When σ′0<σ′p<σ′f, use Δe=Cr log10(σ′p/σ′0)+Cc log10(σ′f/σ′p). Divide Δe by 1+e0 and multiply by the original layer thickness to estimate one-dimensional settlement. The calculation assumes the laboratory index is representative of the in-situ layer and that the stress increment is suitably assigned to the layer.

Depthwise integration. A footing-induced stress change varies with depth. For a clay layer split into three sublayers, compute initial vertical effective stress and a representative stress increase at each sublayer midpoint. The total predicted settlement is the sum of Hi Δei/(1+e0,i). This is a conceptual improvement on treating every part of a thick stratum with one averaged stress increment; it still omits lateral deformation and nonuniform drainage that require more comprehensive analysis.

Rate from dissipation. Under ideal Terzaghi conditions, Tv=cv t/Hdr²; mean degree of consolidation can be evaluated by a rapidly convergent Fourier series. The coefficient cv has units of length squared per time. If it is quoted in m²/year in a laboratory report but substituted as m²/s, time predictions may be wrong by over seven orders of magnitude. Before plotting, convert every parameter to coherent units. At very small time factor, early consolidation rises approximately with the square root of Tv under the model. At large Tv the leading exponential term dominates the remaining excess-pressure fraction.

Drainage boundaries matter. A 6 m clay layer with drainage top and bottom has nominal Hdr=3 m. If its base is impervious and only the upper boundary drains, Hdr=6 m. To reach the same Tv at unchanged cv, the single-drainage layer takes four times longer. That time ratio follows from the square of drainage length; it is independent of any particular chosen settlement magnitude. This is why the continuity of a sand lens can be more important than a minor change in the laboratory compression index when choosing a construction schedule.

How monitoring informs a model. A settlement plate records integrated vertical movement but cannot independently tell whether an assumed consolidation coefficient or stress-increment profile is correct. Piezometers measure pressure decay in selected horizons; multiple instruments can help distinguish incorrect drainage assumptions from incorrect compressibility assumptions. A staged embankment program should compare observed settlement with predicted envelopes and define what observed rate or excess pore-pressure condition would permit further loading.

Worked sensitivity. For Hdr=2 m, cv=2×10⁻⁷ m²/s and t=365 days, Tv≈1.5768. If cv halves, Tv halves. If the drainage length doubles, Tv becomes one-quarter as large. Create a table showing how mean consolidation changes under both assumptions and label which change is a material-property uncertainty and which is a boundary-condition uncertainty. Conclude by identifying which field test would address each uncertainty.

Prediction: evaluate a baseline and at least two credible alternatives. A single baseline is rarely an adequate basis for choosing a foundation treatment, drainage provision or construction method.

Verification practice. A useful checking sequence is dimensional analysis, limiting behavior and independent arithmetic. Insert the stated units before using a formula; a conductivity given in cm/s cannot be entered as m/s unchanged. Ask whether a computed stress or discharge tends toward zero when its driving load is removed. Then compare with a second hand calculation, a plotted numerical response or a bounding case. A graph from an interactive model provides feedback, not evidence that the assumed governing equation is correct for the site.

Model boundary. Do not double-count primary and secondary settlement from unrelated laboratory intervals. Field settlement plates and pore-pressure instruments help calibrate predictions. Present the result with an explicit number of significant digits justified by the inputs; do not confuse that numerical precision with geological certainty. Professional design requires the governing standard, site-specific investigation and independent review.

Applied dossier: staged loading of a soft clay layer

A new storage facility is proposed above 5 m of compressible clay. The initial average effective vertical stress in the layer is estimated as 60 kPa, the preconsolidation pressure as 110 kPa and the post-construction stress as 180 kPa. Laboratory interpretation gives e0=0.95, Cr=0.05 and Cc=0.30. Because the new stress exceeds the preconsolidation breakpoint, calculate the void ratio decrease in two steps. Recompression contributes 0.05 log10(110/60)≈0.0132, and virgin compression adds 0.30 log10(180/110)≈0.0642. The combined Δe≈0.0774; the ideal one-dimensional primary settlement estimate is 5×0.0774/(1+0.95)≈0.198 m.

This number should be shown as a model estimate, not a precise prediction of footing movement. The stress increase is unlikely to be constant over the clay thickness, stiffness may vary with depth, and the laboratory sample may be disturbed. A layered calculation would integrate settlement over sublayers using depth-dependent stress increments and initial effective stresses. Differential movement also depends on foundation geometry and lateral variability.

Assume drainage can occur at both the upper and lower boundaries of the 5 m layer, giving a 2.5 m drainage path. With cv=1.5×10⁻⁷ m²/s, one year produces Tv≈(1.5×10⁻⁷×31,536,000)/(2.5²)≈0.757. The standard idealized Terzaghi series then predicts a substantial fraction of primary consolidation by that time. If the base turns out to be impervious, the drainage length doubles and the same Tv occurs after four times as much elapsed time. This quadratic sensitivity deserves more attention than minor rounding in settlement.

A staged construction proposal might include settlement plates, extensometers and piezometers. Hold points should be linked to monitored dissipation and observed movements, not solely a predicted number of days. The instrumentation record can also reveal that an assumed sand drainage boundary is actually discontinuous. This is a case where the observation plan is part of the engineering model and can change the schedule before a limit is exceeded.

Technical methods notebook: settlement amount and settlement timing

Compression is a stress-path calculation. A normally consolidated idealized oedometer line gives Δe=Cc log10(σ′f/σ′0). For a soil with overconsolidated initial state, the total compression may combine recompression and virgin compression. When σ′0<σ′p<σ′f, use Δe=Cr log10(σ′p/σ′0)+Cc log10(σ′f/σ′p). Divide Δe by 1+e0 and multiply by the original layer thickness to estimate one-dimensional settlement. The calculation assumes the laboratory index is representative of the in-situ layer and that the stress increment is suitably assigned to the layer.

Depthwise integration. A footing-induced stress change varies with depth. For a clay layer split into three sublayers, compute initial vertical effective stress and a representative stress increase at each sublayer midpoint. The total predicted settlement is the sum of Hi Δei/(1+e0,i). This is a conceptual improvement on treating every part of a thick stratum with one averaged stress increment; it still omits lateral deformation and nonuniform drainage that require more comprehensive analysis.

Rate from dissipation. Under ideal Terzaghi conditions, Tv=cv t/Hdr²; mean degree of consolidation can be evaluated by a rapidly convergent Fourier series. The coefficient cv has units of length squared per time. If it is quoted in m²/year in a laboratory report but substituted as m²/s, time predictions may be wrong by over seven orders of magnitude. Before plotting, convert every parameter to coherent units. At very small time factor, early consolidation rises approximately with the square root of Tv under the model. At large Tv the leading exponential term dominates the remaining excess-pressure fraction.

Drainage boundaries matter. A 6 m clay layer with drainage top and bottom has nominal Hdr=3 m. If its base is impervious and only the upper boundary drains, Hdr=6 m. To reach the same Tv at unchanged cv, the single-drainage layer takes four times longer. That time ratio follows from the square of drainage length; it is independent of any particular chosen settlement magnitude. This is why the continuity of a sand lens can be more important than a minor change in the laboratory compression index when choosing a construction schedule.

How monitoring informs a model. A settlement plate records integrated vertical movement but cannot independently tell whether an assumed consolidation coefficient or stress-increment profile is correct. Piezometers measure pressure decay in selected horizons; multiple instruments can help distinguish incorrect drainage assumptions from incorrect compressibility assumptions. A staged embankment program should compare observed settlement with predicted envelopes and define what observed rate or excess pore-pressure condition would permit further loading.

Worked sensitivity. For Hdr=2 m, cv=2×10⁻⁷ m²/s and t=365 days, Tv≈1.5768. If cv halves, Tv halves. If the drainage length doubles, Tv becomes one-quarter as large. Create a table showing how mean consolidation changes under both assumptions and label which change is a material-property uncertainty and which is a boundary-condition uncertainty. Conclude by identifying which field test would address each uncertainty.
