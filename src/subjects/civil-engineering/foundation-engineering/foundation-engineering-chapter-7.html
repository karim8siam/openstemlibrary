§7.1 Liquefaction, lateral loads, scour and construction

Liquefaction, lateral loads, scour and construction

Foundations are exposed to hazards that can invalidate their original boundary conditions. Seismic shaking may generate excess pore pressure in susceptible deposits, creating liquefaction, lateral spreading or loss of stiffness. River scour can remove soil support, altering pile lateral behavior and exposing footing edges. Lateral loading from wind, earthquakes, soil movement or impact may govern pile deflection while its axial resistance remains comfortable. Groundwater operations during construction can create settlement outside the excavation.

A probabilistic risk view begins with plausible scenarios and consequences rather than one deterministic safety factor. The design team should distinguish a screening tool from a consequence model. A simplified CSR–CRR comparison identifies whether further seismic evaluation may be justified; it does not calculate lateral spreading displacement or liquefaction-induced settlement. Likewise, an idealized lateral pile spring illustrates sensitivity to head stiffness but cannot substitute for a site-calibrated p–y profile or a three-dimensional interaction model.

Hydraulic conditions require their own specialist evidence. Scour assessment involves flow, channel geometry, sediment transport, flood history and potential changes over the structure's life. The relevant foundation case may be the lowest credible bed elevation rather than the bed seen during construction. The chosen pile embedment then influences bending, axial performance and stability under combined actions. Climate trends and upstream works can make historical baselines incomplete.

Construction itself is an exposure. Dewatering, excavation, driving and equipment loads can change groundwater pressure and soil stresses before the final system has been completed. Controls include method statements, staged checks, hold points, instrumentation and defined response thresholds. A good design recognizes that a structurally sound final foundation can still be damaged or cause damage during installation.

Case integration: bridge foundations after a major flood. A highway bridge pier was designed using a riverbed profile obtained years before channel alteration upstream. After a flood, inspection finds lower sediment levels near one pier, and the originally embedded portion of its piles may now be partially exposed. The immediate task is to compare actual bed elevation against credible design-low scour scenarios, then assess both hydraulic and structural consequences. A simple pile-head stiffness model illustrates why loss of near-surface lateral restraint can increase deflection, but it does not represent nonlinear soil reaction. Prepare two structural load envelopes: one for normal service and another for the degraded-bed condition, including axial and lateral actions. Check whether local erosion undermines an adjacent footing or utility. Monitoring should combine bathymetric surveys, visual inspections and specific trigger levels. A responsible engineer should avoid concluding that the bridge is safe solely because it remains standing after one flood. The design basis should also consider future flood and channel change, not just a snapshot of the last observed bank profile.

§7.2 Four lessons and their applications

Four lessons and their applications

Lesson 7.1 — Earthquake-induced liquefaction screening

Why this mechanism matters

Cyclic ground shaking can raise pore pressure in susceptible saturated granular soils, reducing effective stress and stiffness. Screening procedures often compare cyclic stress ratio and cyclic resistance ratio with corrections for magnitude, stress and aging. Ground deformation may continue after shaking and can affect buried utilities and deep foundations. Screening values have significant method and site uncertainty.

Evidence, scales and the engineering model

The choice of model changes the interpretation of earthquake-induced liquefaction screening: a single observation establishes a condition at a place and time, while an engineering prediction requires a link from that observation to a repeatable physical mechanism. Establish the relevant load or environmental scenario, then identify whether the response is governed by strength, deformation, pore pressure, geometry, or construction sequence. The question to ask before entering numerical values is not simply which equation applies? but which field evidence could make the equation inapplicable? The answer often determines whether the apparent precision of an output is justified.

Decision and evidence. An investigation should resolve what changes the foundation selection, not maximize tests per hectare. Ground interpretation must be linked to an explicit load and performance objective. For this lesson, make the assumed boundary conditions visible in a section drawing and distinguish measured inputs from correlations. A parameter that is merely convenient should not be presented as if a project investigation had confirmed it.

A useful field-to-model workflow is to identify an observed quantity, the geological or structural mechanism, the chosen constitutive assumption, the calculation, and a practical verification measurement. If the observation cannot distinguish two mechanisms, carry both possibilities into the sensitivity analysis. The reported result should preserve units, stress convention, drainage condition, stage and uncertainty bounds. It must be possible to trace a final design recommendation back to the underlying evidence.

Worked case 25 — method and interpretation

Given. For screening, peak acceleration amax=0.25g, total vertical stress 150 kPa, effective stress 95 kPa and rd=0.90.

Solution. CSR≈0.65×0.25×(150/95)×0.90≈0.231. If illustrative CRR=0.30 then simple screening FS≈1.30.

Interpretation. This is not a project-specific liquefaction determination: stress and magnitude corrections and consequences are absent.

The numerical answer above uses deliberately narrow assumptions to make the dependency transparent. If any assumption is relaxed, the expression may need a different correction or an altogether different mechanical model. Before applying the result to another footprint, soil layer or construction state, ask whether the test procedure and the selected parameters represent the new condition. Check dimensional consistency and confirm that the direction of a predicted change agrees with the underlying physics.

Deeper technical application

Diagnostic study — triggering is not consequence. Liquefaction screening asks whether cyclic stresses may exceed resistance under a prescribed shaking scenario in susceptible saturated ground. It does not directly predict lateral spreading, building settlement or the post-event stiffness remaining in a soil layer. The simplified cyclic stress ratio uses peak acceleration, total-to-effective stress ratio and a depth-reduction factor; more complete procedures require site-specific field test corrections, magnitude considerations and often layered treatment. A high water table can increase the total-to-effective stress ratio, but the effect also depends on the actual deposit and drainage behavior. A coarse soil name does not replace characterization of gradation, density and fines behavior.

Decision exercise. In the sample with amax=0.25g, total stress 150 kPa, effective stress 95 kPa and rd=0.90, the idealized CSR is about 0.23. Comparing it with assumed CRR=0.30 produces a ratio around 1.30; this is a screening illustration, not an approved liquefaction factor of safety. Repeat for 0.35g and identify the decrease in the ratio, then discuss how a deeper water table changes the effective-stress calculation. A foundations engineer should separately assess potential loss of bearing support, lateral ground displacement and pile bending demands if triggering is plausible. The response may involve detailed investigation, ground improvement or changed foundation configuration. Document the limitations of using an arbitrary CRR slider instead of calibrated CPT or SPT data.

Counterfactual test

In this lesson, begin by writing the input assumption in a calculation sheet and introduce one perturbation at a time. Consider a 20% increase in the most uncertain load, a plausible change in groundwater, and a different depth or stiffness of a critical layer. Do not automatically multiply every output by the same factor: soil response is often nonlinear and the mechanism itself can change. Record which sensitivity is purely numerical and which requires more field data. If the decision to use shallow versus deep foundations changes, that is evidence that additional investigation may have a higher value than further decimal places.

Field practice and failure-first review

Practical action. Distinguish triggering assessment from consequence modeling, including lateral spreading and post-earthquake settlement. Prioritize direct observations whenever a correlation could conceal a critical stratum or groundwater path. Document who reviews anomalies, whether repeat tests are required, and which construction stage is exposed. Failure trap: A screening factor of safety is not a forecast of exact displacement.

Make a brief alternative hypothesis for the same observations. Ask whether a weak pocket, high water level, unrecognized load eccentricity, or installation defect might explain an unexpectedly large deformation. The right response is not always more conservative material strength; sometimes it is a different foundation system, a modified construction sequence, or a targeted test that resolves the uncertainty. Protect neighboring structures and utilities when the mechanism can propagate outside the footprint.

Lesson 7.2 — Lateral pile loads and group response

Why this mechanism matters

A laterally loaded pile interacts with depth-dependent soil resistance, pile bending stiffness and head condition. Simple beam-on-spring models use lateral springs, while calibrated p-y methods account for nonlinear soil resistance and cyclic effects. Group shielding and cap constraints influence response. Lateral deflection serviceability can govern long before structural capacity is reached.

Evidence, scales and the engineering model

The choice of model changes the interpretation of lateral pile loads and group response: a single observation establishes a condition at a place and time, while an engineering prediction requires a link from that observation to a repeatable physical mechanism. Establish the relevant load or environmental scenario, then identify whether the response is governed by strength, deformation, pore pressure, geometry, or construction sequence. The question to ask before entering numerical values is not simply which equation applies? but which field evidence could make the equation inapplicable? The answer often determines whether the apparent precision of an output is justified.

Constitutive and hydraulic assumptions. Strength and stiffness must match the stress path, drainage regime and level of deformation being checked. Worked case 26 — method and interpretation

Given. A pile lateral spring idealization has head stiffness k=20,000 kN/m and applied horizontal load H=100 kN.

Solution. Head displacement H/k=0.005 m or 5 mm. Doubling the assumed stiffness halves this displacement in the linear model.

Interpretation. Actual p-y relations are nonlinear and pile bending stiffness and head condition also control response.

The numerical answer above uses deliberately narrow assumptions to make the dependency transparent. If any assumption is relaxed, the expression may need a different correction or an altogether different mechanical model. Before applying the result to another footprint, soil layer or construction state, ask whether the test procedure and the selected parameters represent the new condition. Check dimensional consistency and confirm that the direction of a predicted change agrees with the underlying physics.

Deeper technical application

Diagnostic study — lateral pile behavior is a coupled boundary-value problem. Lateral pile loading causes bending in the pile and distributed reaction in the surrounding soil. Reaction generally depends on displacement, depth, soil type and repeated loading; the response is therefore not fully described by a single head stiffness. Classical beam-on-nonlinear-spring models use site-dependent p–y curves, while more advanced continuum models resolve interaction in greater detail. Pile head fixity, diameter, EI, cap rotation and group shielding change the displacement shape. The interactive exponential curve illustrates sensitivity but is not derived from the actual pile differential equation and must not be presented as a prediction of pile moment or capacity.

Decision exercise. At H=100 kN and prescribed k=20,000 kN/m, the simple head movement is 5 mm. With the same stiffness, a 150 kN force gives 7.5 mm, but an actual pile may soften and depart from this linear change. Compare a free-head and fixed-head foundation conceptually: their bending-moment profiles will differ even under equal lateral load. Ask what soil tests, pile geometry and cyclic demand would be needed to construct credible p–y curves. Also explain how loss of near-surface soil from scour reduces lateral support, forcing the bending response to concentrate deeper. An engineering report should show deflection and moment envelopes rather than only a head-spring number.

Lesson 7.3 — Scour and hydraulic erosion

Why this mechanism matters

Water flow around piers, abutments and foundations can remove supporting soil and reduce embedded length. Local scour, contraction scour and long-term riverbed change are distinct processes. Erosion can increase the effective unsupported length of piles and expose footing edges. Hydraulic and geotechnical specialists need a shared set of design flood and bed-level scenarios.

Evidence, scales and the engineering model

The choice of model changes the interpretation of scour and hydraulic erosion: a single observation establishes a condition at a place and time, while an engineering prediction requires a link from that observation to a repeatable physical mechanism. Establish the relevant load or environmental scenario, then identify whether the response is governed by strength, deformation, pore pressure, geometry, or construction sequence. The question to ask before entering numerical values is not simply which equation applies? but which field evidence could make the equation inapplicable? The answer often determines whether the apparent precision of an output is justified.

Equilibrium and load distribution. Global force and moment equilibrium are necessary conditions even for sophisticated nonlinear models. A model that balances actions can still be wrong in local deformation and contact assumptions. Worked case 27 — method and interpretation

Given. An exposed pile has 2 m unsupported above the bed; a design scour scenario removes another 3 m.

Solution. The visible unsupported length increases from 2 to 5 m, a factor of 2.5; an isolated Euler-like L^-2 stiffness analogy would change by (2/5)^2=0.16.

Interpretation. The analogy is not a pile buckling model because embedded soil provides lateral resistance; rigorous hydraulic and structural design is required.

Deeper technical application

Diagnostic study — changing ground support beneath water. Scour occurs when flowing water transports soil away from piers, abutments, banks or channels. The exposed foundation condition may be far more demanding than the as-built geometry. Local scour around a pier, contraction scour caused by reduced channel area and long-term bed degradation are separate contributions, and the design may need a credible combined lower bed elevation. Uncertainty includes extreme-flow scenarios, sediment mobility, debris and alterations of the river regime. An existing embedment depth measured from the current bed does not establish safety under a rare design flood.

Decision exercise. The unsupported pile length in the synthetic example grows from 2 m to 5 m after the scour scenario. The change factor is 2.5. A free-column Euler analogy would imply a dramatic reduction in buckling stiffness proportional to 1/L², but a real embedded pile is not an isolated column: soil still restrains it below the scoured bed and lateral loads impose bending. Use the analogy only to motivate the sensitivity to unsupported length. Sketch two combined axial-and-lateral load cases for current and eroded bed elevations. Specify the hydraulic study, geotechnical profile and condition-inspection records needed to establish scour criteria. A river-stage photograph cannot substitute for a design hydraulic and geomorphic assessment.

Lesson 7.4 — Construction effects and neighboring assets

Why this mechanism matters

Excavation dewatering can alter effective stresses and induce movement outside the property. Driven piles can cause vibration and displacement; bored piles can suffer necking, soil loss or unstable holes. Temporary loads and sequence matter as much as final loads. Monitoring points must be chosen to detect movement of the right assets at useful thresholds.

Evidence, scales and the engineering model

The choice of model changes the interpretation of construction effects and neighboring assets: a single observation establishes a condition at a place and time, while an engineering prediction requires a link from that observation to a repeatable physical mechanism. Establish the relevant load or environmental scenario, then identify whether the response is governed by strength, deformation, pore pressure, geometry, or construction sequence. The question to ask before entering numerical values is not simply which equation applies? but which field evidence could make the equation inapplicable? The answer often determines whether the apparent precision of an output is justified.

Serviceability and sequence. Movement develops with loading, unloading, water changes and consolidation. Record construction stages and timescales before selecting a stiffness model. A parameter that is merely convenient should not be presented as if a project investigation had confirmed it.

If the observation cannot distinguish two mechanisms, carry both possibilities into the sensitivity analysis. The reported result should preserve units, stress convention, drainage condition, stage and uncertainty bounds. It must be possible to trace a final design recommendation back to the underlying evidence.

Worked case 28 — method and interpretation

Given. A construction dewatering plan expects 1 m drawdown in the excavation and 0.3 m at a neighboring building.

Solution. Under simplified hydrostatic assumptions, the pore-pressure reduction at a fixed depth below the initial water table is approximately 9.81×0.3 = 2.94 kPa near the neighbor.

Interpretation. Effective-stress increases can induce settlement in compressible soils, and pumping induces gradients; monitor levels and movement together.

The numerical answer above uses deliberately narrow assumptions to make the dependency transparent. If any assumption is relaxed, the expression may need a different correction or an altogether different mechanical model. Before applying the result to another footprint, soil layer or construction state, ask whether the test procedure and the selected parameters represent the new condition. Check dimensional consistency and confirm that the direction of a predicted change agrees with the underlying physics.

Deeper technical application

Diagnostic study — neighboring ground moves during construction. Dewatering lowers groundwater head and changes effective stress within and beyond an excavation. Where compressible soils exist, increased effective stress can cause settlement of structures that are not being built or excavated. Pumping can also induce seepage gradients and potentially cause piping, heave or loss of fines. Similarly, pile driving may densify granular deposits or displace clay, while drilling can cause ground loss if casing or fluids fail to support the hole. These effects appear before the completed foundation and its intended service loads exist. The construction method therefore belongs inside the foundation risk model.

Decision exercise. A 0.3 m groundwater drawdown near a neighboring foundation reduces local hydrostatic pore pressure by roughly 2.94 kPa at a fixed depth where the same aquifer assumptions apply. That value does not predict settlement without a compressibility model and drainage-path interpretation. Prepare two possible mechanisms for an unexpected 6 mm movement: regional drawdown and localized excavation volume loss. Select monitoring that could distinguish them, such as nested groundwater instruments plus surface settlement points. Add clear hold points before extending dewatering or excavation when trigger readings exceed their set ranges. Document the response authority, alternative pumping plan and neighbor communication process before starting work.

§7.3 synthesis

Unit 7 synthesis

This unit connects its four lessons through one overarching design decision: the right model follows from the mechanism and evidence, not from a convenient formula. Prepare a one-page design-basis table listing assumptions, parameter origin, uncertainty, sensitivity, and the condition requiring professional escalation. Pair that table with a labeled cross-section. For the investigation or structure in your own scenario, justify whether the controlling concern is ultimate resistance, unacceptable movement, unexpected groundwater, temporary construction loading or interaction with neighbors. Do not confuse a numerical check with an approval; identify the external standard and reviewer required for a real project.

Paired simulations: [Lab 13](../labs/lab-13.html) and [Lab 14](../labs/lab-14.html).

Assessment: [Unit 07 questions](../assessments/unit-07.html).

Project: Hazard register and inspection program — For a riverside bridge support, assess scour, seismic loss of soil strength, lateral pile load and construction pumping as separate scenarios. Distinguish screening metrics from consequence models. Produce an owner-assigned hazard register with evidence gaps, observable precursors and escalation triggers.
