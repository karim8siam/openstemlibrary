Generators, Electrical Integration and Plant Controls

Comprehensive university textbook unit covering fundamental principles, governing relationships, and engineering applications of Generators, Electrical Integration and Plant Controls.

About this textbook. Original English teaching material for university Power Plant Engineering study. Eight units with verified worked problems and 16 interactive simulations illustrating fundamental principles and core applications. Numerical checks and an editorial review support this edition.

§7.1 Generator mechanical-electric conversion

**Core topic:** shaft torque and rotational speed. **Representative relation:** `P_shaft = torque*omega`. **Case context:** turbine-generator coupling under rated load.

§7.2 Three-phase output and power factor

**Core topic:** electrical real power measurement. **Representative relation:** `P_3ph = sqrt(3)*V_LL*I_L*PF`. **Case context:** synchronous generator terminal measurements.

§7.3 Frequency response and ramping

**Core topic:** power-rate limits and balancing. **Representative relation:** `Ramp = (P2-P1)/(t2-t1)`. **Case context:** automatic generation control response.

§7.4 Auxiliary systems and protection boundary

**Core topic:** meter alignment and station service. **Representative relation:** `P_net = P_generator-P_station_service`. **Case context:** high-voltage switchyard and feedwater pump loads.

§7.5 Operating data, state estimation and alarm review

**Core topic:** instrument disagreement and trend interpretation. **Representative relation:** `Residual = measured - predicted`. **Case context:** supervisory historian data quality audit.

Step-by-Step Power Plant Engineering Solutions

Solved Problem Example 7.1: Calculation & Verification: Generator mechanical-electric conversion

Problem Statement: At 3000 rpm a generator rotor turns at 3000 × 2π/60 = 314.16 rad/s. With shaft torque 250 kN·m, mechanical shaft power is 250,000 × 314.16 = **78.54 MW** before losses. A torque estimate based on 3000 rather than 314.16 would be dimensionally incorrect.

Worked Engineering Derivation:

**Verification and limitations.** Rotor dynamics, overspeed margins and protective systems are outside this basic calculation. The result is illustrative and does not replace equipment-specific property data, measurements or professional review.

Solved Problem Example 7.2: Calculation & Verification: Three-phase output and power factor

Problem Statement: A balanced three-phase generator has line voltage 11 kV, line current 5000 A and power factor 0.90. Real power is √3 × 11,000 × 5000 × 0.90 = **85.74 MW**. Apparent power is about 95.26 MVA, a distinct quantity that matters for thermal loading.

Worked Engineering Derivation:

**Verification and limitations.** A three-phase formula must use consistent line-to-line voltage and line-current definitions. The result is illustrative and does not replace equipment-specific property data, measurements or professional review.

Solved Problem Example 7.3: Calculation & Verification: Frequency response and ramping

Problem Statement: An operator record shows net output rising from 120 MW to 180 MW in 15 minutes. The average ramp rate is (180 − 120)/15 = **4 MW/min**. This scalar does not mean the equipment can repeat the ramp indefinitely or that the grid operator may request it at any condition.

Worked Engineering Derivation:

**Verification and limitations.** Turbine thermal stress, minimum stable generation and emission limits require independent operating constraints. The result is illustrative and does not replace equipment-specific property data, measurements or professional review.

Solved Problem Example 7.4: Calculation & Verification: Auxiliary systems and protection boundary

Problem Statement: A generator terminal meter records 200 MW(e) while feedwater pumps, fans and other station loads consume 12 MW(e). Net export is **188 MW(e)**. A rise in auxiliary use from 12 to 16 MW lowers export to 184 MW even if generator terminal output remains unchanged.

Worked Engineering Derivation:

**Verification and limitations.** Separate gross generation, contracted station service and net settlement-meter export. The result is illustrative and does not replace equipment-specific property data, measurements or professional review.

Solved Problem Example 7.5: Calculation & Verification: Operating data, state estimation and alarm review

Problem Statement: A simple performance model predicts 38.0% net efficiency for a defined state, but a calibrated measurement yields 37.0%. Residual = measured minus predicted = **−1.0 percentage point**, approximately −2.63% relative to the prediction. Sensor uncertainty, differing fuel heating value and physical degradation are competing explanations.

Worked Engineering Derivation:

**Verification and limitations.** The residual is a diagnostic signal, not itself proof of a specific equipment fault. The result is illustrative and does not replace equipment-specific property data, measurements or professional review.
