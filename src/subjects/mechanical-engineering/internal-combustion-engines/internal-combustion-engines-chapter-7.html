Solved Problem Example 7.1: Calculation & Verification: Internal Combustion Engines — U07-L01

Problem Statement: The working relation used here is `Normalize mass emissions by useful work: g/kWh = grams emitted / brake work in kWh.`. Define every variable before substitution, keep a single system of units, and check whether its result should increase or decrease when an input changes. Ask which assumptions make the expression possible: steady conditions, a perfect gas, constant properties, a lumped state, negligible pressure loss or an ideal mechanical linkage may be necessary. If an assumption does not hold in the intended application, identify the direction of the expected deviation when physics permits. When that direction is not clear, do not guess; schedule a measurement or use a higher-fidelity calculation. **Assumed numerical case:** An illustrative run emitting 20 g of a constituent while delivering 50 kWh yields 0.4 g/kWh.

Worked Engineering Derivation:

**Calculation walkthrough:** If measured pollutant mass is 20 g over a test yielding 50 kWh of brake work, the normalized intensity is 20/50 = 0.40 g/kWh. A reading of 100 ppm cannot be directly substituted for 20 g because mass flow and species molecular mass are needed. Ensure the mass and work cover exactly the same time interval. Interpret the result, including failures **Critical interpretation:** Concentration in ppm cannot be compared as mass rate without exhaust-flow information. A misleading numerical result can arise even when the arithmetic is perfect: the problem may be a wrong system boundary, an inappropriate reference property, a time-averaged signal used for a crank-angle event, or an unrecorded controller response. State which of those failure mechanisms is most likely here. Think about the change that would be observed in cylinder pressure, exhaust temperature, manifold state, or brake output if the explanatory mechanism were genuinely active rather than merely correlated with an unrelated change. **Conservation audit:** Start by marking the system boundary on a sketch. Separate fuel energy entering the cylinder from indicated work on the piston and brake work at the shaft. A computed improvement that violates energy conservation is a mistake, not a new engine phenomenon. Where an experimental result seems to contradict the calculation, investigate calibration, hidden storage, auxiliary loads and the interpretation of reference conditions before adding complexity to the model. Applied to **formation mechanisms for nox, co, hc and particulate**, this discipline helps decide whether the model is a prediction with independent evidence or merely a restatement of its input measurements. Solved Problem Example 7.3: Calculation & Verification: Internal Combustion Engines — U07-L03

Problem Statement: Use `A mass capture estimate is eta_c=(m_in-m_out)/m_in.` as the initial closure relation. It is tempting to interpret the number as a design guarantee; instead define a reference operating point, perturb each independent input separately and observe the output response. Check whether apparently independent variables are actually correlated by conservation or control logic. Treat a high-sensitivity variable as a candidate for better measurement. If the result requires a parameter measured by the same output that is being predicted, the model is partly circular and its validation requires an independent observation. **Assumed numerical case:** If particulate entering a filter is 2.0 g/h and leaving is 0.1 g/h, apparent capture is 95%.

Worked Engineering Derivation:

**Calculation walkthrough:** For 2.0 g/h particulate entering and 0.1 g/h leaving, capture fraction is (2.0−0.1)/2.0 = 0.95, or 95%. The value is not necessarily sustained during regeneration or different particle-size distributions. Check differential pressure and filter loading before comparing filter architectures.
