Solved Problem Example 2.1: Calculation & Verification: Internal Combustion Engines — U02-L01

Problem Statement: Use `For a closed cycle Delta U = Q_in - W_out; over a full cycle Delta U is zero.` as the initial closure relation. It is tempting to interpret the number as a design guarantee; instead define a reference operating point, perturb each independent input separately and observe the output response. Check whether apparently independent variables are actually correlated by conservation or control logic. Treat a high-sensitivity variable as a candidate for better measurement. If the result requires a parameter measured by the same output that is being predicted, the model is partly circular and its validation requires an independent observation. **Assumed numerical case:** If a closed ideal cycle receives 800 J and rejects 480 J, the ideal net work is 320 J.

Worked Engineering Derivation:

**Calculation walkthrough:** For one full cycle the closed-system internal energy returns to its initial value. From 0 = Q_in − Q_out − W_net, obtain W_net = 800 − 480 = 320 J. The ideal cycle efficiency is 320/800 = 0.40, or 40%. Real combustion energy and real heat rejection require different measurement boundaries. Check the sign and order of magnitude against a physical limiting case. Finally, ask which measured input would dominate an uncertainty interval. A large discrepancy from an independently computed check is grounds to revise the work, not to adjust the accepted answer without explanation. ### 3. Interpret the result, including failures **Critical interpretation:** The ideal heat addition is not identical to chemical energy release in an actual cylinder. A misleading numerical result can arise even when the arithmetic is perfect: the problem may be a wrong system boundary, an inappropriate reference property, a time-averaged signal used for a crank-angle event, or an unrecorded controller response. State which of those failure mechanisms is most likely here. Think about the change that would be observed in cylinder pressure, exhaust temperature, manifold state, or brake output if the explanatory mechanism were genuinely active rather than merely correlated with an unrelated change. **Conservation audit:** Start by marking the system boundary on a sketch. Separate fuel energy entering the cylinder from indicated work on the piston and brake work at the shaft. A computed improvement that violates energy conservation is a mistake, not a new engine phenomenon. Where an experimental result seems to contradict the calculation, investigate calibration, hidden storage, auxiliary loads and the interpretation of reference conditions before adding complexity to the model. Applied to **closed-system energy balances**, this discipline helps decide whether the model is a prediction with independent evidence or merely a restatement of its input measurements. Solved Problem Example 2.2: Calculation & Verification: Internal Combustion Engines — U02-L02

Problem Statement: The working relation used here is `eta_Otto = 1 - r_c^(1-gamma) for constant gamma and ideal gas.`. Define every variable before substitution, keep a single system of units, and check whether its result should increase or decrease when an input changes. Ask which assumptions make the expression possible: steady conditions, a perfect gas, constant properties, a lumped state, negligible pressure loss or an ideal mechanical linkage may be necessary. If an assumption does not hold in the intended application, identify the direction of the expected deviation when physics permits. When that direction is not clear, do not guess; schedule a measurement or use a higher-fidelity calculation. **Assumed numerical case:** For r_c=10 and gamma=1.4, eta is about 60.2% in the air-standard model.

**Calculation walkthrough:** For compression ratio r=10 and γ=1.4, use η = 1 − 1/10^0.4. Because 10^0.4 ≈ 2.5119, η ≈ 1 − 0.3981 = 0.6019 or 60.19%. The result is an ideal air-standard sensitivity estimate; it cannot be read as a measured brake thermal efficiency. Solved Problem Example 2.3: Calculation & Verification: Internal Combustion Engines — U02-L03

Problem Statement: The smallest useful model is `eta_Diesel=1-(1/r_c^(gamma-1))*((rho^gamma-1)/(gamma*(rho-1))).`. Write it first in symbols because symbols expose which quantities are inputs and which are derived. Conduct a dimensional check. For example, an energy rate must have dimensions of power, and a dimensionless efficiency must not quietly carry a pressure unit. Then inspect the limiting cases. A vanishing input, a zero flow or an impossible temperature ratio should trigger a validity warning rather than a numerically polished answer. The equation belongs to the assumptions stated with it, not to every engine. **Assumed numerical case:** At fixed compression ratio, a larger ideal cutoff ratio generally reduces efficiency in this simple model.

Worked Engineering Derivation:

**Calculation walkthrough:** Consider the air-standard relation at r=17, γ=1.4 and cutoff ρ=2. The compression factor is 17^0.4 ≈ 3.106, while the heat-addition factor is (2^1.4−1)/(1.4×(2−1)) ≈ 1.1708. Hence η ≈ 1 − 1.1708/3.106 ≈ 0.623. Solved Problem Example 2.5: Calculation & Verification: Internal Combustion Engines — U02-L05

Problem Statement: The working relation used here is `Exergy destruction = T_0 * S_generated for a fixed environmental reference temperature.`. Define every variable before substitution, keep a single system of units, and check whether its result should increase or decrease when an input changes. Ask which assumptions make the expression possible: steady conditions, a perfect gas, constant properties, a lumped state, negligible pressure loss or an ideal mechanical linkage may be necessary. If an assumption does not hold in the intended application, identify the direction of the expected deviation when physics permits. When that direction is not clear, do not guess; schedule a measurement or use a higher-fidelity calculation. **Assumed numerical case:** An entropy generation of 0.20 kJ/K at T0=300 K corresponds to 60 kJ destroyed exergy.

Worked Engineering Derivation:

**Calculation walkthrough:** The destruction relation has the structure I = T0 S_gen. At 300 K reference temperature and 0.20 kJ/K entropy generation, I = 60 kJ. Energy was not destroyed; the capability to convert it into useful work was reduced. The reference environment must be stated or two exergy calculations may disagree while both are internally consistent.
