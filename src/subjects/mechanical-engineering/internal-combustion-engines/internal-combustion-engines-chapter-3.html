Solved Problem Example 3.1: Calculation & Verification: Internal Combustion Engines — U03-L01

Problem Statement: The smallest useful model is `For C_x H_y, ideal complete oxidation requires x + y/4 moles of O2 per mole fuel.`. Write it first in symbols because symbols expose which quantities are inputs and which are derived. Conduct a dimensional check. For example, an energy rate must have dimensions of power, and a dimensionless efficiency must not quietly carry a pressure unit. Then inspect the limiting cases. A vanishing input, a zero flow or an impossible temperature ratio should trigger a validity warning rather than a numerically polished answer. The equation belongs to the assumptions stated with it, not to every engine. **Assumed numerical case:** Methane CH4 requires two O2 molecules per fuel molecule and produces one CO2 plus two H2O.

Worked Engineering Derivation:

**Calculation walkthrough:** Balance methane as CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O. Count carbon: one on each side. Count hydrogen: four on each side. Count oxygen: four atoms on each side. The oxygen coefficient follows x + y/4 = 1 + 4/4 = 2. To calculate air rather than pure oxygen, specify the oxygen fraction and mass basis of air. Solved Problem Example 3.3: Calculation & Verification: Internal Combustion Engines — U03-L03

Problem Statement: The working relation used here is `Crank angle elapsed in one millisecond = 0.006*N degrees with N in rpm.`. Define every variable before substitution, keep a single system of units, and check whether its result should increase or decrease when an input changes. Ask which assumptions make the expression possible: steady conditions, a perfect gas, constant properties, a lumped state, negligible pressure loss or an ideal mechanical linkage may be necessary. If an assumption does not hold in the intended application, identify the direction of the expected deviation when physics permits. When that direction is not clear, do not guess; schedule a measurement or use a higher-fidelity calculation. **Assumed numerical case:** At 3000 rpm, a 1 ms ignition delay spans 18 crank degrees.

Worked Engineering Derivation:

**Calculation walkthrough:** A crank rotating at 3000 rpm turns at 50 revolutions per second, or 18,000 degrees per second. Over one millisecond (0.001 s), it turns 18 degrees. A fixed ignition-delay time therefore spans more crank angle at higher engine speed. This is why timing and delay should not be compared without the speed axis. Solved Problem Example 3.5: Calculation & Verification: Internal Combustion Engines — U03-L05

Problem Statement: Use `For fixed gamma in a closed segment, dQ_app=(gamma/(gamma-1))*p*dV+(1/(gamma-1))*V*dp.` as the initial closure relation. It is tempting to interpret the number as a design guarantee; instead define a reference operating point, perturb each independent input separately and observe the output response. Check whether apparently independent variables are actually correlated by conservation or control logic. Treat a high-sensitivity variable as a candidate for better measurement. If the result requires a parameter measured by the same output that is being predicted, the model is partly circular and its validation requires an independent observation. **Assumed numerical case:** Integrated apparent release may be below fuel chemical energy because wall losses and blow-by are not resolved.

Worked Engineering Derivation:

**Calculation walkthrough:** The first term of dQ_app measures pressure work associated with cylinder-volume change; the second measures pressure rise at a given volume. Integrating the expression requires time- or angle-aligned p and V, consistent pressure pegging and a defensible γ. It is called apparent heat release because unmodeled wall heat transfer and leakage remain inside the inferred result.
