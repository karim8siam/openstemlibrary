Solved Problem Example 6.1: Calculation & Verification: Process selection and design for manufacture

Problem Statement: An engineering practitioner analyzes a mechanical system governed by **Process selection and design for manufacture** under steady operational loading.

Worked Engineering Derivation:

**Core relation or design rule:** `Unit cost = variable cost + fixed cost / batch size` **Worked case.** A process requiring 10,000 currency units in tooling and 15 per unit has different economics from a tool-free process costing 35 per unit. Their simplified break-even occurs at 500 parts, before yield and financing adjustments. The first check is dimensional: if the symbols represent different physical dimensions, convert to a common SI basis before evaluating. The second check is one limiting case: set a load to zero, make the design very stiff, or let an efficiency approach unity—whichever is physically relevant—and ask if the predicted direction makes sense. The third check is to estimate the size of an ignored term. A compact hand calculation is valuable because it can expose a reversed ratio or a misplaced conversion factor that would otherwise pass through a spreadsheet unnoticed. Convert the worked case into a design memo: state the known inputs, unknown output, assumptions, calculation, units and consequence of crossing the limit. Make two alternative estimates, one at a favorable operating point and one at a credible adverse point. Explain why the apparent improvement may not translate into a better total system. The correct comparison uses the same function, service life and boundary for each alternative rather than comparing one design at peak efficiency with another at average duty. ## 3. Scope, counterexample and failure conditions A low quoted unit price may exclude inspection and rejected material. A part fabricated successfully once may not have reliable process capability. A calculation is only as good as its assumed configuration. Ask whether the actual mechanism is linear, whether geometry remains unchanged, whether a temperature-dependent property was treated as fixed, and whether contact, vibration or actuator saturation could introduce a discontinuity. If the model ignores an effect because it appears small, report the evidence supporting that decision. Quantification is not a substitute for identifying hazards: an attractive weighted objective cannot excuse an unverified guard, protection feature, pressure boundary or emergency stop. A counterexample helps establish the scope. Imagine the original design moved from a controlled teaching bench to a dirty or wet industrial location. The incoming load spectrum, corrosion exposure, calibration frequency and repair resources may be different. Solved Problem Example 6.5: Calculation & Verification: Lifecycle cost and responsible sourcing

Problem Statement: An engineering practitioner analyzes a mechanical system governed by **Lifecycle cost and responsible sourcing** under steady operational loading.

Worked Engineering Derivation:

**Core relation or design rule:** `LCC = CapEx + PV(Energy + Maintenance + Replacement − Salvage)` **Worked case.** Two motors differ by 1,000 currency units in purchase price. The more efficient unit saves 300 per year for eight years. Discounting at 8% gives present savings around 1,724, so the higher cost may be justified before repairs and taxes. The first check is dimensional: if the symbols represent different physical dimensions, convert to a common SI basis before evaluating.
