Solved Problem Example 2.1: Calculation & Verification: Conservation laws and model boundaries

Problem Statement: An engineering practitioner analyzes a mechanical system governed by **Conservation laws and model boundaries** under steady operational loading.

Worked Engineering Derivation:

**Core relation or design rule:** `dE/dt = Qin − Qout + Win − Wout` **Worked case.** For a 2 kW electrical heater delivering 1.5 kW to a fluid at steady state, at least 0.5 kW is not reaching the fluid. That difference may be heat loss or a mismatch in instrumentation boundaries. The first check is dimensional: if the symbols represent different physical dimensions, convert to a common SI basis before evaluating. The second check is one limiting case: set a load to zero, make the design very stiff, or let an efficiency approach unity—whichever is physically relevant—and ask if the predicted direction makes sense. The third check is to estimate the size of an ignored term. A compact hand calculation is valuable because it can expose a reversed ratio or a misplaced conversion factor that would otherwise pass through a spreadsheet unnoticed. Convert the worked case into a design memo: state the known inputs, unknown output, assumptions, calculation, units and consequence of crossing the limit. Make two alternative estimates, one at a favorable operating point and one at a credible adverse point. Explain why the apparent improvement may not translate into a better total system. The correct comparison uses the same function, service life and boundary for each alternative rather than comparing one design at peak efficiency with another at average duty. ## 3. Scope, counterexample and failure conditions Confusing heat with stored energy or neglecting shaft work causes inconsistent budgets. A steady state equation cannot predict warm-up duration. A calculation is only as good as its assumed configuration. Ask whether the actual mechanism is linear, whether geometry remains unchanged, whether a temperature-dependent property was treated as fixed, and whether contact, vibration or actuator saturation could introduce a discontinuity. If the model ignores an effect because it appears small, report the evidence supporting that decision. Quantification is not a substitute for identifying hazards: an attractive weighted objective cannot excuse an unverified guard, protection feature, pressure boundary or emergency stop. A counterexample helps establish the scope. Imagine the original design moved from a controlled teaching bench to a dirty or wet industrial location. The incoming load spectrum, corrosion exposure, calibration frequency and repair resources may be different. Name at least one parameter from the equation that remains meaningful and one omitted mechanism that becomes important. Explain how the design review would change if that mechanism is plausible. Record any consequence whose acceptability must be judged by a competent safety professional rather than inferred from a classroom equation.

Worked Engineering Derivation:

**Core relation or design rule:** `Residual_i = measured_i − predicted_i` **Worked case.** Calibrate a thermal resistance using one heat-flow experiment, then predict another at a different ambient temperature. A 2 °C bias that is small at low temperature may become critical near an insulation limit.
