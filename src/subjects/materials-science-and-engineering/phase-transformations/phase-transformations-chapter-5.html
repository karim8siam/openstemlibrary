§5.1 A physical orientation for this unit

Guiding question: Why do heat-treated steels display distinct transformation products?

Three anonymously labeled specimens are pearlitic, bainitic and quenched-and-tempered; optical contrast alone yields uncertain classifications. A complete analysis separates the phase identities, the relevant conserved quantities, the imposed temperature–time path, and the measurable response. It is not enough to state that 'a new phase formed': the experiment must establish what is new, where it appeared, whether composition changed and whether the rate is constrained by diffusion, interface kinetics or transformation strain. This unit emphasizes steel product identity, using a austenite transformation pathway to test plausible mechanisms instead of treating a suggestive curve as proof.

Infer plausible path classes from known TTT/CCT domains, compare phase identity with morphology, and design a sequence to quantify retained austenite and hardness. Before analyzing each lesson, prepare a ledger of observations, assumed boundary conditions and competing interpretations. Distinguish thermodynamic possibility from a nucleation pathway, a transformation rate and eventual property evolution. Include the thermal program and a practical measurement time resolution. Repeat a calculation at two values of a chosen input; the sensitivity often exposes whether the dominant uncertainty lies in material constants, microstructure or instrumentation.

§5.2 Vocabulary and conservation checkpoints

State the initial constituent, product state, composition basis and time origin. A phase fraction may be on a mass, volume, mole or diffraction-intensity basis, and those scales cannot be exchanged without a conversion model. State precisely whether each coefficient is a measured value, an empirical correlation, a thermodynamic state function or a synthetic teaching assumption. For this unit, pay particular attention to carbon partitioning versus displacive strain and to possible confusion from martensite and diffusional products. A suitable cross-check would be diffraction after controlled heat treatments.

§5.3 Austenite, ferrite and cementite

Concept, boundary conditions and physical meaning

Austenite has face-centered-cubic iron-rich crystal structure, while ferrite is body-centered-cubic and cementite is an iron carbide with a distinct crystal structure. Carbon has substantially different solubilities in these constituents. Their coexistence and fractions depend on carbon composition and heat-treatment history. Thermodynamic phase fractions are not interchangeable with the proportions of microstructural features such as pearlite colonies.

Working relationship. For a two-phase tie line, use phase compositions at one temperature and a consistent mass-fraction basis. Read the terms in this relationship as quantities attached to a specific state, geometry or path. Do not assume that a convenient equation has proved the physical mechanism. For austenite, ferrite and cementite, define the initial state and possible product before assigning a fraction, temperature or rate. A bulk average can conceal a sharp local concentration field, different lattice orientations or particles too small for the selected imaging method. When temperature or composition is not uniform, a single numerical parameter is at best a spatially weighted approximation; every reported number should state which part of the specimen it represents.

§5.4 Pearlite formation and interlamellar spacing

Concept, boundary conditions and physical meaning

Pearlite is a lamellar mixture of ferrite and cementite produced by a diffusional eutectoid reaction. Its colony nucleation, interface migration and carbon redistribution control kinetics. Lower transformation temperature may refine spacing within a useful temperature range because more driving force supports thinner diffusion paths, but very low temperatures also slow carbon migration. Pearson-style generic strength estimates are not a substitute for measured pearlite spacing distributions.

Working relationship. Eutectoid gamma→alpha+Fe3C is a coupled diffusional transformation; its morphology is not itself a single phase. §5.5 Bainite and incomplete reactions

Concept, boundary conditions and physical meaning

Bainitic ferrite forms over a characteristic temperature range with mechanisms that combine structural change and carbon redistribution according to alloy and conditions. Upper and lower bainitic microstructures differ in carbide morphology and processing ranges. The interpretation of bainite has active mechanistic subtleties, so a simple declaration that all transformations are wholly diffusional or wholly diffusionless is misleading. Interrupted reactions can leave retained austenite and nonuniform carbon enrichment.

Working relationship. A calibrated phase-fraction model is required; dimensional change need not be directly proportional to phase fraction. Read the terms in this relationship as quantities attached to a specific state, geometry or path. Do not assume that a convenient equation has proved the physical mechanism. §5.6 Martensitic transformations and Ms

Concept, boundary conditions and physical meaning

In steel martensite forms by a cooperative lattice deformation with minimal long-range diffusion during the interface event; carbon may subsequently rearrange during aging. Start temperature Ms depends on composition and parent austenite state. The transformation can proceed during cooling over a range of temperatures and can leave retained austenite after reaching room temperature. Fresh martensite is often highly stressed and requires tempering for many applications.

Working relationship. f_M=1-exp[-alpha(M_s-T)] for Tmartensitic transformations and ms, define the initial state and possible product before assigning a fraction, temperature or rate. A bulk average can conceal a sharp local concentration field, different lattice orientations or particles too small for the selected imaging method. When temperature or composition is not uniform, a single numerical parameter is at best a spatially weighted approximation; every reported number should state which part of the specimen it represents.

§5.7 Tempering and transformation sequences

Concept, boundary conditions and physical meaning

Tempering quenched martensite permits carbon redistribution, recovery and carbide evolution, reducing internal stresses and changing strength-toughness balance. Time, temperature and alloying additions control competing processes. Some alloys exhibit secondary hardening; other conditions may cause temper embrittlement. A tempering instruction must specify starting microstructure, section size and target property, not merely a furnace set point.

Working relationship. A tempering parameter can correlate processes in restricted alloys but is not a universal mechanistic law. Read the terms in this relationship as quantities attached to a specific state, geometry or path. Do not assume that a convenient equation has proved the physical mechanism. For tempering and transformation sequences, define the initial state and possible product before assigning a fraction, temperature or rate. §5.8 Integrated investigation: Steel product identification without oversimplification

Scientific problem and hypotheses

Three anonymously labeled specimens are pearlitic, bainitic and quenched-and-tempered; optical contrast alone yields uncertain classifications. Define a primary observable and a rival mechanism. Hypothesis A should predict the observed conversion from the intended transformation; hypothesis B should explain a similar gross signal through a competing event. Name the observations needed to prefer one. A theory that can be made to fit every possible thermal or structural trace is not adequately discriminating. Describe a negative control, a second spatial sampling location, and an independent physical modality capable of challenging the initial phase identification.

Mathematical framework

Infer plausible path classes from known TTT/CCT domains, compare phase identity with morphology, and design a sequence to quantify retained austenite and hardness. Distinguish equilibrium state functions from kinetic rate parameters, then write the relevant conservation law. If a temperature is specified, record whether it is a furnace set point, actual specimen center, interface point or surface reading. If a phase fraction is inferred, identify the normalization and its endpoint calibration. Compare output at two values using the unit's interactive models; report the numerical change, dimension and range of acceptable inputs. Where a constitutive relation is approximate, propagate rather than conceal parameter uncertainty.

A rigorous explanation should include what would happen at slow cooling compared with quenching, and explain the conditions under which both trajectories would arrive at similar or different microstructures. Include whether substitutional diffusion, interstitial transport, cooperative lattice change, or interface movement supplies the controlling timescale. If the sample contains gradients, explain how a local transformation result might differ between edge and core. The comparison should be phrased as testable predicted signatures, not a guessed dramatic micrograph.

Measurement and validation sequence

Use XRD, heat-treatment logs, SEM imaging and multiple hardness indents with spatial uncertainty; include austenitizing-state metadata. First capture the untreated material's structure and chemistry. Then document time-temperature history using two relevant temperature locations. Finally collect phase-sensitive and morphology-sensitive data at several interrupted processing states. Specify whether each observation is destructive, whether thermal cycling alters the sample, and how replicate variance will be measured. Blind a second analyst to the proposed mechanism and ask them to calculate a phase fraction or kinetic parameter from the primary traces. Record disagreements before revising interpretation.

Claim boundaries and report requirements

Do not infer martensite fraction solely from a single dilatometry signal or claim all bainite follows a universally settled microscopic mechanism. A polished final report must include: starting microstructure; phase definitions; a reaction or chemical-potential argument; a checked worked estimate; a temperature–time diagram annotated with sensor location; synthetic model graphs clearly marked as simulated; a second-method validation plan; and a concise list of conditions that would make the engineering conclusion unsafe or invalid. Numerical compatibility is a necessary implementation check but is not sufficient evidence of mechanism or service suitability. Explain precisely what further data would turn the exercise into a candidate processing procedure.
